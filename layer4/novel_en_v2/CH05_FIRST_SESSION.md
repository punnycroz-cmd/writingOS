# CHAPTER 5: FIRST SESSION

Lena knew she shouldn't be doing this.

She knew it while she locked the waiting-room door, while she turned up the heat so the soaked man would stop shivering, while she took a towel from the cabinet and handed it to him without a word. A stranger with no appointment, walking in after dark, calling her by a name that wasn't hers. Every professional rule and every ordinary instinct for self-preservation said she should call building security right now.

But his name was lying in her safe, in Corwin's handwriting.

"Did you know Corwin Daly?" she asked.

Elias sat on the edge of the armchair in the waiting room, the towel over his shoulders, his hands clasped between his knees. He looked up. "The missing reporter. Your fiancé. I only know what the paper said."

"He wrote about you," Lena said. "Your name is in his notebook, along with a phrase. *Subject Zero.*"

Elias didn't move, but she saw his jaw tighten.

"That's what it said on my chart," he said quietly. "At Halloway, this morning."

They looked at each other across the small room. Rain tapped on the glass. Somewhere in the building a pipe gurgled.

"I could scan you," Lena heard herself say. "A basic exploratory session. No diagnosis, no treatment. Just to see what's in there. If Corwin knew about you, there had to be a reason, and I want to know what it was."

"You're not afraid of me?"

The question was so unexpected she nearly laughed. "Are you planning to hurt me?"

"No."

"Then I'm not afraid."

That was only half true. She wasn't afraid of him. She was afraid of the way her body was reacting to him, because she had no chart that could explain it.

---

The exam room was separated from the office by a thick glass partition. The light inside was dialed down to two thousand Kelvin, a soft amber, dim enough not to stimulate the visual cortex and bright enough for her to read a patient's face. Elias sat down in the leather treatment chair and leaned back the way she told him to.

She cleaned the skin at his temples with an alcohol swab. When the cold pad touched him, a small muscle under his jaw twitched, but he didn't pull away. His skin was hot, unusually hot at the temples, while the hands resting on the armrests were cold as stone. She had read about that heat pattern in the literature on neural overload but had never seen it in a living person.

"I have to say this for the recording," she said, switching to her professional voice. That voice always steadied her. "This is an after-hours exploratory scan with no legal standing. If I find evidence of unauthorized memory intervention, I'm obligated to preserve the raw data and report it to the Department of Health."

"If you see something," Elias said, "something that isn't mine... will you tell me the truth?"

She looked down at him. From this close she could see small charcoal flecks around his gray irises, and a scar as fine as thread running along his left hairline, an old surgical scar. Very old.

"The truth is all I work with," she said. "It's the only thing I know how to do."

She fitted the titanium sensor ring on his head and turned the knob until the six probes clicked and seated against bone. She told him to close his eyes, breathe in for four, hold for two, out for six. He did, and his breathing slowed and evened out. She stepped over to the console and started the scan sequence.

> `[CONNECTION: STABLE]`
> `[LAYER 2 — PASSIVE PROBE MODE]`
> `[DELTA WAVE: 2.4 Hz]`
> `[THETA-GAMMA SYNC: ABNORMAL — OFF SCALE]`

Lena frowned and touched the last line. This machine's scale covered 99.9 percent of human brains, and Elias Vane's brain blew past it like a race car tearing down a bike lane. And there was something else: a very faint band of signal running parallel to the main waves, at a frequency she had seen only once, in a scientific paper that had been retracted five years ago, a paper people in the field mentioned only half joking and half afraid.

*A Layer 4 trace.*

"You've been in Layer 4," she blurted.

"They told me it was a sync malfunction," Elias said, his eyes still closed.

"They lied." She swallowed. Layer 4 was still considered a legend. There was theory, there was math, but no one had ever proven that a person had gone in and come back out. "I'm going to open the image decoder. You might see some strange things. Don't try to hold on to them. Just let them pass."

She swiped across the console. Above it, the holographic projector hummed and bloomed into a sphere of blue light about the size of a volleyball. At first there was only noise, tiny white specks swirling like snow in a snow globe. Little by little they gathered into shapes: a street in the rain, a six-story building, an empty room. Recent memories, from the last few days, sharp but flat.

She adjusted the filter and pushed deeper. The image blurred and changed color.

A kitchen. Morning sun through a window hung with blue gingham curtains, and on the sill, a pot of basil, half dead. A woman standing with her back turned, dark hair in a loose knot, wearing an oversized gray sweater. She turned around with a mug of coffee and smiled at whoever was watching.

Lena stopped breathing.

It was her kitchen, the apartment she and Corwin had shared on Mercer Street. That was the basil she always forgot to water, the sweater of Corwin's she wore on Sunday mornings. And the woman in it was her.

But not her as she saw herself in a mirror. It was her through someone else's eyes, someone standing in the kitchen doorway, a head taller than she was, looking at her with a tenderness she had never been able to see from the inside.

*Corwin.*

"That kitchen," Elias whispered, his eyes still closed but his brow furrowing. "It's you, in that kitchen. I spent twenty-nine years in that kitchen." He swallowed. "I've never set foot in it in my life."

"No," Lena said, and her voice cracked. "You never have."

She didn't understand how Corwin's memory had ended up inside this man's head. She didn't know what it meant. Had Corwin met him? Had Corwin... She didn't let herself finish the thought.

The image drifted away, and she couldn't hold it. The decoder pushed deeper on its own, following that faint Layer 4 band down into levels her machine had never reached.

A white corridor. Two doors set right next to each other. Fluorescent lights. The smell of bleach and boiled cabbage.

Then the audio decoder caught a frequency, a melody. A woman's voice, low and a little rough, singing very softly behind a door. A lullaby in a minor key, slow and sad, rising and falling in three-four time like waves. The words were in a language she didn't know. Old Spanish, maybe.

Lena didn't know this song. She was certain she had never heard it. And yet tears were running down her face before she realized she was crying.

She raised a hand to her cheek and looked at her wet fingers as if they belonged to someone else. Her throat closed. Something in her chest was breaking slowly, like thin ice on a pond in early spring, and she didn't know what was under it. *I don't know this song,* she thought. *So why am I crying?*

In the chair, Elias's breath started coming fast. His fingers clenched the leather armrests until his knuckles went white.

"Someone's singing," he said through his teeth. "She's singing for... for us. Every night. Through the door."

*Us.*

The song broke apart. The sphere of light went orange, then blazing red, and the images slammed into each other so fast she could only catch fragments. Smoke. A concrete corridor with spinning alarm lights. A thick steel door. Sirens. A woman with a burn running down her left arm, her face smeared with ash, prying loose a ventilation grate while flames licked the ceiling behind her.

And a nine-year-old girl with ragged short black hair and dark eyes, crawling into the mouth of the vent, then turning back to grab a skinny boy's wrist, and screaming. This time the decoder caught the sound too, distorted and crackling, but clear:

***"My name is Maren! Don't forget me! Don't forget me!"***

*Snap.* A blue spark shot out of the sensor ring. The console let out a shrieking alarm. The holographic sphere shrank in on itself and then burst into a cloud of red pixel dust that dissolved into the air.

Elias jerked upright off the back of the chair, his mouth open with no sound coming out, his eyes wide and staring. Blood ran from his left nostril, a thick, dark red line that crossed his upper lip and dripped onto his collar.

Lena lunged for him. She pulled the sensor ring off his head and pressed two fingers to his carotid. A hundred and forty. She grabbed a handful of gauze, pressed it to the side of his nose, and pushed his head forward.

"Don't tip your head back. Breathe through your mouth. Look at me. Look at me."

He looked at her with panicked eyes, the eyes of someone just pulled up from the bottom of the water.

"That girl," he said through the gauze, his voice thick. "That girl is the one I remember. That's Maren."

Lena didn't answer. She held the gauze tight and counted his heart rate down. A hundred and twenty. A hundred and ten. Ninety-eight. Her hands were steady. When she had something to do, her hands were always steady. But her eyes never left the screen.

The decoder had crashed, but the last frame was still frozen on the secondary monitor, blurred and grainy but clear enough: the girl's face, the mouth open in a scream, the eyes staring straight out of the frame.

Lena had seen that face before, exactly once. In the drawer of Margaret's nightstand there was a photograph, the only photograph of her as a child, taken in the hospital a few days after "the accident." Ragged black hair, chopped short as if someone had cut it in a hurry with scissors. Dark eyes staring straight into the lens, not smiling.

The two faces were identical.

"Are you all right?" Elias was looking at her. The bleeding had stopped.

She wasn't all right at all. The fingers of her right hand, resting on the armrest right next to his, were tapping. Three quick, two slow. And she could not make them stop.
