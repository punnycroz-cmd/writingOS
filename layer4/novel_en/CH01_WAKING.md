# CHAPTER 1: WAKING

In the twenty-ninth year of a life that wasn't his, Elias noticed the kitchen clock had no numbers.

It hung where it always hung, above the refrigerator, a round white plastic face with a second hand that ticked along as steadily as ever. Where the numbers should have been there was only a clean white ring. He stood looking at it with a cup of coffee going cold in his hand and tried to remember how long it had been like that, and couldn't. All he knew was that every morning for twenty-nine years he had glanced up at that clock, and not once had he known what time it was. The coffee always tasted faintly of salt, too, and he had never once wondered why.

The kitchen was painted yellow, the yellow of butter left out on a summer counter. The curtains were blue gingham. On the windowsill sat a pot of basil that was half dead, and for twenty-nine years it had stayed exactly half dead, never dying the rest of the way, never coming back. He had never watered it. He had never seen anyone else water it either.

The woman at the window turned around.

She always turned around at this point. Dark hair in a loose knot, a gray sweater three sizes too big with the sleeves shoved up to her elbows, a mug held in both hands like she was warming them. She smiled, easy and unguarded, the smile he had seen, by his count, on more than ten thousand mornings. He had never asked her name. Somewhere in him was the certainty that if he asked, something would break.

That morning, in the middle of the smile, her face changed.

It lasted half a second. Her features drew in and shrank, a gray smudge appeared on one cheek, her hair turned ragged at the shoulders as if someone had hacked it off with craft scissors, and the woman became a girl of nine who was screaming. He couldn't hear the scream. He could only see the wide-open mouth and the dark eyes fixed on him, as though he were the one thing in the world she needed to see.

Then she was the woman with the coffee mug again, smiling, as if nothing had happened.

And for the first time in twenty-nine years, he noticed that she wasn't smiling at him. She was looking past his shoulder, toward the kitchen doorway, where nobody stood.

The cup slipped out of his hand and hit the floor. It didn't break. It lay there on its side, and not a drop of coffee spilled.

That was when someone knocked on the wall behind him.

Three quick. Two slow.

He turned. The wall behind the stove was an outside wall. On the other side there was nothing but a patch of yard and a wooden fence. There was no one there. There couldn't be anyone there.

Three quick. Two slow.

He knew that knock. He had never heard it in his life.

His hand came up before he could think about it. His knuckles touched the yellow paint, and he knocked back, three quick, two slow, as smoothly as if he had done it a thousand times before. The wall was warm. Then it was hot. The paint under his hand puffed up into little blisters and split open, and through the cracks came smoke, thin as thread.

"Honey?" the woman at the window called, still looking at the empty doorway. The steam rising off her coffee had thickened into smoke.

The kitchen started to peel away like old wallpaper, and behind the yellow there were other things. A train station he didn't recognize, at midnight. The deck of a ship, salt spray slapping his face, though he was sure he had never set foot on a ship. A small child calling him Dad. A man calling him by a name that wasn't his. A woman singing a lullaby in a language he didn't speak. A white corridor with two doors set right next to each other. Thirty years of someone's life, of several someones' lives, stacked like panes of glass, and every pane cracking at once.

Then fire, fire everywhere, licking up the ceiling and swallowing all of it.

He reached into the middle of the blaze. He couldn't hold on to the kitchen, or to the woman. He managed to grab only one thing: the lips of the nine-year-old girl in the smoke, shaping a name.

---

The first thing to come back was the noise, a hum as thin as a wire that ran the length of the ceiling and threaded all the way down into the roots of his teeth. He lay there listening to it for a long time before he understood it was a sound, and longer still before he understood it was a fluorescent light. *Sixty hertz.* The thought arrived as neatly as a reading on a meter, and he had no idea how he knew it.

Apart from that number, he knew nothing at all.

He didn't know where he was, or what day it was, or what year. He didn't know how old he was, what he did for a living, whom he had loved or hated, or what he was supposed to have eaten for breakfast. Every time he reached for one of those things, his hand met a smooth, cold blankness, like a sheet of glass laid over the mouth of a pit whose bottom he couldn't see.

And yet one thing had stayed.

*Maren.*

Five letters, with no voice attached and no smell, lying quietly in the middle of his chest. It was heavy and solid, like a pebble in a child's fist, the kind of thing a child refuses to let go of no matter how the grown-ups coax.

With the name came two faces: a nine-year-old girl screaming through smoke, and a woman holding a coffee mug by a window. The kitchen had burned. The thirty years had burned. But those two faces were still here. He didn't know who they were, but every part of him insisted that they were the same person, and that her name was Maren.

"Heart rate's up to one-twenty," a woman's voice said somewhere to his left. "He's coming out of Delta. Dr. Bradley? He's waking up."

He opened his eyes and the light slapped him across the face. Tears ran on reflex, down his temples and into his ears. The ceiling was white, perforated acoustic tiles laid in a perfect grid, and directly above him a crack spread like a spiderweb from the corner of an air vent.

He looked at the crack and started counting the branches. One main line. Three smaller ones on the left. Five on the right. Strangely, with every branch he counted, the pit behind his eyes drew back a little further. He had the feeling he had done this many times before, in some place he could no longer remember.

A face pushed into his field of vision: a nurse in clear safety glasses, lines of amber numbers scrolling across the lenses. She shone a penlight into his left eye, then his right.

"Can you hear me? How many fingers am I holding up?"

"Two." His voice came out like sandpaper on wood, his throat raw in the particular way of someone who has just had a breathing tube pulled out.

"Good. Do you know where you are?"

"No."

"Halloway Medical Center, Neurotrauma Unit. I'm Nurse Vance. Do you remember your name?"

He shut his eyes and let a line down into the dark, and something caught on the end of it, weightless and stiff as a plastic card.

"Elias," he said. "Elias Vane."

"Date of birth?"

The white wall again. "I don't know."

"What year is it?"

"I don't know."

"Do you remember why you're here?"

The question touched something in his head, and that something burst open.

He saw the yellow kitchen again, from high above this time, tiny, as if he were looking down a well, and he was falling toward it through layer after layer of light, each layer a stretch of a life. Five years old, standing in the rain outside a wooden house. Fourteen, sitting on the steps of a train station. Twenty, thirty, forty. Faces flashed by too fast to hold, names people called him and none of them his, and at the bottom of everything, still, fire.

Thirty years. He had lived thirty whole years in there, and then watched them shatter like a window.

His back arched off the mattress. The heart monitor by the bed started beeping in a frantic run, and Nurse Vance flinched back half a step.

"Easy, Mr. Vane."

A middle-aged man stepped into view, thinning gray hair, heavy bags under his eyes, a lab coat creased at the elbows. He put a hand on Elias's shoulder and pressed him back down, not hard, but firmly.

"Breathe slowly. Your brain is trying to reorganize some damaged storage areas. What you just saw is noise. None of it's real."

"Thirty years," Elias said through his teeth. "I lived thirty years in there."

Dr. Bradley was quiet for a moment. It was a short moment, but long enough for Elias to see his eyes slide to Nurse Vance before they came back.

"You took part in a cognitive synchronization trial," Bradley said. His voice was smooth, like he was reciting something he'd learned by heart. "The system had a voltage fault. You had an atypical seizure and were in a coma for eleven days. What you've just been through was produced by your brain while you were under. It'll fade."

*Eleven days.* Elias didn't understand why that number made his spine go cold. A calculation ran through his head by itself, as fast and automatic as the counting of the cracks: eleven days multiplied by some factor he couldn't recall came out to thirty years.

"Five milligrams of a sublingual vasodilator," Bradley told the nurse. "Keep the room quiet. Dr. Halloway wants the full log before one o'clock."

He left. The door slid shut behind him with a soft hiss.

The pill dissolved under Elias's tongue, bitter and cold, and the pain behind his eyes faded to a dull throb. Nurse Vance had her back to the bed, bent over a panel on the wall, typing.

Elias looked around the room. An IV stand, the heart monitor, a locker in the corner, a curtained window with a gray sky behind it. At the foot of the bed, clipped to the steel rail, was an aluminum chart holder with actual paper in it. In a place this full of machines, he thought, there was only one reason to keep something on paper. Somebody didn't want it on a network.

He inched forward. His calves shook from eleven days of lying still, but his hand reached the chart. Nurse Vance was still typing.

Across the cover sheet, a line had been stamped diagonally in red ink:

> **SUBJECT ZERO — PROJECT MNEMOSYNE**
> **Access: Level 0**
> **Post-incident status: Extensive loss of autobiographical memory. Unidentified data fragment retained.**
> **Discharge directive: Release under Alpha-7 Monitoring Protocol. Neural data to be transmitted directly to Dr. I. Halloway.**
> **DO NOT INTERCEPT. Allow subject to move freely.**

He read it three times.

Subject Zero. A number used in place of a person's name. *Allow subject to move freely.* They hadn't even bothered to call him a patient. He didn't understand all of it, but one thing he understood perfectly: they didn't intend to keep him here. They wanted him to leave, and they wanted to see where he went.

That frightened him more than anything he had seen in the thirty shattered years.

The second page was a discharge form with an address printed on it. *Apartment 6C, 118 Harlow Street.* His home, presumably. He tore the page out, folded it in quarters, and tucked it into the waistband of his pants.

Nurse Vance still hadn't turned around.

He lowered his feet to the floor. The tile was ice-cold, and his knees nearly gave, but he stayed up. In the locker there were gray sweatpants, a dark shirt, and an oversized navy wool coat, probably from the hospital's lost-and-found bin. He put them on. His fingers shook on the first button, less on the third, and by the last one they had stopped.

"Mr. Vane!" Nurse Vance's voice shot up. "What are you doing? You haven't been cleared to get out of—"

He turned and looked at her. He didn't know what his face looked like right then. He only knew that she stopped talking.

"I've been discharged," he said. "Read the chart."

She looked at him, then at the chart on the bed. And she did something he hadn't expected. She turned back to her panel and didn't say another word.

Elias walked out into the corridor.

---

Halloway Medical Center was a white maze where every hallway looked like every other hallway: polished floors, even light, sliding doors without handles. People passed him without a glance, doctors, technicians, a janitor pushing a cart. Nobody asked him anything. Nobody stopped him. That indifference was what made his skin crawl.

He followed the green exit signs. At one turn there was a glass wall overlooking the main lobby, and on the far side hung a giant screen running an ad. A family of three sat in a sunlit garden. The little girl was drawing a whale flying through the sky, and then the whale swam right off the paper. Beside her sat a small fox that glowed pale blue. It cocked its head at the whale, then turned to look straight at the viewer and wagged its tail. Words faded in: *SOMNIA. The world you dream, every day. With ECHO, the companion who knows you best. A Halloway Neuro-Systems product.*

Elias stopped to watch. He didn't remember what Somnia was, but his hands curled on their own, as if around a game controller. So the muscles still remembered things the mind had lost.

Then he caught his own reflection in the glass: a tall, thin man whose shoulders hunched forward a little, as if trying to take up as little space in the world as possible. Dark hair plastered flat. High cheekbones. Gray eyes sunk deep behind two dark rings. He looked at that man and waited for some flicker of recognition. Nothing came. The man in the glass looked back at him like a stranger.

He raised his hand and touched the glass. The other man touched back, and their fingertips met at one cold point. He asked himself who he was, and the blank space in his head didn't answer. There was only the pebble in his chest, and the five letters carved into it.

He lowered his hand and kept walking.

The main doors slid open and the outside air rushed in, cold and damp, full of the smell of rain on asphalt and exhaust. The leaves on the trees across the street had gone yellow at the edges. Early September, probably. A fine drizzle hung in the air without quite falling, settling on his face and hair like a film.

He stood on the steps, and for the first time since he'd opened his eyes, he let himself take a deep breath.

Across the street sat a black sedan with no civilian plates. The windows were tinted dark, and the rear end rode a little low, the way cars do when someone has added steel in places nobody can see. The engine wasn't running. It was just parked.

Elias looked at it for exactly one second and turned away. He didn't need to look longer, because when he started walking, he heard its engine start, and when he turned the corner, he heard it roll after him. He didn't know where he had learned to listen like that, either.

They wanted him to move freely? Fine. He would move.

He took out the folded paper and read the address again. He had no idea where Harlow Street was, but there were street signs out here, and maps at bus stops, and he still had a brain that knew how to count.

Before he went, he closed his eyes one more time to make sure. The girl was still there. The woman by the window was still there. And the name was still there, heavy and warm.

He didn't know who Maren was, where she was, whether she was alive or dead, whether she remembered him or would ever want to see him again. He only knew that out of a whole life wiped clean, this was the one thing left. And if something had survived a thing like that, it had to have a reason.

Elias turned up the collar of his coat and stepped out into the rain. Behind him, the black sedan pulled quietly away from the curb and followed.
