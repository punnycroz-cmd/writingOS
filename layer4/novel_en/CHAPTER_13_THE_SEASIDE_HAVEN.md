# THE NAME THAT SURVIVED
## CHAPTER 13: THE SEASIDE HAVEN (FULL NOVEL EDITION)

The coastal highway ran along the jagged, razor-sharp crest of towering slate cliffs, with the roaring Atlantic churning fifty meters below in a violent fury of white foam, black rock, and freezing spray.

For two hours, Elias drove with cold, calculated precision, using the dense coastal fog and the treacherous hairpin turns of the cliffside road to shake their pursuers. By three in the afternoon, the flashing strobe lights of the black SUVs had vanished from the rearview mirror, swallowed by the gray squall behind them.

By four o'clock, the sedan was dying.

White steam whistled violently from the radiator grille, carrying the sweet, noxious stench of boiling ethylene glycol. The temperature gauge on the instrument cluster was pinned in the flashing red zone, and the transmission shuddered with a sickening, metallic clatter every time Elias downshifted.

"We won't make another five miles," Maren said, her hand resting on the dashboard, feeling the violent vibrations rattling through the chassis. "The water pump has seized."

"We don't need five miles," Elias said, his voice calm. "We're here."

He turned the steering wheel hard to the right, steering the sputtering sedan down an overgrown, unpaved dirt track concealed behind a thick wall of gorse bushes and a rusted iron gate hanging off its hinges.

The track wound toward the edge of a rocky promontory jutting a quarter-mile out into the open ocean. 

At the tip of the headland, rising like an ancient granite sentinel against the bruised purple twilight sky, stood **The Point Lookout Beacon**. 

It was a 19th-century coastal lighthouse constructed of dark, interlocking granite blocks, flanked by a squat, reinforced concrete bunker built during the height of the Cold War to serve as an early-warning coastal microwave telecommunications relay station. Rusted steel guy-wires stretched from the top of the eighty-foot stone tower, anchoring an industrial high-gain microwave dish antenna that pointed out toward the open sea.

Elias killed the engine. The sedan shuddered one last time and died, steam billowing into the wet air.

"My squad used this relay station during the winter competitive leagues three years ago," Elias explained as they stepped out into the biting ocean wind. The air smelled of salt, wet kelp, and ozone. "The municipal grid abandoned the facility in the nineties, but the underground microwave backbone is still hardwired into the transatlantic fiber cable that surfaces at the naval depot ten miles north. It has an industrial diesel generator in the sub-basement, and the lower bunker is encased in three-foot-thick steel-reinforced concrete with a copper Faraday mesh. Halloway’s directional scanners can’t penetrate this rock."

They hauled their gear up the slippery stone path leading to the heavy iron door of the keeper's quarters.

Elias fished a rusted brass skeleton key from a hidden notch beneath a granite step, inserted it into the heavy mortise lock, and threw his weight against the iron panel.

With a deep, groaning screech of weathered hinges, the door swung inward.

Inside, the air was chilly, dry, and smelled pleasantly of old cedar timber, brass polish, dried sage, and sea salt. 

They carried the heavy field kit and computer satchels up a winding iron spiral staircase that curled upward into the lantern room at the top of the tower.

The lantern room was magnificent in its desolate beauty. A thirty-foot diameter circle enclosed in thick, curved panoramic storm-glass offered an uninterrupted three-hundred-and-sixty-degree view of the vast, churning Atlantic. Far below, massive swells crashed against the jagged reefs with a deep, rhythmic thunder that resonated through the stone foundation like the heartbeat of a sleeping leviathan.

Elias descended into the sub-basement. Three minutes later, the low, comforting rumble of an industrial diesel generator vibrated through the floorboards.

Along the curved walls of the lantern room, old incandescent brass lamps flickered to life, bathing the dark timber and charts in a soft, warm amber glow that pushed back the freezing darkness of the approaching storm.

Maren set her carbon-fiber field rig onto a heavy oak chart table in the center of the room. She sank into a high-backed wooden chair, resting her face in her hands, her shoulders slumping as forty-eight hours of terror, running, and physical exhaustion finally caught up with her.

Elias walked over, setting a chipped porcelain mug of hot water on the table in front of her.

"We have power," he said softly, looking out at the dark horizon. "We have bandwidth. And for the next eight hours... we are safe."

Maren looked up at him through her fingers, a faint, exhausted smile touching her lips for the first time since the waking.

"Then let's make it count," she whispered.
