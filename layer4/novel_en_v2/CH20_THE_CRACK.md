# CHAPTER 20: THE CRACK

Kev's technical file was forty-three pages long, and Lena read it twice the first night.

They stopped at a roadside motel called the Pine Rest Motor Court, in a town whose name she couldn't remember, because it had one traffic light and a doughnut shop that closed at six. Two adjoining rooms: Kev in one, Lena and Linh in the other with two single beds and a wall-mounted TV that only got the weather channel and the shopping channel.

By eleven, Linh was asleep, curled up under the blanket with her mouth slightly open, still hugging the bag of hard drives. Lena sat on the other bed with her back against the headboard and Kev's old laptop on her knees, the light of the screen falling across the stained flowered wallpaper.

The file was written in Kev's dry style: brainwave charts, timestamps, a cross-sectional scan of Elias's left temporal lobe taken from the first session at her clinic, with a small area circled in red.

*Conditioned neuron cluster. Estimated 4,000–6,000 neurons. Synchronous firing on a 40-minute cycle, frequency 0.3 Hz, with encoded identifier.*

*Proposed approach: reconditioning via deep Layer 2. Requires a guide capable of direct synchronization with the subject's Theta rhythm.*

*Only candidate: L. Hale.*

Kev had underlined the last line, though he hadn't needed to. She'd figured it out by page three.

Lena closed the laptop.

She knew what she should do. She knew it perfectly well. If this were a case at the clinic, if the man with the red circle on his brain were any patient at all, she would read the file, schedule the sessions, prepare the equipment, and do it. No need to feel anything. No need to forgive anyone. Just do the work.

She picked up her phone, the prepaid Linh had given her, and opened the crow app. Her thumb hovered over Elias's name in the contacts for a moment, then slid to a different conversation.

The black crescent moon on purple. The two messages from Nyx she hadn't answered. And one message she had sent, at two in the morning two nights ago, while everyone in Viper's house was asleep.

> **Lena:** How long did you know Thomas?

No reply. Just two gray check marks: sent, seen.

She hadn't told anyone about this message. She told herself it was because there was no reply yet, so there was nothing to tell. She told herself Kev would object, and telling him would only make trouble.

Lena looked at her own message for a long time.

She thought about Elias that afternoon, standing in the middle of Viper's living room, not knowing what to do with his hands while everyone rushed past him. She had seen it, and she'd thought: he looks like someone waiting to be sentenced. At the time she hadn't understood why.

Two nights ago, when she sent this message, she had been waiting in exactly the same way.

She turned off the screen and put the phone face down on the blanket. Then she picked it up again.

---

He answered on the first ring, as if he'd been holding the phone in his hand.

"Lena."

"I read the file," she said.

Silence on the line. There was a low hum, like a refrigerator or an idling engine. They were probably sitting in the taco truck, almost time to move again.

"Kev says you're the only one who can do it," he said.

"Kev's right." She looked at the yellowed flowers on the wallpaper. "Technically it isn't hard. Three or four dives, twenty minutes each. It needs somewhere quiet. It needs me monitoring in person."

"Okay."

"I haven't agreed."

Silence again. She could hear him breathing.

"Ten days," she said. She hadn't meant to say it. It just came out. "Ten days, Elias. Ten days I slept in that house. Ten days Rosie came home from school and ran across that yard. Ten days I went into your head, and you lay there with your eyes closed, and you knew."

"I know."

"I told you about my mother. On the roof. I told you about Thomas, about the creek, about the apple. I told you things I've never told anyone, not even Corwin." Her voice was starting to rise. She brought it down fast and glanced over at Linh asleep. "And you let me tell you, knowing full well that every forty minutes your head was sending my location to the woman who killed my mother."

He didn't say anything.

"You led them to me," she said. "From the very first day. Then you led them to Viper's house. To Rosie."

"I know."

"Stop saying that."

A long, shaky breath. "Then what do you want me to say?"

She didn't know either. She wanted him to argue. She wanted him to say he had his reasons, that he was scared, that he meant to tell her and couldn't find the moment. She wanted him to give her something to be angry at, something to tear down. But he didn't give her anything. He just sat there on the other end of the line and took all of it.

"You're not going to defend yourself?" she asked.

"There's nothing to defend."

"Sure there is. You were afraid I'd leave, weren't you? You were afraid that if I knew, I'd look at you like a transmitter, not like..." She stopped. She didn't know how that sentence was supposed to end.

The silence on the other end went on so long she thought she'd lost the signal.

"I didn't have anything else," he said finally, very quietly. "Before I met you, I had nothing. No memories, no family, nobody. Just a name. Then I found you, and you remembered me. Someone remembered me." He paused. "I didn't want to lose that. I know that isn't a reason. I know it's selfish. But it's the truth."

Lena closed her eyes.

She had examined hundreds of statements. She knew what a true one sounded like. It was never smooth. There was always a lag in it, a place where the speaker had to stop because they knew the next thing they said would make them look worse. What he had just said had exactly that lag in it.

And the worst part was that she understood it, understood it so well it made her sick. Because two nights ago, at two in the morning, she had messaged a stranger online, someone who might be an enemy, just because that person had called her by her real name, just because that person *knew* her. And she hadn't told anyone, because she was afraid they'd make her stop.

She opened her eyes. She didn't say anything about that.

"I need time," she said.

"I know."

"Elias."

"...Sorry."

She meant to say something else, though she didn't know what. Something different. Something gentler, or one more thing to hurt him. She wasn't sure which she wanted.

On the other end of the line came Viper's voice, distant and gruff. "Hey. Hey, Vane. You're bleeding."

Rustling. Something falling.

"Elias?" Lena sat bolt upright.

"It's fine," he said, his voice muffled. "Just my nose."

"Head down. Don't tip it back—"

"I know."

"Do you have the Alpha-9? I gave Marcus two vials, the left one—"

"Lena." Viper's voice, closer now. He had taken the phone. "I've got it. You go to sleep."

"Marcus—"

"Go to sleep, Doc." His voice was tired but not angry. "He'll still be alive in the morning. I promise."

The line went dead.

Lena sat there holding the phone, watching the screen go dark. In the next room she could hear Kev pacing, then the electric kettle. Kev wasn't asleep. Kev never slept.

Across the room, Linh rolled over and opened one eye.

"You okay?" she asked, her voice thick with sleep.

"I'm okay."

"You're as bad a liar as he is."

Lena didn't answer. Linh looked at her a little longer, then closed her eyes and pulled the blanket up to her chin.

"I'm really mad at him," Linh said, her eyes still shut. "So mad. But I'll be over it by tomorrow morning. I know I will. I can only stay mad at someone for one night." She paused. "I don't know how long you can stay mad."

"I don't know either."

"You know, when I first joined the team, I made us lose a match once because I went out with friends without telling anyone and disconnected in the middle of the game. He didn't say anything. Didn't yell at me at all. The next day he sent me exactly one message: *Next time, give us a heads-up.*" Linh yawned. "That was it. And I cried all night over that one line."

Lena looked up at the ceiling. In the corner there was a water stain shaped like Italy. She looked at it for a long time before she realized why she kept looking at it.

"Go to sleep, Linh," she said.

She picked up her phone one last time and opened the conversation with the crescent moon. Still no reply.

She typed a message to Kev, deleted it, typed it again, deleted it again. In the end she sent only:

> **Lena:** I need to talk to you in the morning. Not about Elias. About Nyx.

She put the phone down, turned off the light, and lay there watching the Italy-shaped stain sink into the dark.
