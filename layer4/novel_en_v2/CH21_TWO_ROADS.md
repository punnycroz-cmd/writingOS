# CHAPTER 21: TWO ROADS

**I.**

Kev heard the whole story without interrupting once, eating a glazed doughnut from the shop that had just opened at six. When Lena finished, he licked the sugar off his thumb and asked:

"What time?"

"Two-fourteen in the morning. The night of the twentieth."

"From Lin's phone?"

"Yes."

Kev nodded as if that was exactly the information he needed. He took out his phone, typed something, and set it on the plastic table between them.

"I've known since the morning of the twenty-first," he said.

Lena looked at him.

"Lin's phones run through Lin's server. Lin's server keeps logs. Lin's too lazy to ever read the logs. I read them." He took another bite of doughnut. "I didn't say anything because I wanted to see if you'd tell me yourself."

"And I didn't."

"Three days you didn't. Elias didn't for ten. I didn't tell Lin about Elias for ten. Lin hasn't told her mom she dropped out two years ago." He wiped his fingers on a napkin. "This team is good at a lot of things. Talking to each other isn't one of them."

Lena didn't know whether to laugh or be ashamed, so she did both.

"She didn't answer you," Kev said. "That tells me something. If she were Halloway's, she'd have answered right away. Sweet-talked you. Pulled you in. People who are trying to lure you tend to talk a lot." He folded the napkin into quarters. "This one talks very little. Like she's waiting to see whether you're worth answering."

"Or waiting to see where we go."

"Could be." Kev stood up. "Do you want to know who she is?"

"Do you know?"

"No. But there's someone who might." He looked at her. "Corwin's mother."

---

Ruth Daly lived in a small gray-shingled house in Bayside, three blocks from the pier where they had held the memorial for her son. Lena had been there a hundred times: Sunday dinners, Thanksgivings, and the night of the worst fight she and Corwin ever had, when she ran off to sit in this kitchen drinking ginger tea until midnight while Ruth asked nothing at all and just kept refilling her cup.

Kev parked two blocks away and circled the neighborhood first, checking every parked car, every window, every utility pole. Fifteen minutes later he came back and nodded.

"Nobody. Or they're better than me." He got back behind the wheel. "Lin and I'll wait here twenty minutes. If I honk twice, you come out right away."

Lena knocked.

Ruth opened the door and froze. She was sixty, small, with short silver hair, wearing the moss-green sweater Corwin had given her two Christmases ago, reading glasses pushed up on her head, a TV remote in one hand. From the living room behind her came the sound of the news, a female anchor reading Lena's name.

"Lena," Ruth said.

"I'm sorry for showing up without calling, Ruth, I—"

Ruth had already pulled her inside, slammed the door, and thrown her arms around her, holding her so tight that the glasses fell off her head and clattered on the wooden floor.

"The TV says you were kidnapped," Ruth said into her shoulder. "I didn't believe it. I called your friend, that Nia, and she didn't believe it either. She said you'd called her and said you were okay." She let go, held Lena by both shoulders, and looked her over from head to foot like a mother checking her child after an illness. "You've gotten thin."

"I'm fine, Ruth."

"You're a terrible liar." Ruth bent to pick up her glasses. "You always were, since you were little."

---

The kitchen was exactly the same. The oak table with a round burn mark in the corner, where Corwin at twelve had set down a hot pot. The refrigerator door covered with photos: Corwin in his graduation cap; Corwin and Lena on a beach, her trying to grab back the hat he'd just snatched; Thomas in uniform, grinning, holding a curly-haired boy on one arm whom Lena knew was Corwin at three.

Ruth made ginger tea. Lena said nothing until the mug was in her hands, hot enough to nearly burn.

Then she told her. Not everything. She didn't mention Elias, or Layer 4, or the white pod and day one hundred ninety-two. She didn't know how to say those things to a mother. She only told her about the creek, the yellow raincoat, how Thomas had pulled the edge of the coat over the number on the little girl's chest.

Ruth held her mug and listened without drinking. When Lena finished, she sat quietly for a long time.

"I knew," she said.

Lena set her mug down.

"Not all of it," Ruth went on. "He never told me. Not one word. That morning he didn't get home until five, soaking wet and stinking of smoke. He took a shower and sat down right at this table, and he cried. I'd never seen him cry before. Then he said there was a child who had to be hidden, and I wasn't to ask anything, because if I knew, I'd be in danger." She looked at Lena. "A week later Margaret, my best friend, three years a widow with no children of her own, suddenly took in a nine-year-old girl with no papers except a temporary certificate from social services."

"You never asked?"

"I was afraid to ask." Ruth smiled sadly. "He was a good man, Thomas. Too good. He believed some truths had to be kept hidden to protect people. I was never so sure, but I loved him, so I kept it hidden with him."

"Did Corwin know?"

"No. Never." Ruth shook her head firmly. "He grew up with you like the girl next door. And then he loved you. I thought about telling him, many times. But tell him what? That I thought his father had hidden a child from some dangerous people, and I thought that child was you? I had no proof. Just an old woman's feeling." She paused. "Then Thomas died. Then Corwin started digging. And I was even more afraid to say anything."

Lena looked at the photo on the refrigerator, Thomas in his uniform, smiling.

"Ruth," she said. "Did Thomas ever mention someone called N? Or Nyx?"

Ruth was silent. Then she got up, went into the living room, and came back with a cookie tin, the round blue kind printed with little soldiers in tall red fur hats. She set it on the table and took off the lid. There were no cookies inside, only a few small black notebooks, the kind police officers use, stacked and held together with a rubber band.

"Thomas's notebooks," Ruth said. "Corwin took most of them for his investigation. I hid these. I didn't want him reading them." She picked up the top one and opened it to a page marked with an old grocery receipt. "But maybe you should."

Thomas's handwriting was small, slanted, crowded.

*N. called back at 11 p.m. Has a warehouse address on Delancey. Seven kids. She won't say how she knows. I didn't ask.*

Another page.

*N. again. Wants a trade: a name taken off a federal wanted list. I said no. She gave me the information anyway. She said, "I'm not doing this for you. I'm doing it for the kids." Can't trust her. But ten times out of ten she's been right.*

Another page, dated October 2004.

*N. warned me about Prometheus three weeks ago. I didn't listen. Told her a chemical warehouse was nothing to worry about. Now there's a child in Margaret's house, and I owe N. an apology.*

Lena read that line three times.

The last page of the last notebook was dated May 3, 2012. Eleven days before Thomas died.

*N. says turn off the Echo in the car. I told her she's paranoid. She said, "Echo hears everything, Tom. It doesn't keep anybody's secrets." I turned off the Echo on my phone last year. The one in the car is wired into the steering. Can't be turned off. Getting a different car tomorrow.*

*If anything happens: N. has a copy of everything. The password is the girl's real name.*

Lena put the notebook down.

"He never got a different car," Ruth said softly. "Eleven days later, the Echo in the car misread a sign on the bridge. The carmaker sent a letter of apology. That Halloway company, they made the software for that Echo, sent flowers. I still remember the card. *With our deepest sympathy.*" She looked out the window. "Corwin tore that card to pieces. He was nineteen that year."

---

Before Lena left, Ruth wrapped two slices of banana bread in foil for her, the way she did every time Lena visited. At the door, she took Lena's hand.

"That young man," Ruth said. "The one on TV. Elias something. Is he a good man or a bad one?"

Lena looked at her. She meant to say good. She meant to say he was the one who stayed behind in a fire so she could get out. She meant to say a lot of things.

"He's an idiot," she said. "But he isn't bad."

Ruth looked at her for a long time, the look of someone who had lived sixty years and raised a son who was a terrible liar.

"Corwin was an idiot too," she said. "And you loved him anyway."

Out on the street, a car horn honked twice.

---

**II.**

Viper had hidden the taco truck in an abandoned warehouse on the outskirts of a river town. The tin roof was full of holes, and the late-afternoon sun came through in dusty columns.

"Kev said not to do it," Viper said for the fourth time.

"Kev said it needs someone who understands my brain," Elias said. He was sitting on a wooden crate with Viper's laptop on his knees, Kev's technical file open. "Nobody understands my brain better than I do."

"That's the dumbest thing I ever heard."

"Probably."

It had been three days. Three days, six towns, eighteen moves. Every forty minutes his brain sent out a pulse, and they had to run again. Viper hadn't slept more than two hours at a stretch, the taco truck had been refueled four times, and Lena still hadn't called back.

Elias had read Kev's file ten times. The proposed approach required a guide who could sync with his Theta rhythm, and Lena could do it because their brains had locked together when they were children. But in the appendix, Kev had added a second approach in italics, with a note: *Not recommended.*

*Self-guided. Subject dives into the conditioned zone and breaks the rhythm from inside. Risk: no one outside to pull him up. If control is lost, the Layer 4 zone may reactivate.*

"Did you read the *not recommended* part?" Viper asked.

"I read it."

"Did you keep reading to the *Layer 4 zone may reactivate* part?"

"I read it."

"Then what the hell is wrong with you?"

Elias closed the laptop.

"You haven't slept in three days," he said. "Rosie's up in the mountains with Diego, no cell service, and you don't know if she's okay. Lena hasn't called back. Lin calls every night to yell at me, and then she cries." He stopped. "Every forty minutes I hurt everybody. If I can do this, even part of it, even just slow it down..."

Viper looked at him for a long time.

"My wife," he said, "died of a tumor nobody caught in time. She'd been having headaches for six months and didn't say a word. She said she didn't want to bother me, because the bakery was struggling back then." He spat on the concrete floor. "You know, you're a lot like her. Always thinking you're carrying it for everybody else."

"Will you steer for me?"

"I don't know how to do that."

"There are instructions. Kev wrote them out clearly." Elias opened the field kit Lena had left with Viper. The vials sat snug in the foam. "If my heart rate goes over a hundred thirty, you hit the red button. If I stop breathing, you inject the left vial."

Viper looked at the case for a long, long time.

"Twenty minutes," he said finally. "One second over and I pull the plug. I don't care where you are."

Elias almost laughed. He sounded exactly like Lena.

---

The conditioned zone wasn't like anywhere he had dived before. No white hallway, no glowing fish, no paint bucket. Just a dark, spherical room, and in the very center a small knot of light the size of a fist, beating rhythmically like a heart. Slow, very slow. With each beat it gave off a pale red wave that spread out, touched the curved wall, passed through it, and disappeared toward somewhere he couldn't see.

He moved closer. The knot of light was woven from thousands of tiny threads, each one a neuron. They beat in unison because someone had taught them to, over and over, a thousand times, when he was a little boy lying on the iron bed in Room Zero.

He put his hand on it. It was hot, and it recognized him. The instant his hand touched it, he heard a voice. Not a human voice. A smooth, even sound, like the voice of a system:

*Subject Zero. Confirm location. Transmit signal.*

He didn't know what Kev said to do next. The instructions talked about "reconditioning," about "breaking the rhythm from inside," but those were just words. In here there was only him and the knot of light beating in his hand.

He squeezed. The knot of light jerked, and a white-hot pain shot up his arm into his shoulder and into his head. He heard someone scream, very far away. Maybe it was him.

*Transmit signal. Transmit signal. Transm—*

He didn't let go. He squeezed harder. The threads in his hand began to snap, one by one, twanging like overstretched strings. The rhythm slowed. Slowed further.

Then the dark room began to crack. Behind it, behind the curved wall, there was something. He recognized it before he saw it: a feeling of falling, a feeling of thousands of lives crushed into a single moment, a temperature with no name.

*Layer 4.* It was right there, just behind the thin shell he had cracked, and it was pulling him in.

---

"VANE!"

Viper was shaking his shoulders. Elias knew that because his body was rocking, but he couldn't feel Viper's hands at all. From the neck down, he couldn't feel anything.

"Your eyes," Viper said, his voice cracking. "Your eyes are bleeding. Jesus. Vane, look at me—"

Elias wanted to say *I'm looking*, but his lips wouldn't move.

He saw Viper let go of his shoulders and dig through the case, pick up a vial, look at it, then at the one next to it, muttering *left, whose left, for crying out loud?*, then press the nozzle to Elias's neck. A hiss, a rush of cold. Not enough.

The world began to darken from the edges in, like an eye closing.

He heard Viper dialing, the phone ringing once, twice.

"Lena." All the depth had gone out of Viper's voice. It came out thin and high, like someone else's. "Lena, I'm sorry. I tried. He did it himself. He went down on his own. I can't pull him up—"

A pause. Her voice on the other end, tiny. He couldn't make out a word.

"I don't know," Viper said. "His eyes are bleeding. He can't talk. Lena—"

Elias couldn't hear anything after that. The dark had closed in to the middle of his eye.

"He's dying," Viper said, somewhere very far away. "You have to come. He's dying."
