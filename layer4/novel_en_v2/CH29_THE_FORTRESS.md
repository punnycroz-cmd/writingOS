# CHAPTER 29: THE FORTRESS

In Layer 3, they walked for eleven hours inside a fiber-optic cable.

Outside, it was under seven minutes. Elias knew it the way he knew a lot of things about Layer 3, not from memory but from his body. Years of Vanguard had taught his body how to read stretched time, how to keep from panicking when a five-minute match outside lasted a whole afternoon inside.

The inside of the cable was a cylindrical tunnel wide enough for two people to walk side by side, its walls made of a milky white light flowing endlessly forward like a river. The floor was transparent, and through it they could see the sea. Or rather, a reconstruction of the sea out of everything the data in this cable had ever described: sonar columns, depth charts, schools of fish counted by echo-sounder, all of it drifting below them like a giant aquarium built by someone who had never actually seen a fish.

"I'm hungry," Viper said in the seventh hour.

"You're not hungry," Kev said. "Your brain thinks you're hungry because it thinks you haven't eaten in seven hours."

"Then my brain's hungry."

"Here," Linh said, tossing Viper something she'd just conjured out of the air: a bánh mì stuffed with meat, cilantro, and pickled vegetables. "Saigon-style. It won't fill you up, but at least your brain will shut up."

Viper looked at the sandwich, took a bite, chewed, and his face slowly relaxed into something close to bliss.

"This is better than my chili," he said with his mouth full.

"I know."

Elias walked in front. His Layer 3 avatar was different from the one in the Crow's Nest. It was the armor he'd worn in Vanguard for three years: light, matte gray, blue energy lines running along the joints, two pulse blades crossed on his back. This body remembered everything, even the things he had forgotten.

And in some corner of his head, he could feel her. No voice, no picture. Just something like the warmth of a person sitting in the next room. When he walked faster, the warmth stretched like a rubber band. When he stopped, it eased. Now and then small pulses ran through it, quick and sharp when she was tense, slow and even when she was doing her four-two-six breathing.

In the eighth hour it jerked hard, as if someone had shoved her. Elias stopped short.

"What?" Linh asked.

"Something's happening out there." He closed his eyes and tried to feel it. The line was still taut, still there, the pulses quick and sharp but steady. "She's okay. She's... busy."

Kev looked at him but didn't ask anything else. He didn't need to. Out there, in the real world, nearly seven minutes had passed. Enough time for four trucks to reach the concrete building.

"Keep moving," Kev said. "Fast as we can."

---

In the eleventh hour the cable ended. The tunnel of light opened like an eye, and they stepped out onto a step of black stone polished like a mirror.

The Mnemosyne fortress sat in the middle of a dead city. That was Elias's first impression. Not ruined or abandoned. Dead the way a place is when nobody has ever lived in it. Tall black glass towers, square, identical, lined up in perfectly straight rows all the way to the horizon, with no windows and no doors. The streets between them were wide and completely empty, paved with the same glossy black stone under their feet. The sky was dark red, with no sun and no clouds, only lines of bright red hexadecimal characters drifting slowly down like snow.

In the center, about a mile and a quarter off, stood the fortress: a huge black hexagon ten times taller than the towers around it, ringed by three walls, each one higher than the last. Watchtowers stood along the top of each wall, evenly spaced like the teeth of a comb, and every tower had a red light sweeping back and forth.

"Oh," Linh said. "Oh, no."

"Three walls," Kev said. He had already unslung his sniper rifle and was looking through the scope. "Automated gunners on every tower. Inside the second wall, at least twenty people. Real people. Level-four admins." He lowered the scope. "They respawn from the server cache. Three seconds."

"Three seconds?" Viper said.

"Three seconds."

"So how are we supposed to fight that?"

"We don't fight," Elias said.

Everyone turned to look at him.

"We don't need to kill them all," he said. He was looking at the fortress, and his brain was doing what it always did: counting, calculating, measuring. How far apart the towers were. How long the red lights took to sweep a full circle. Where the blind spots were. "We just need to reach the inner gate. Lin cracks the lock. We go in, grab the archive, get out. We don't need to win. We just need to not lose before we get there."

"Like the Ice Cave map last winter," Linh said, her eyes lighting up. "We didn't kill the boss. We ran around it, grabbed the chest, and ran."

"Exactly."

"We lost that match," Viper said.

"Because you stopped to fight the boss," Linh said.

"It insulted my mother!"

"It was a bot, Viper. It doesn't know what a mother is."

Kev had started marking the watchtowers on a map floating in front of him. "Each light takes fourteen seconds to sweep around. At the northeast tower there's a four-second blind spot when two lights cross." He looked at Elias. "Enough for one person to get through. Not four."

"Then we make more blind spots," Elias said. "Viper, make noise. As much as you can. On the west side. Pull every light over there."

Viper grinned and hauled out his anti-tank railgun, bigger than he was, and shouldered it. "Noise I can do."

"Kev, get up high. That empty building." Elias pointed at the nearest black glass tower. "Drop the people inside the second wall when they run toward Viper. You don't have to kill them. Three-second respawn means we only need three seconds each time. Lin comes with me through the northeast blind spot, straight to the gate."

"And you?" Linh asked.

"I clear the way."

Nobody spoke. All three of them were looking at him. Then Viper said, more quietly than usual:

"Since when do you talk this much?"

Elias didn't know how to answer. He shrugged.

"Since somebody told me next time I have to say something," he said.

Linh turned away. Kev cleared his throat. Viper clapped him on the shoulder so hard his avatar nearly fell off the step.

"Let's go, captain," Viper said.

---

For twelve minutes the plan went exactly right. Twelve minutes in here. Seven seconds outside.

Viper swung around to the west, to an empty plaza right at the foot of the first wall, planted himself in the middle of it, aimed the railgun at the sky, and fired. The sound was like the sky being torn in half. A blazing orange column shot straight up and burned through the falling red characters, leaving a round hole in the sky.

Then he yelled. No words, just one long, loud yell, the yell of a man who had held everything in for thirty-eight years and was finally allowed to let it out.

Every red light on the west wall swung toward him, then the lights on the north wall, then the south.

"Go," Kev said over the comms.

Elias and Linh ran. Across the eastern plaza, along the base of the first wall, to the northeast watchtower. The red light on top had turned away, and the four-second blind spot had become ten seconds, twenty, because Viper was still yelling, still firing orange columns into the sky.

Elias drew a pulse blade and cut into the base of the wall, not to break it but to make handholds. The energy blade went into the black stone like a hot knife into wax. One notch, then another higher up, making steps. They climbed.

The first wall, then the second. At the second wall the first figures appeared, tall, in faceless black armor, with glowing red rifles. Four of them ran west toward Viper's yelling, and one turned his head. A small crack came from the distant black glass tower, and the soldier who had turned dropped, dissolving into red pixels. Three seconds later he reappeared in the same spot, standing there confused, with no idea where he had just been. Three seconds was enough. Elias and Linh were over.

"Nice one, Kev," Linh said, panting.

"I know," Kev said dryly.

The third wall was the highest, and it had no blind spot. Elias climbed first, cutting each step. Linh climbed after him, her small fingers clawing into the notches. Halfway up, her foot slipped. Elias reached down and grabbed her wrist and held on. She looked up at him. He looked down at her.

"Lose a match, you play it again," she said, out of breath. "Lose a person, you don't. Right?"

"Right."

He pulled her up.

---

Inside the third wall was a wide courtyard paved with black stone, completely empty. In the middle, about two hundred yards off, stood the gate of the fortress: a huge hexagonal door with red characters crawling around its face like ants.

And between them and the gate were the animals.

At first Elias didn't see them. They were standing still, black on black stone, with only a dull silver glow around their eyes. He didn't see them until they moved. Dozens. Maybe a hundred. Wolves, foxes, crows, cats, dogs, birds of no kind he could name. Every one of them black, every one of them watching.

"Echoes," Linh whispered, her voice shaking. "Those are... those are Echoes."

But they didn't belong to anyone. No usernames floating over their heads, no pink bows, no soap bubbles. Just the shapes of Echoes, empty, like suits of armor with nobody inside.

"Kev," Elias said into the comms. "You seeing this?"

A beat of silence. "I see it."

"What are they?"

"I don't know." For the first time, Kev sounded unsure. "They aren't in any of the documents."

The animals stayed where they were. They didn't attack. They only watched.

Then, from the middle of the pack, a small bird rose into the air. A crow, its black feathers glinting blue, its eyes ringed with dull silver. It circled once over the pack and landed right in front of Elias, three steps away, and tilted its head at him, one side and then the other.

Elias froze. He didn't remember it. He never had. But he had seen it once, in the Crow's Nest, perched on the railing, looking at him for a while before it flew away.

"Eli," Linh said, very softly. "That's Coal."

The crow gave a short, hoarse caw, like a question.

Then something boomed behind the gate, a mechanical roar. On top of the hexagonal door a gun emplacement rose out of the stone, a black three-barreled turret, turning slowly toward them, the red lights at the tips of the barrels brightening like the eyes of an animal waking up.

The crow turned its head to look at the turret, then looked back at Elias. Then it flew off and vanished into the black pack, and he couldn't tell it apart from the others anymore.
