# CHAPTER 23: THE ONE WHO STAYED

The boy was running, holding tight to another hand.

Elias knew this was a memory. He knew he was lying on the floor of a warehouse with his head in Viper's lap and a cable running from his head to Lena's. He knew all of that the way you know you're dreaming. But he couldn't feel any of it. All he could feel was cold, slippery linoleum under his cloth slippers, smoke stinging his nose, and the hand in his hand, a little bigger than the boy's, much smaller than his own now, the nails bitten short just like her mother's. And for the first time, he knew that the other person was feeling exactly this too, from the other side of the same hand.

"Don't look back," Maren's mother said.

She ran three steps ahead of them, a flashlight in one hand and a crowbar in the other. She had taken off her lab coat and tied it over her nose and mouth, and her left arm was angry red from wrist to elbow. The boy didn't know when she'd been burned. Probably when she opened his door. The handle had been scorching hot.

Behind them, the west corridor was burning. Not like in the movies, with pretty flames dancing. It burned like an animal swallowing everything, slowly and steadily, from the ceiling down. Chunks of ceiling drywall fell in burning sheets, and the red alarm lights spun through the smoke and made it look soaked in blood.

There were footsteps somewhere behind them, behind the walls. Boots pounding the floor, a lot of people, and a man shouting into a radio.

"Shaft three," Maren's mother said. "Hurry, both of you."

The boy ran faster. The hand in his hand squeezed tighter.

---

Ventilation shaft three was at the end of a side corridor, down at the base of the wall, as high as a grown-up's knee. The grate had already been taken off, its four screws lying on the floor. Maren's mother had prepared for this. The boy understood it the way children understand things, without words, only a certainty that this grown-up had been thinking about tonight for a very long time.

The opening was dark and narrow. Big enough for a child, not for an adult.

She knelt and shone the flashlight inside. "It comes out on the slope out back, down by the creek. Crawl straight. Don't turn. When you get out, run toward the road, not toward the fire. Understand?"

"Aren't you coming, Mamá?" Maren asked.

Elena looked at her daughter. The red alarm light swept across her face, went out, swept across it again.

"I won't fit, sweetheart."

"Then go a different way."

"Okay. I'll go a different way."

She said it too fast. The boy heard how fast, and he thought Maren heard it too.

The boots were closer now. At the head of the side corridor, where they had just run through, was a thick steel door with a brass bolt that Elena had shut behind them. On the other side, someone began pounding on it.

"Maren first," Elena said.

Maren knelt and put her head into the opening, then stopped, twisted back, and grabbed the boy's wrist.

"You're coming with me."

"It's too narrow. One at a time," the boy said.

"Then you go right behind me."

"Okay."

The steel door shook. A boom. The brass bolt bent a little.

Elena stood up, went to the door, and wedged the crowbar between the bolt and the frame. Her burned arm trembled as she bore down on it.

The boy looked at her, then at Maren, halfway into the shaft with her hand still locked on his wrist, then at the door.

Seven years in a place where grown-ups taught him to count. Count the time between tests. Count the beeps on the machine. Count the steps from Room Zero down to the lab on the lower floor. Now he counted without anyone telling him to.

The shaft was long, and Maren crawled slowly. If he crawled right behind her, it would take them at least a minute to reach the other end. The steel door wouldn't hold for a minute. Elena wouldn't hold for a minute. And when the door opened, the men in black would see the hole in the wall, see the grate taken off, shine their lights into the shaft, and see two pairs of feet. But if there was only one pair of feet, and that pair was already out the other end, they'd see an empty shaft. And a kid sitting in front of it.

Elias, twenty years later, felt the boy arrive at that decision. There was no bravery in it. No sacrifice. Just a calculation, done by a brain that had been trained to calculate, producing a result.

And he felt, from the other side of the hand, Lena understand it at the very same moment.

"Maren," the boy said. "Let go."

"No."

"Crawl. I'm right behind you."

"You're lying." She didn't let go. She held on so hard her bitten nails dug into his skin. "You're lying just like my mom."

The steel door let out a long groan of metal. Elena shouted something in Spanish.

With his other hand the boy pried Maren's fingers off his wrist, one, then another. She was crying. He wasn't. He didn't know why he wasn't. Maybe because he had been taught not to. Maybe because there was no time to cry.

The moment the last finger came loose, he gave her heel a gentle push, pushing her deeper into the shaft.

Maren turned back one more time. In the dark of the shaft, all he could see were her eyes, reflecting the red alarm light.

"My name is Maren!" she screamed. The scream bounced along the metal shaft, warped and echoing. "Don't forget me! Don't forget me! You promised you'd come find me!"

"I promise," the boy said.

"Say it again!"

"I promise. I'll come find you. Crawl, Maren."

She crawled. The scrape of knees and hands on metal got fainter, and fainter, and then it was gone.

The boy sat down and blocked the mouth of the shaft with his back.

---

The door didn't open because the men in black broke it down. It opened because the ceiling above it collapsed.

The boy saw Elena thrown backward, saw the crowbar go flying, saw a burning slab of drywall come down right where she had been standing, saw her crawl out of the rubble with half her face blackened and her burned arm no longer red.

She didn't crawl toward the door. She crawled toward him.

"Eli," she called, her voice raw, torn by smoke. "Eli, is she gone?"

"She's gone."

"Why didn't you go?"

The boy didn't answer. He just sat there, his back blocking the mouth of the shaft.

Elena looked at him, at the way he was sitting, at the way that tiny back covered the hole where the grate had been. And Elias saw on her blackened face an expression he would never be able to forget: the look of a mother watching her child do something so right and so wrong that it was unbearable.

She raised her unburned hand and laid it on his cheek.

"You silly boy," she said. "The two of you, just as silly as each other."

Behind her, through the smoke, the flashlights mounted on gun barrels were sweeping closer.

"Listen to me," Elena said, fast and urgent, her face close to his. "They're going to make you forget. They'll make you forget everything. Tonight, me, her, even your own name. Do you understand?"

The boy nodded, though he didn't understand at all.

"Keep her name," she said. "They can take anything else, but keep that name. Hide it somewhere very deep, somewhere they can't find it, until you find her again." Her hand trembled against his cheek. "Promise me."

"I promise."

Elena smiled, a smile on a blackened face, in the middle of smoke and fire and sirens, and it was the most beautiful smile the boy had ever seen.

Then she stood up, turned her back to him, turned her face toward the oncoming flashlights, and spread her arms wide across the narrow corridor. And she began to sing.

*Nana, niña, nana...*

At 3:10, the west corridor collapsed.

---

Elias opened his eyes, and he was crying.

He didn't know when he had started. He couldn't remember the last time he had cried. He didn't have a single memory of it. And yet the tears kept running down his temples and into his ears, and he couldn't make them stop.

Someone was holding his hand, so hard her nails dug into his skin. He turned his head.

Lena lay beside him on the concrete floor, her head on a rolled-up jacket, the sensor ring still on her forehead. She was crying too, without a sound. She opened her eyes and looked at him, and she didn't let go of his hand.

Viper sat between them, one hand on Elias's shoulder and one on Lena's, saying nothing. Linh sat at their feet with both hands over her mouth. Kev stood by the link box, looking at the screen, though it had gone dark a long time ago and he kept looking at it anyway.

"The signal's gone," Kev said, very quietly, as though afraid of breaking something. "Two hours now. Nothing."

Nobody answered him.

Lena spoke first. Her voice was hoarse, small, and completely unlike her usual voice. It sounded like a nine-year-old talking through a wall.

"You kept your promise."

Elias looked at her.

She hadn't said *Elias*. She hadn't said it in her careful, grown-up voice, the one with a table's width of distance built into it. She'd said it the way two kids talk through a brick wall in a building without calendars, when there's nobody else in the world to talk to.

"I didn't remember anything," he said, and his voice came out the same way hers had, plain and small, strange as a foreign word and familiar as breathing. "Twenty years. I didn't remember anything. Not you, not your mom, not that night."

"But you kept the name."

"She told me to."

Lena made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and held his hand tighter.

"My mom," she said. "My mom sang. At the end. Did you hear it?"

"I heard it."

"I always thought she was scared. For twenty years, even when I didn't remember anything, I had this feeling that somebody had died terrified because of me." She closed her eyes, and tears ran across the bridge of her nose and dripped onto the concrete. "She wasn't scared. She sang."

They lay there on the floor of an abandoned warehouse, holding hands, watching the last columns of afternoon light come through the holes in the tin roof, and neither of them said anything more.

Viper got up first. He wiped his face with his sleeve, cleared his throat, went to the taco truck, opened the fridge, and took out the half pot of chili they'd brought from Ashgrove.

"I'm heating this up," he announced to no one. "Anybody who doesn't eat goes hungry."

Linh laughed through her tears. She crawled over and lay down on Lena's other side, hugging her arm like a teddy bear.

Kev was still standing by the link box. He took off his glasses and wiped them on the hem of his shirt, for a long time, much longer than they needed.

---

They ate chili sitting cross-legged on the concrete floor, with plastic spoons and paper bowls, by the light of a flashlight hung from a hook in the rafters.

Lena sat next to Elias, not close, not far. Now and then, when she reached for bread, their shoulders touched.

She hadn't said she forgave him, and he hadn't apologized again. They both knew it wasn't finished. But it had moved somewhere else. It wasn't between the two of them anymore. It was between the two of them and everything that had been done to them.

Lena's phone buzzed on the concrete floor. She looked at the screen and saw the black crescent moon on purple.

> **[N]:** 1999. Thomas caught me selling a list of child-trafficking routes to the highest bidder. He didn't arrest me. He told me to pick a side.
> **[N]:** He left me an archive. The password is a name. I've been waiting to see if you'd remember it.

Lena looked at the screen for a long time. Everyone had stopped eating. They were watching her.

Kev set down his bowl. "Don't answer yet. She could be—"

Lena had already typed it.

> **Lena:** Maren.

Sent. Seen. Ten seconds, twenty seconds. Linh held her breath. Viper's spoon hung in midair.

The screen lit up.

> **[N]:** 43°58'12" N, 69°09'40" W. Point Lookout Light. An old Prometheus relay station.
> **[N]:** Corwin was there three days before he sailed. He left something for you.

Lena handed the phone to Kev. He looked at the numbers, opened a map on his laptop, and typed them in. A red dot appeared on the coast of Maine, at the tip of a spit of land jutting into the sea like a finger.

"Two hundred miles," Kev said. "Five hours' drive."

Elias looked at the red dot, then at Lena. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the map, and her left hand had found the chain under her collar and closed tight around the ring.

He saw all of it, and he didn't say anything.

Viper dropped another ladle of chili into his bowl. "Eat. It's a five-hour drive. You're going to need it."
