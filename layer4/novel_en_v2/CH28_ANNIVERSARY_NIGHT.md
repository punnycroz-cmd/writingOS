# CHAPTER 28: ANNIVERSARY NIGHT

Nobody ever tells you that getting ready for a fight is exactly like getting ready for emergency surgery, Lena thought. There's no time to be afraid. There's only a checklist, and your hands run down it faster than your mind can.

Four folding chairs in a row along the west wall of the control room, as close to the server cabinet as they could get. Four sensor rings. Four fiber-optic cables running from each head to the link box Kev had built, and from there down to the basement, out to sea, to the steel box lying four hundred and sixty feet down. Four IV bags of Alpha-9 hanging from four coat hangers bent into IV poles. Lena had diluted her last vial into four. Kev said it wasn't enough. She said it was all she had.

Her console sat in the middle of the room, facing the four chairs. A fifth sensor ring connected directly to Elias's head through a separate red cable. The anchor line.

"Eighteen minutes," Kev said from the first chair. He already had his ring on and was typing the last commands into the laptop on his knees. "The first vehicle just passed the iron gate at the head of the dirt road."

"What about the drones?" Viper asked.

"Three of them, circling offshore about a mile and a half out. Not coming in yet."

Viper stood at the foot of the iron staircase to the basement with his hands on his hips, looking around the room as if figuring out where a stove might fit. Then he went to the corner, where there was a steel cabinet chest-high, and opened it.

Inside was the security gear he'd left behind from when the team used this station as a tournament server, three years ago. Lena had seen it the first day: a rifle-style stun gun, the kind security guards carry at sporting events, two boxes of electric rounds, a bullhorn, a portable floodlight kit, and a coil of steel chain as thick as a wrist.

He took out the stun gun, checked the battery, loaded a magazine, and held it out to Lena. "You know how to use one of these?"

Lena looked at the gun. "I shot fourteen robots in Linh's practice range."

"These aren't robots."

"I know."

Viper looked at her a second longer, then put the gun in her hands.

"The spiral stairs down from the top of the lighthouse only come into this room one way," he said. "I'm chaining the steel door up front and locking the door to the lighthouse. If they want in, they come through the front. You stand behind the console and aim at the door." He paused. "Don't shoot us."

"You'll be in a dream."

"Then don't shoot our chairs."

He went to the steel front door, wrapped the chain around the handle and the frame twice, locked it with a padlock the size of a fist, then went back to the third chair, sat down, and put on his sensor ring.

"I've never been in a real fight," he said to no one. "Out here, I mean. Thirty-eight years, never punched anybody."

"You don't have to punch anybody," Linh said. She was in the second chair, between Kev and Viper, her feet not reaching the floor, the crooked paper crane perched on Kev's knee. "In there you've got an anti-tank railgun bigger than you are."

"Okay. That helps."

Elias sat in the last chair, the one closest to Lena's console. He hadn't said a word since they ran down from the lighthouse. He had quietly put on his ring, plugged in his cable, checked his IV bag, and now he was just sitting there, hands on his thighs, looking at the floor.

Lena went over and knelt in front of his chair. She swabbed the inside of his elbow, slid the IV needle into the vein, and taped it in place. Her hands were steady. When she had something to do, her hands were always steady.

"I promised," he said, very quietly.

"I know."

"I won't open it."

"I know, Eli."

She plugged the red cable into his sensor ring with a small click. On the console screen a second waveform appeared, running alongside his. Hers. The two lines were slightly out of step at the peaks, like two people walking side by side who hadn't yet fallen into the same stride.

She put both her hands over his. On the screen the two waveforms drew closer, the gap narrowing, narrowing, and then they locked.

Kev looked at the screen from the first chair and nodded. "Ninety-four percent sync. That'll do."

Lena didn't let go right away. She held on a few seconds longer, then tapped the back of his hand. Three quick, two slow. He turned his hand over and tapped her palm. Three quick, two slow.

She stood up and went back to the console.

---

"Twelve minutes," Kev said. "I'll trigger it from this chair. Counting down from five."

Lena set the stun gun on the console right next to the keyboard, barrel aimed at the steel door. She put on her own sensor ring, not a diving one but a shallow one, for sync only. She would still be here, still see the room, hear the waves, feel the concrete under her feet. But in some corner of her head, she would also be able to feel him.

"Five."

Viper closed his eyes. His lips moved. Lena couldn't hear it, but she guessed it was his daughter's name.

"Four."

Linh took Kev's hand. Kev let her.

"Three."

Elias turned his head and looked at Lena. She looked back.

"Two."

He didn't say anything. Neither did she. There was nothing left to say.

"One."

Four bodies in four chairs went slack at the same moment, like puppets with their strings cut.

On the screen, four waveforms went from green to orange. Layer 3, a hundred times faster than real time. Every minute out here was more than an hour and a half in there. And in a corner of her head, Lena felt him fall, like missing a step in the dark. Then another feeling: cold, bright, vast, a city of black glass opening up beneath his feet.

She held on tight to the edge of the console.

---

The control room was dead quiet. There was only the hum of the server cabinet, the ticking of the clock Kev had stuck on the wall, and the breathing of four sleeping people.

Lena watched the four waveforms. Elias stable. Kev stable. Linh a little fast but within range. Viper slow and even, like a man taking an afternoon nap.

She looked at the clock. 10:02. Only two minutes out here, and in there it had already been three hours and twenty minutes.

Then she heard engines. Deep, heavy, a lot of them, coming through three feet of concrete wall. Tires grinding on gravel, doors opening and closing, boots.

She stood up and picked up the stun gun.

The secondary monitor in the corner of the console showed the feed from the old security camera Kev had fixed and mounted on the roof of the concrete building, black and white and blurry but clear enough. Four black trucks parked in a half-circle on the open ground in front of the lighthouse, headlights blazing straight at the door of the concrete building. Figures pouring out of the backs of the trucks: ballistic helmets, night-vision goggles, submachine guns. Lena counted. Ten, twelve, sixteen.

In the sky, the three black dots had left the water and were dropping lower, circling the top of the lighthouse.

One figure separated from the soldiers and walked to the front of the lead truck. He stood in the glare of the headlights with no helmet and no gun: a middle-aged man with graying hair in a gray wool overcoat.

Lena recognized him immediately. Dr. Bradley. The young technician in Elias's memory who had slipped a little boy a mint. The tired doctor in Elias's first memory at the hospital. Twenty years between those two moments.

Bradley held a bullhorn. His voice came through the concrete, distorted but clear.

"Dr. Hale. Or should I say Dr. Solis." A pause. "We know you're in there. We know Mr. Vane is with you. Nobody wants this to end badly. You open the door, and I promise no one gets hurt."

Lena didn't answer. She stood behind the console with the gun in her hands, watching the screen.

Her phone buzzed on the console. Unknown number. An ordinary call, not through the crow app, to the prepaid phone only four people had the number for. She knew who it was before she picked up.

"Maren," Iris Halloway said, smooth and gracious, as if calling to invite her to dinner. "I'm sorry to call so late. It's a special day. I'm sure you understand."

Lena said nothing.

"Twenty years," Halloway said. "Your mother and I were friends, did you know that? Truly. We used to sit in the institute cafeteria until two in the morning arguing philosophy. Elena believed memory was sacred. I believed memory was a tool like everything else. We never agreed." A pause. "And then she burned ten years of my research and died in the fire, for a principle. And left you behind."

"My mother left me behind," Lena said, "because she saved me from you."

"She cost you twenty years. Not knowing who you were. Not knowing who your mother was. You call that saving you?" Halloway's voice didn't rise and didn't drop. "I can give you all of it back, Maren. Every memory, every moment with your mother. With Somnia 5.0, it's as easy as flipping a switch. No diving. No pain. All you have to do is open the door."

Lena looked at the screen, at the four orange waveforms, at Elias's waveform running alongside her own fainter one.

"Your mother made the wrong choice," Halloway said. "Don't repeat her mistake."

Lena brought the phone close to her mouth.

"My mother sang," she said. "At the end. Did you know that? When your people came, my mother stood across that corridor and sang." She stopped. "That isn't in your files, is it, Iris? You don't have that memory. I do."

She hung up.

Outside, Bradley's voice on the bullhorn went silent. Then another voice, harder, gave an order without a bullhorn.

The first explosion made the steel door shudder on its hinges.
