# CHAPTER 22: COMING BACK

It was three hours and ten minutes from Bayside to the river town where Viper was hiding. Kev did it in two hours and forty.

Lena remembered nothing about the drive. She remembered Ruth's banana bread falling onto the floor of the truck and not picking it up. She remembered Linh in the back seat on the phone with Viper the whole way, her voice eerily calm, like a 911 dispatcher: *Take his pulse at the neck, two fingers, right under the jaw, count fifteen seconds and multiply by four.* She remembered Kev passing a semi on a curve on a two-lane road, and nobody in the truck saying a word. She remembered thinking, very clearly and very coldly: *If he dies, the last thing I said to him was "stop saying that."*

---

The warehouse was dark and cold, the tin roof full of holes, the columns of late sun gone orange-red. The taco truck was parked in a corner with its back doors flung open.

Viper sat on the concrete floor with his back against a wheel and Elias's head in his lap. He was pressing a wad of bloody paper towels to Elias's face, his big hand shaking. When he heard the door, he looked up, and Lena saw that his eyes were red and wet.

"I gave him two shots," Viper said. "I didn't know which was which, so I gave him both."

Lena knelt.

She didn't look at Elias's face first. She looked at what she'd been trained to look at. Unequal pupils, the left more dilated than the right. Carotid pulse fast and thready, a hundred forty-two. Shallow breathing, with abnormally long pauses. Hands and feet ice-cold, temples burning hot. Blood running from both nostrils, and just as Viper had said, from the corner of the left eye too, a line as fine as a pencil stroke.

Every sign was a number, a threshold, a conclusion she had read in the literature. She put them together in her head, as fast and automatically as she fit the peaks on a chart, and the conclusion was not good.

"He's stuck somewhere between Layer 2 and Layer 4," she said, her voice even. "He can't come up, and he hasn't gone all the way down. His brain is burning itself to keep a door from opening."

"Can you pull him up?" Viper asked.

"Not from outside." She turned. "Kev, the dual-link unit in the truck. The one you built for the reconditioning. Bring it in."

Kev was moving before she finished.

"What's a dual link?" Viper asked.

"I'm going in there with him." Lena opened the case, took out two sensor rings, and checked the batteries. "Our brains were linked when we were kids. Kev thinks they still remember how. I'm going to go down there, find him, and pull him up."

"Is it dangerous?"

She didn't answer. Linh was standing behind Viper with her arms wrapped tight around her elbows, watching her, and understood what the silence meant.

"Lena," Linh said.

"If my heart rate goes over a hundred fifty," Lena said, setting one ring on Elias's head and then the other on her own, "unplug me first. Not him. Me. Understand?"

"No," Linh said.

"Linh."

"I understand. I don't agree." The girl knelt down beside Viper and took one of Elias's cold hands. "I'm not unplugging anybody. You two have to climb out on your own."

Kev came back with a metal box the size of a shoebox, a fiber-optic cable running from each end. He knelt, plugged one cable into each of their sensor rings, and typed a few commands into the laptop sitting on the lid.

"Searching for Theta sync," he said. "Thirty percent. Fifty. Lena, your heart's going too fast. Slow your breathing."

She slowed it. In for four, hold for two, out for six, the way she had taught a thousand other people.

She leaned down and pressed her forehead to Elias's. Titanium touched titanium with a small click.

"Idiot," she said, very quietly, just loud enough for him to hear if he could still hear anything. "The rules were very clear about not doing this alone."

"Eighty percent," Kev said. "Ninety. Locked."

---

She fell.

This time was nothing like the dives before. No warm water, no memory fish. She fell straight through something thick and hot, like falling through asphalt in midsummer, and landed on a curved floor.

A spherical room, pitch dark, with something smoldering in the middle.

Elias was kneeling there, both hands still wrapped around it. From what was left, she guessed it had once been a knot of light the size of a fist. Now it was a tangle of half-burned threads, glowing red where they'd snapped, still beating, but wrong, fast and then slow, stopping completely and then jolting back. Every time it jolted, a red wave burst out and hit the wall of the room.

The wall had cracked wide open, one long split from the floor to the top of the dome, as wide as a hand. Through the crack came a light Lena couldn't find a word for. It was closer to a feeling than a color: the feeling of too much, like standing at a door that opened into a room holding every voice of every human being who had ever lived.

It was pulling. She could feel it even from where she stood, a gentle, smooth, almost pleasant pull.

Elias didn't turn around. His hands were burned black up to the elbows.

"Eli."

He flinched and turned his head, slowly, like an old man. In here he looked younger, thinner, more like the boy in the file photo than the twenty-seven-year-old man.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"You're the one who shouldn't be here."

"I've almost got it. It's almost quiet." He looked down at the burned threads in his hands. "Just a little more."

"You're killing it by killing yourself." She walked toward him, and every step brought her closer to the crack and made the pull stronger. "You don't have to destroy it. Kev got it wrong. You don't break it. You teach it again."

"Teach it with what?"

She knelt in front of him, on the other side of the knot of light, and put both her hands over his. His hands were as hot as an oven, but she didn't pull away.

"With the thing it knew before they ever taught it anything," she said.

Then she began to tap on the knot of light itself, with her fingertips, lightly and evenly. Three quick, two slow.

The knot jerked. Elias hissed with pain. Three quick, two slow.

"What are you doing—"

"Tap."

He looked at her, and then he understood. His blackened fingers, next to hers, began to tap along. Three quick, two slow. Three quick, two slow.

The knot stopped jerking. For a moment it went still, like an animal that has just heard an unfamiliar call. Then the remaining threads began to beat along with them, to their rhythm, nothing to do with Halloway's forty-minute rhythm anymore. The rhythm of two small hands on a wall, every night, in a building without calendars.

The red wave pulsed out one last time, weakly, touched the wall, and couldn't get through. It broke apart like breath on cold glass.

The knot of light changed color. Not red anymore. A pale, warm blue, like the light of Rosie's moon lamp.

From far away, Kev's voice came down, distorted and tiny: "...signal's... gone... no identifier anymore... Lena, it's off..."

Elias looked at the blue light held between their hands, then at her.

"It's off," he said.

"It's not off," Lena said. "It's still talking. It just isn't talking to them anymore."

---

The crack in the wall was still there.

They stood up together, still holding hands, because neither of them was sure what would happen if they let go. The pull from the other side had weakened, but it hadn't gone.

"We have to go up," she said.

"Yeah."

They turned their backs on the crack. And the light on the other side of it changed.

Lena felt it before she saw it, with her nose. Smoke. Real smoke, burning plastic, burning paper, and something sweetish she knew, without knowing how she knew, was the smell of an incendiary catalyst. Then a siren, very far away, wailing and stopping, wailing and stopping.

Elias's hand tightened in hers. "Can you smell that?"

"Yes."

She didn't turn around. She knew that if she turned around, she would see it. For twenty years she hadn't known that night existed, and for the last ten days, in every dive, she had steered around it the way you avoid looking at a wound that hasn't been dressed.

But Elias had turned. She knew because his hand pulled her around with him.

Through the crack, the light wasn't nameless anymore. It was the orange of fire thrown against concrete walls. A corridor, red alarm lights spinning, a steel door, and two children in white cotton clothes, one printed with a zero and one with a one, running hand in hand toward a vent grate someone had already taken off.

His memory. Her memory. For the first time, not two halves anymore. One.

They didn't choose to step in. It pulled them both in at once.
