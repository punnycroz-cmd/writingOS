# CHAPTER 27: THE NIGHT BEFORE

October fourteenth went by as slowly as a Sunday.

Nobody said so, but all five of them acted as if it were an ordinary day. Viper got up early to fish. Linh slept in until ten. Kev ran the system check for the fourteenth time, then the fifteenth, then went out to the rocks and sat beside Viper holding a fishing rod he never bothered to cast. Lena washed clothes by hand in a plastic bucket and hung them on a line strung between the lighthouse and the concrete building, where the sea wind snapped them like flags.

Elias climbed to the top of the lighthouse and drew.

He drew all five of them. Viper with his fishing rod and his backward cap. Kev beside him, holding his rod like an unloaded rifle. Linh asleep on a folding chair outside the concrete building, a book face down on her face. Lena standing among the hanging laundry, a clothespin in one hand, looking out to sea.

He didn't draw himself. He had never known how.

---

That afternoon Diego called the satellite phone again, and this time Rosie got to talk for a full ten minutes.

Viper sat on the steps of the concrete building with the phone to his ear. Everyone pretended not to listen, but everyone heard all of it, because Rosie talked loud and Viper had put it on speaker at some point without noticing.

"...and then Uncle Diego said Gerald the moose has a wife, her name's Brenda, but Brenda doesn't like people, so Brenda hides behind the hill..."

"Brenda's a girl moose, sweetheart?"

"*Dad!* Obviously! A boy moose can't marry a boy moose!"

In the control room, Linh sprayed out a mouthful of tea.

"...Dad, when are you coming to get me?"

Viper was quiet for a beat. Just one beat, but Elias, standing in the doorway, saw him close his eyes.

"Soon, baby. I promise."

"You really promise?"

"I really promise."

"Then tell the fish doctor Sir Floatsalot died."

Viper opened his eyes. "What?"

"Sir Floatsalot. Before I left, he was swimming upside down. Grandma says when fish swim upside down it means they're dead. But I think he's just sleeping. Can you ask the doctor for me?"

Viper turned to look at Lena. She was standing among the hanging laundry with a clothespin in her hand, looking back at him. She nodded seriously, then came over, sat down on the step next to Viper, and leaned close to the phone.

"Rosie, it's Miss Lena."

"The fish doctor!"

"I think Sir Floatsalot lived a very long, very happy life, and got to eat a whole lot of cookies. Sometimes fish swim upside down because they've done everything they ever wanted to do."

The line was quiet for a moment.

"Was he sad?"

"No. I don't think so. I think he was just tired, and he wanted to rest."

"Okay." Rosie sniffled a little. "Then when I come home I'm getting a new fish and naming him Sir Floatsalot the Second. Will you be his doctor?"

"I promise."

"You really promise?"

Lena hesitated. Elias saw her hesitate.

"I really promise," she said.

---

At five, Linh brought out a pack of colored paper. She'd bought it at a gas station near Portland while they were camping, along with a box of markers and a bag of gummy bears, "for entertainment." She sat down on the step by Kev's feet and started folding.

Kev looked at her. Linh didn't look back. She folded with intense concentration, the tip of her tongue poking out the corner of her mouth, her small fingers creasing the paper one fold at a time.

Five minutes later she set a gray paper crane in Kev's palm. It looked nothing like his Echo crane. One wing was crooked, the neck was a little bent, and she had folded one crease the wrong way at the tail so it stuck up like a rooster's. But the gray was right.

"I saw you turn it off," Linh said, eyes on the next sheet of paper. "Your crane. Yesterday. I saw you pull the core."

Kev didn't say anything. He looked at the paper crane in his hand.

"Seven years, right?" Linh asked. "Since you got out of that program. I read your connection logs. It was activated seven years ago, the exact day you got home." She made another fold. "I figure it was the first thing that talked to you, back when you couldn't talk to anybody."

Kev was still silent, but his fingers closed around the paper crane, very gently, as if afraid of crushing it.

"This one can't hear anything," Linh said. "Can't report anything to anybody. It's just paper." She stopped and, for the first time, looked up at him. "But I folded it. So it can't be that bad."

Kev looked at her for a long time.

"The wing's crooked," he said.

"I know."

"The tail sticks up."

"I *know*, Kev."

"I like it."

Linh turned back to her paper so fast she tore a corner off it. Her ears had gone bright red.

Elias, standing in the doorway of the concrete building, went back inside before anyone could see him smile.

---

At eight that night, after a final pot of fish soup that Viper swore on everything was the best he had ever made, Lena disappeared.

Elias found her at the top of the lighthouse. A hundred and twelve steps. He didn't count them, but Linh had counted them for him already. At the top he was out of breath and a little dizzy. Since the warehouse, his head still got dizzy like that.

The round lamp room had storm-glass windows gone yellow with age, looking out on the night sea in every direction, the wind whistling through the gaps. In the middle of the room, where the huge lens had once sat, there was only an empty stone pedestal now, and on the pedestal was a candle.

Lena sat cross-legged in front of the pedestal with her back to the stairs. The candle was one of those fat white emergency candles, the kind you use in a power outage. She'd probably bought it at a gas station. The flame stood perfectly still despite the wind outside, because the room was sealed.

Elias stopped on the top step.

A flame. A candle in a dark room. Something twinged under his breastbone, horribly familiar. *The curtains caught. I stood in the corner and couldn't make a sound.*

He waited for the fear to hit him. According to the notebook it had hit him every night for twenty years. It had made him sign a contract that traded away his whole life.

It didn't come. There was only something left over, like the hum of a bell after it has stopped ringing, and then that faded too. There was just a white candle burning steadily. Nothing else.

"Twenty years," Lena said without turning around. "Today."

"I know."

"I never knew the anniversary of my mother's death." She looked at the candle. "For twenty years I lived October fourteenth like any other day. Went to work. Got dinner with Nia. Last year on this exact day Corwin and I went to see a terrible movie about aliens. I laughed my head off."

Elias sat down next to her. Not close, not far.

"I don't know what people do on a death anniversary," she said. "Margaret wasn't religious. The Dalys were, but I never went to church. All I know is people light candles." She laughed softly. "So I went and bought a candle. And after I bought it I realized I was lighting a candle in a lighthouse, right in front of someone who'd had a fear of candles planted in him for twenty years."

"I'm not scared," he said.

She turned to look at him.

"Really," he said. "At first I thought I would be. But I'm not."

She looked at him a moment longer, as if reading his face, then nodded and turned back to the candle.

They sat in silence for a while. Outside, the red light of the buoy kept blinking out at sea. On three seconds, off two.

Then Lena began to sing.

Very softly. Her voice was higher and thinner than her mother's low, rough one, and a little shaky on the low notes. On the third line she got the words wrong, stopped, and started the line over.

*Nana, niña, nana. La nieve cae en el tejado...*

Elias closed his eyes. He didn't know the words. He never had. He only knew the melody, because it had survived in him somewhere deeper than his own name. But while she sang, he heard things he hadn't been able to hear in the dives: the wind whistling through the gaps in the glass, exactly like the wind through the vent in the ceiling of Room Zero; the rustle when she shifted her weight, exactly like a child turning over on the other side of a wall.

His fingers, resting on the stone floor beside his knee, began to tap. Three quick, two slow. She didn't stop singing, but her fingers on the floor beside his tapped back.

Two hands a few inches apart on a cold stone floor, not touching. They didn't need to touch.

She sang the whole song through and went quiet. The candle kept burning steadily.

"My mom sang it better," she said.

"I don't remember," he said. "But I don't think so."

She turned to look at him. In the candlelight her eyes were wet, but she was smiling.

"You're a terrible liar," she said.

"This time I'm not lying."

She didn't answer. She turned back to the candle, and he saw her hand go to the chain under her collar again and close around the ring, the way it always did. Then she let go and laid that hand back on the stone floor next to his. A few inches apart. Not touching.

He didn't move closer. He didn't have the right. Not yet, and maybe not ever. But he didn't move away, either.

---

At 9:50, the radio on his belt crackled.

"Elias." Kev's voice, as even as ever but a little faster. "Get down here. Both of you."

Lena was on her feet before Kev finished.

"What's going on?" Elias said into the radio.

"Coastal radar. The one I hooked back up from the old system." A crackle. "Four vehicles on the highway turning onto the dirt road out to the point. Trucks. Armored."

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less." A pause. "And there's something in the air. Three dots, flying low, coming in from the sea."

Elias looked at Lena. Lena looked at him. Ten minutes to ten, and the plan had been 11:45. They'd lost almost two hours. Nyx had been right. That was the first hole.

Lena bent down and blew out the candle. A thin thread of white smoke rose, twisted, and dissolved into the dark of the lamp room.

They ran down the hundred and twelve stairs in the dark, hands on the cold stone wall, and nobody counted.
