# CHAPTER 35: DAYBREAK

Later, people asked Maren about that night many times, in an FBI interview room, in Ruth's living room, in front of a congressional committee with a microphone that kept sagging down, and every time she could only remember things that didn't matter.

She remembered the helicopter rotors blowing the ash from the burned trucks up into the air, black ash rising like snow falling the wrong way. She remembered a medic from the rapid response team, a young woman with freckles and red hair in a ponytail, kneeling beside her on the concrete floor and asking *are you a doctor*, and her answering *yes*, and the woman handing her the defibrillator without another question. She remembered the woman's green latex gloves with Elias's blood on them. She remembered the machine giving one *beep* before each shock. Three times.

She remembered counting. Thirty compressions, two breaths, thirty compressions. She remembered his breastbone under the heel of her hand, harder and thinner than she'd expected. She remembered thinking, very clearly, between the seventeenth compression and the eighteenth: *It's been so long since I did this on a real person.* For years she had only read charts.

She remembered the glowing crow on his shoulder. It didn't fly away. Through all three shocks it stayed there, the faint projection flickering every time the current ran through him, but it didn't fly away. The red-haired medic glanced at it once, frowned, and didn't look at it again.

After the third shock, the flat line on the monitor jumped.

One beat, then another. Slow, very slow, forty-one beats a minute. But there.

She sat down on the floor. She remembered sitting there with her hands on her thighs and her gloves covered in blood, unable to get up. The red-haired medic put a hand on her shoulder, gently, and went off to do something else.

---

Kev and Linh woke up before the helicopter arrived.

Maren didn't know exactly when. She was doing compressions on Elias when she heard someone coughing and choking in the second chair. Linh sat bolt upright, ripped the sensor ring off her head, and threw up on the floor. In the first chair, Kev was already on his feet, swaying like a drunk. He came over, knelt beside Linh, and held her hair back.

Nobody said anything. Linh finished throwing up, wiped her mouth on her sleeve, looked up, saw Maren doing chest compressions on Elias, and burst into tears.

Kev looked at Maren across the smoke-filled room. He didn't ask anything. He just got up, went to the console, and looked at the broadcast screen.

> `[PROGRESS: 100%]`
> `[RECEIVED: 419/419 ADDRESSES]`

He stood there reading that line with the crooked paper crane Linh had folded for him the evening before still in his hand, and his shoulders shook. Maren had never seen Kev cry. She wasn't sure he was crying. She didn't look closely. She was still counting.

---

Iris Halloway was found at 11:31 that night on the floor of her office on the forty-second floor of the Halloway Neuro-Systems tower, unconscious, her pulse weak, her pupils unequal. A night-shift security guard on his rounds had heard a chair fall and knocked, and knocked, and gotten no answer, and finally called 911.

By the time she was loaded into the ambulance, the first files had already appeared on the screens of the overnight editors at Reuters and AP. By seven in the morning they were everywhere.

The 2004 fire report. The 2010 memo about implanting false memories in "Subject Zero." The recordings Thomas Daly had made in his own bedroom in 2012, along with his handwritten analysis of the data packets his Echo sent to Halloway's private server. The operating log from the Echo in his car twelve days later, showing a remote command sent six seconds before the car went into the river. The technical documentation for Somnia 5.0. The diagram of the paired-subject configuration.

There was also a three-minute video, which Nyx released at five in the morning with exactly one line of text: *Her name was Elena Solis.*

It was footage from Prometheus security cameras on the night of October 14, 2004, the footage Thomas Daly had kept and Nyx had hidden for twenty years. A corridor full of smoke. A little boy sitting in front of a ventilation shaft. And a woman with a burned arm standing with her back to him, arms spread wide between the boy and the flashlights coming closer.

The video had no sound. But if you looked closely, you could see the woman's lips moving in a steady rhythm, as if she were singing.

Somnia 5.0 did not launch at midnight. At 11:58, two minutes before the automatic update, the Department of Justice issued an emergency suspension order, after Nyx passed the first few dozen pages of the technical documentation to an old acquaintance there. Maren didn't learn about that until three days later.

---

Dawn came at 6:12 a.m. on October fifteenth.

Maren watched it from the window at the end of the hallway in the neuro ICU at Maine Medical Center in Portland. From up there you could see all of Casco Bay. The water went from black to gray to a thin silver, and the sun came up in the east, red, round, not blinding, like an apple.

The crow had disappeared when they carried him onto the helicopter. She thought maybe the phone's battery had died. Maybe not.

Somewhere to the east, under the fog on the water she couldn't see, eighty nautical miles out, there was an island.

She looked toward it for a long while, then turned and went back in.

---

At 6:30 that morning, Nia Okafor appeared on a morning news show wearing the mustard-yellow blazer Maren always said made her look like a lottery host, and said, calmly and clearly, that she had worked with Dr. Lena Hale for eight years. That the "kidnapping" video broadcast back in early September was fake, and she could prove it, starting with the woman's shadow falling ten degrees off. That she had a copy of all the clinic's data from before it was burned, because she had backed it up following the weekly procedure Dr. Hale made her do, which she was too lazy to do every single week, except that week, for some reason, she had.

Then she looked straight into the camera and said that if her friend was watching, she still owed her a bowl of phở at the place on Fourth, which was open till ten, and this time she'd better not say no.

Maren didn't see it. Linh watched it on her phone and played her just the last line, and Maren laughed so hard a nurse had to come out and shush her.

---

Viper woke at nine in the morning, in the next room. He didn't remember anything after the red sky went away. He remembered his own name, his daughter's name, and his chili recipe. The doctor said that was a good sign.

Rosie arrived at two in the afternoon with Diego and Rosa, after six hours in the car down from the mountains in Vermont. She ran down the hospital corridor, her yellow boots thumping on the linoleum, and launched herself onto her father's bed before anyone could stop her. Viper wrapped her up in his arms, crying and laughing at the same time, loud, the way of a man who had never in his life managed to do anything quietly.

"Did you win, Dad?" Rosie asked, her face pressed into his chest.

Viper looked up, over his daughter's head, toward the door, where Maren and Linh were standing. He didn't remember. Maren could see it plainly on his face, the look of a man who has woken up after a battle without knowing which side he was on.

"He won," Linh said. Her voice was still hoarse, but it didn't shake at all. "I was there. I saw the whole thing. Your dad stood there by himself and held off a whole wall so we could get through." She sniffed. "And he told me himself to tell you that."

Viper looked at Linh, at her red nose, her swollen eyes, her chin stuck out as if daring anybody to argue. Then he nodded, and held Rosie tighter.

---

Kev and Linh stayed at the hospital, sleeping on the plastic chairs in the waiting room, taking turns going down to the cafeteria for coffee. They weren't allowed in the ICU, but they stayed anyway. On the second night, Maren saw Linh asleep against Kev's shoulder, and Kev sitting perfectly still, staring straight ahead, one hand resting lightly on her knee as if afraid she'd slide off. The crooked paper crane was in his shirt pocket with only its bent head sticking out. Maren didn't tell anyone.

Ruth came on the morning of the second day with a thermos of chicken soup, a bag of clean clothes, and Maren's gray sweater, which she had left behind in Bayside. Ruth hugged her in the middle of the hallway, hugged her so long her glasses went crooked, and Maren said into her shoulder: Ruth, I remember. I remember all of it.

"I know," Ruth said. "I saw the news." She let go and straightened her glasses and looked at her the way you look at a child who's just come home from far away. "Eat first, Lena. Then tell me whatever you want."

Maren let the name be. She realized it didn't bother her at all.

Dr. Bradley had stayed at the lighthouse, sitting on the threshold, until the FBI came, and told them everything. Forty-eight hours later his lawyer called Maren to ask whether she'd be willing to testify that he had cooperated. She said yes. She wasn't sure why. Maybe because of a mint.

---

In the twenty-three terabytes that had gone out, there was one document Maren read over and over for three nights, sitting on a plastic chair in the waiting room with a tablet borrowed from Kev on her lap.

*Mark-IV Neural Recovery Pod. For subjects following Layer 4 exposure. Installation site: Island Ecological Station.*

She read the specifications until she knew them by heart. She read the margin notes, the numbered trials, the recovery curves that a year ago she would have sat analyzing with Nia until midnight. She also read the small line at the bottom of page fourteen: *Section C, Pod 02: in use (since 02/2024).*

February 2024. The month Corwin went out to sea.

On the morning of the third day, she told the FBI agent in charge of the case about the Island Station, about the notebook at the lighthouse, about the white pod. The agent was a tired middle-aged woman named Petrakis, who listened without interrupting once, then flipped open her notebook and tapped her pen against a page.

"We know about it," she said. "It's registered to an ecological research foundation out of Nova Scotia. It sits just on the other side of the Hague Line, which puts it in Canadian waters. We've sent a request for cooperation." She paused. "These things usually take a few weeks, Doctor. Sometimes longer."

Maren thanked her, then went out into the hallway and stood at the window looking out over the bay, and stood there until the sun was high over the water.

---

That afternoon, the head of the neuro ICU called her into his office.

He was a kind, tired man of about sixty, and he told her things she already knew. Widespread microvascular damage in both temporal lobes. Cerebral edema under control but not yet reduced. The patient was comatose, Glasgow Coma Scale six. There was no precedent for this kind of injury, because no one had ever been into Layer 4 twice.

"We can keep him alive," he said. "But I have to be honest with you, Doctor. We don't know how to fix it. Nobody does."

Maren nodded. She thought about page fourteen, about a foundation in Nova Scotia, about the words *a few weeks*. She didn't say any of it.

"He has no family," the doctor went on, sliding a sheet of paper across the desk. "The court will appoint a guardian, but that takes time. Meanwhile, the hospital needs an emergency contact. Someone we can call if something happens."

It was an ordinary form, the kind she had filled out a hundred times for other people. A box for a name, a box for a phone number, a box for *Relationship to patient*. The doctor handed her a pen.

For twenty years she had signed her name thousands of times, on forensic reports, on apartment leases, on the birthday card for Margaret that was the same every year. Lena Hale. A slanted L, an H with a long crossbar. Her hand knew it so well she never had to think.

She put the pen to the paper, then stopped for a moment.

*Maren Solis.*

The handwriting wavered a little on the M. Unpracticed. Like someone writing her own name for the very first time.

At the box for *Relationship to patient*, she stopped longer. She thought of the words people usually put in that box, wife, husband, son, daughter, sister, brother, partner, and none of them were right. Then she wrote:

*Friend since childhood.*

She looked at the two lines a moment longer before she slid the form back to the doctor.

---

That evening she was allowed into his room for ten minutes.

The ICU room was dark, lit only by the blue glow of the monitors and filled with the steady sound of the ventilator. In, out. In, out. Elias lay in the bed with a breathing tube in his mouth, his head wrapped in white bandages, a hospital sensor band around his forehead. He looked smaller. He looked like the boy in the file photo. Maren pulled the chair close to the bed and sat down.

She didn't say anything. For eight months she had talked to Corwin on the nights she couldn't sleep, talking into the dark as if he could hear, and she knew it didn't help. It was for the person talking, not the person listening.

She put her hand over his. His hand was cold. The two stitches she'd put in under the lights of a laundromat had healed into two thin scars. She tapped on the back of his hand.

*Three quick. Two slow.*

*I'm here.*

The ventilator went on. In, out, in, out. Nothing.

She tapped again. Three quick, two slow. Still nothing.

She didn't tap a third time. She just sat there with her hand on his, watching the blue waveform run steadily across the monitor, the small peaks, the quiet stretches.

Five minutes. Seven.

The nurse knocked softly on the glass. Time was up.

Maren stood and drew her hand away. And his index finger moved.

Not much. Just a little, the small twitch of a sleeping person's finger. Once. Once more, quick. Then again. A pause. Then twice, slow.

Maren stood frozen by the bed, her hand still hanging in midair, staring at his finger.

The monitor registered nothing unusual. The nurse outside the door didn't see anything. It could have been a reflex. It could have been a random muscle twitch, the kind of thing she had seen a hundred times on the charts of coma patients and written up in her reports as *no clinical significance*.

She was an examiner. She knew the difference between memory and testimony. She knew that people can sincerely believe in something that isn't real.

She bent down, laid her hand back over his, and stayed like that until the nurse had to come all the way in and pull her out.
