# CHAPTER 2: A STRANGER'S APARTMENT

118 Harlow Street was a six-story brick building wedged between a laundromat and a phone repair shop. Its front was stained black with soot, and a rusted iron fire escape zigzagged down one side like a clumsy row of stitches. Elias stood in the rain and looked at it for a while, waiting for some small spark of familiarity, even just the way light fell from a third-floor window or the faint smell of soap drifting from the laundromat. But the building looked at him the way you look at a stranger, and he looked back at it the same way.

The front door wasn't locked. On mailbox 6C, someone had stuck a strip of tape and written *E. VANE* in marker, the letters straight and leaning a little to the right. He looked at them and realized with a jolt that the handwriting was his. His memory didn't know it, but his hand did. It kept wanting to trace the shapes.

The elevator was out, so he climbed all six floors. By the fourth his thighs were trembling. On the fifth he had to stop and lean on the railing to catch his breath, cold sweat breaking out on the back of his neck. Eleven days of lying still had eaten away at his body more than he'd realized.

The key was under the doormat. Of course it was, he thought. A man who lived alone and never had visitors would have no reason to think anyone wanted to get in.

He opened the door.

---

The apartment was so empty his footsteps echoed. Old wooden floor, a few boards lifting. A single bed pushed against the wall, the blanket folded into a square as tight as a barracks bunk. A small dining table with exactly one chair. The kitchen was clean enough that nothing suggested anyone had ever cooked in it: an electric kettle, a jar of instant coffee, and a stack of instant noodle cups lined up in a cupboard.

No photos, no pictures on the wall, not a single plant or book. It was as though whoever lived here had made a point of leaving no trace of himself.

Only one corner was different. By the window stood a heavy industrial desk that took up nearly half the wall, and on it sat a rig assembled with a care that was close to love: three curved monitors arranged in an arc, a transparent tower with liquid-cooling tubes looping through it, a mechanical keyboard whose keys had worn shiny in exactly the places a hand would rest. Beside it, on its own stand, sat a neural link headset with a slim titanium ring, the padding rubbed thin from use.

Elias rested his hand on the back of the chair. The leather had sunk into the shape of a body, his body. This was where he actually lived, he thought. Not in the apartment. In the chair.

He didn't sit down yet. He searched the apartment first, methodically, left to right, top to bottom. The closet held five dark T-shirts, two pairs of jeans, and a windbreaker, all so alike they might have been bought in one trip just to get it over with. The desk drawer held cables, screws, a screwdriver, and a half-empty box of painkillers. In the bathroom cabinet were three bottles of prescription sleeping pills, the prescriber printed on the label as *Dr. M. Pruitt, Halloway Affiliated Clinic*.

Halloway again.

On the nightstand lay a black notebook. He opened it. The first pages were dated, one day per page, the handwriting slanting to the right, shaky in places, as if written in the dark.

> *March 14. Dreamed it again. The curtains caught. I stood in the corner and couldn't make a sound. She ran in. She didn't run out.*

> *April 2. Three nights in a row. Took two instead of one. Didn't help.*

> *May 19. Dr. Pruitt says it's traumatic memory, completely normal, any orphan who causes the death of the person raising him carries it for life. He says "causes" as lightly as if he were talking about the weather.*

> *June 30. Sometimes I think if anyone could take this out of my head, I'd give them anything.*

Elias sat down on the edge of the bed. The words were his. His hand knew it. But he didn't remember the pain in them. He read them like someone else's diary, and still his chest tightened.

Between the last pages was an old printed photo, its corners curling. It showed a two-story wooden house burned down to a black frame, the brick chimney standing alone in the ashes. On the back, in pencil, in an adult's handwriting that wasn't his: *The Ashford family home. November 2003.*

A memory, or something pretending to be one. He turned the photo over and over. The house stirred nothing in him, but the guilt did, and it didn't come from the picture. It came from somewhere deeper in his body, a place that didn't need to understand in order to hurt.

He slid the photo back into the notebook, closed it, and went to the window.

The black sedan was parked at the curb across the street, lights off, engine off, rain running in streaks down a windshield nobody wiped. According to the clock on the wall it had been sitting there for seventeen minutes. He knew because he had been counting since he walked in. They didn't come up. They didn't knock. They just waited.

Elias pulled the curtain shut.

---

He found it while searching under the bed: a cardboard shoebox with the lid taped all the way around. Inside was a stack of cash, about two thousand dollars held with a rubber band; an old phone, powered off, its screen cracked across one corner; a metal medal engraved *VANGUARD LEAGUE — NORTHEAST REGIONAL CHAMPION — WINTER 2022*; and a document bound in a blue cover.

He turned to the first page.

> **AGREEMENT FOR PARTICIPATION IN ADVANCED COGNITIVE SYNCHRONIZATION TRIAL**
> **Party A:** Halloway Neuro-Systems, Special Research Division.
> **Party B:** Elias Vane.

The pages that followed were thick with clauses: neurological risks, liability waivers, absolute confidentiality. He skimmed all of it, his eyes catching only a few phrases. *Multi-layer synchronization. Beyond standard cognitive thresholds. Party B participates voluntarily and has been fully informed.* Then, on the last page, under Party B's benefits:

> *1. Compensation: USD 400,000.*
> *2. Upon completion of the trial, Party A undertakes to perform a memory restructuring procedure for the* ***permanent removal*** *of Party B's recurring traumatic memories (reference file: Ashford/2003).*

Below that was a signature slanting to the right, dated August 14, 2024.

Elias sat motionless for a long time with the document in his lap. He had signed this. He had walked in on his own. The money wasn't the reason. The cash in the shoebox made it clear he barely spent anything. The reason was the second line, the promise that they would take the nightmare out of his head.

And they had kept their word, in a way. The nightmare was gone, along with everything else.

He laughed, one short, dry sound in the empty room, so strange to his own ears that he stopped. He remembered the line in the notebook, *if anyone could take this out of my head, I'd give them anything*, and he thought, well, I did. I gave them all of it.

---

After ten minutes on the charger the old phone lit up and asked for a passcode. His finger typed six digits he didn't know he knew, and the screen unlocked.

There were two hundred and fourteen notifications, most of them from a messaging app with a black crow icon, in a group chat called *CROW'S NEST 🐦‍⬛*. He opened it and scrolled up from the bottom.

> **Viper_99:** VANE. Where are you.
> **Viper_99:** It's been 3 days you jerk
> **Cipher_Lin:** he prob fell asleep in game again
> **Cipher_Lin:** last time he slept 2 days straight, not even joking
> **Ghost_Kev:** His signal dropped off the Vanguard leaderboard at 14:00 on Aug 21.
> **Ghost_Kev:** He didn't fall asleep.
> **Viper_99:** Kev don't talk like that
> **Cipher_Lin:** ...
> **Cipher_Lin:** eli please answer
> **Viper_99:** I called the hospital. They say there's no patient by that name
> **Ghost_Kev:** Saw a rumor on a forum. Someone says he signed a trial contract with Halloway.
> **Viper_99:** he WHAT
> **Cipher_Lin:** no way. he hates halloway
> **Ghost_Kev:** Maybe not as much as he hates the nightmares.

Elias stopped on that last line. These people knew about his nightmares. Right now they knew him better than he knew himself. He scrolled down to the newest message, sent that morning.

> **Cipher_Lin:** day 11. im opening the Nest tonight same as always. if you come back just come in ok. nobody's mad at you.

Something went hot behind his eyes. He didn't know who these people were. He didn't remember their voices or their faces. But three people had been waiting for him for eleven days, and they were still waiting.

His thumb hovered over the keyboard. What was he supposed to write? That he didn't remember them? That he'd just been wiped clean? That there was a black car parked outside his building?

In the end he typed only:

> **Elias:** I'm back.

Three dots appeared almost instantly, vanished, came back, and then the messages came pouring in so fast the phone buzzed nonstop in his hand.

> **Cipher_Lin:** !!!!!!!!!!
> **Viper_99:** VANE
> **Viper_99:** YOU STUPID MONKEY
> **Viper_99:** WHERE ARE YOU
> **Ghost_Kev:** Are you okay.
> **Cipher_Lin:** get in the Nest. right now. door's open

Elias looked at the rig by the window, at the headset on its stand, at the keyboard worn into the shape of his own fingers. He had just crawled out of a machine that had eaten his whole life, and putting that headset on right now did not sound smart. But those three were the only people in the world who could tell him who he was. And maybe, just maybe, they could help him find her.

> **Elias:** Five minutes.

He got up, went to the window, and lifted one corner of the curtain. The sedan was still there, and behind the windshield a small red point flared and dimmed. Someone had just lit a cigarette.

Elias let the curtain fall, turned around, and for the first time sat down in his own chair. The leather sank under him and fit exactly.
