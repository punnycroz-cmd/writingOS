# CHAPTER 9: THE SECOND FIRE

For the first three seconds, Elias didn't think. He just looked.

The room was dark red, the emergency lights sweeping back and forth. One door to the hallway. One window in the exam room at the back, opening onto the iron fire escape he'd seen that afternoon on his way in. The hallway was sixty feet long, the main stairs at the far end, the elevator in the middle. Twelve men, probably split in two: six up the stairs, six holding the ground floor. From the back alley to the fourth floor by the stairs, carrying heavy gear: ninety seconds, maybe less.

His brain ran all of it without asking him, the same way it counted the cracks in the ceiling and listened for the black sedan's engine. Three years of Vanguard, he thought, and then thought it was probably longer than that. Six years in a lab, maybe, where someone had taught a child how to do the math with his back against a wall.

"Field kit," Lena said. She had already run to the equipment cabinet and was dragging out a silver-gray alloy case the size of a carry-on, with a shoulder strap. "Portable scanner and meds. I never go anywhere without it."

"Anything else?"

She glanced at the safe, and he saw her hesitate for exactly one heartbeat.

"Corwin's notebook," she said, already on her knees in front of the safe, spinning the dial so fast he couldn't follow.

A deep, muffled boom came from the far end of the hall. The stairwell door had been breached. Then boots on stone, fast, a lot of them.

"Forty seconds," Elias said.

Lena pulled the three boxes of documents and the leather notebook out of the safe and stuffed them all into the canvas tote she used for groceries. His hard drive went into the inside pocket of her coat, and she zipped it shut. "Let's go."

He grabbed her wrist and pulled her toward the exam room. The back window was an old wood-framed sash with a brass latch. He yanked the latch. It was stuck solid, painted to the sill years ago.

Out in the hall someone barked an order, then came a knock at 4A next door, then the sound of splintering wood.

Elias stepped back, lifted the steel swivel chair from the console, and drove it straight into the window frame. The glass burst outward into the rain, and cold wind rushed in, driving rain sideways across his face. He used a chair leg to rake away the shards left in the frame.

"Go."

Lena didn't hesitate. She slung the case over her shoulder, threw the tote through the window onto the fire escape landing, and climbed out, her shoes landing on the soaked iron grating. She turned back. "You—"

"Right behind you."

The waiting-room door blew open. He heard it before he saw it: the deadbolt shot through, the wood cracking apart, the hinges shrieking. Then the flashlights mounted on gun barrels swept across the frosted glass of the office partition, two white beams cutting through the red dark.

"Back room! Window!"

A burst of suppressed fire tore through the partition. It didn't sound like the movies. There was no sharp bang, only deep, dull thumps, like someone beating a pillow. But the breaking glass was real, and the acoustic panels ripping apart were real, and so was Lena's computer station exploding into a shower of blue sparks.

Elias ducked. On the wall by the exam room door hung a big red CO₂ extinguisher, a twenty-pounder. He wrenched it off its bracket, pulled the pin, and squeezed the trigger, aiming straight at the doorway just as two black shapes appeared in it. A blast of freezing white gas hit the lead man full in the night-vision goggles. He reeled back, swearing, tearing the goggles off his face. The second man lunged forward, and Elias threw the whole heavy canister into his chest, and while the man was still doubled over, Elias dove for the window and went through it headfirst, rolling across the iron grating. A leftover shard of glass sliced his palm. He didn't feel it.

"Down! Get down, now!"

---

They ran down the fire escape, four floors, the grating slick with rain, the rusted rail rattling under their hands. Lena went first, the case on her shoulder, the tote clutched to her chest. Elias came behind, looking up at every landing.

On the third floor he saw a helmeted head lean out of the broken window above, a gun barrel pointing down.

"Against the wall!"

Lena pressed herself flat against the brick, and Elias pulled her down into a crouch and covered her with his back. Rounds hit the iron rail a foot and a half away and threw sparks. Then the head pulled back in, as if someone had just ordered a withdrawal. Elias didn't understand why, and he didn't stop to find out.

Second floor, first floor. The last section of the fire escape was a sliding iron ladder hauled up high to keep out burglars. Elias kicked the release, and the ladder came down with a crash onto the wet ground.

They dropped into the cobbled alley behind the building. To the left, at the mouth of the alley, the headlights of the three black vans shone straight into the rain. To the right there was darkness, some dumpsters, and a low brick wall.

They ran right. Elias boosted Lena over the wall and climbed after her. On the other side was an empty parking lot, then a street, then another alley. They kept running, on and on, until the fire alarm was only a distant wail behind them, drowned in the rain.

---

They stopped under a railroad overpass, where the rain couldn't reach them and the only water was what dripped from the steel girders overhead. Lena leaned back against a concrete pillar, breathing hard, her wet hair stuck to her cheeks, the case still on her shoulder, the tote still clutched to her chest like a child.

Elias turned and looked back the way they had come. In the distance, over the rooftops, an orange glow was rising in the rain, a column of black smoke climbing and twisting and then being pushed sideways by the wind.

Lena followed his gaze.

"The fourth floor," she said, her voice strangely calm. "They burned it."

Only then did he understand why the man on the fire escape had pulled back. They didn't need to chase anyone anymore. They only needed to leave nothing behind: no data, no notes, nothing a judge or a reporter could pick up and ask questions about. Just like 2004. A fire and one line in a report: *gas leak accident*.

Lena stood watching that orange glow for a long time. Five years. The acoustic panels she'd chosen the color of herself, the brass plate she polished every Monday morning, the armchair where Corwin used to sit reading while he waited for her to finish work. All of it burning.

Then she took out her phone and dialed.

"Nia," she said when the other end picked up. "Nia, listen to me. Where are you?" A pause. "Home? Good. Don't go out. Don't go to the office. If anyone asks you anything tomorrow morning, just say you don't know where I am, because you really don't." A longer pause. "I'm okay. I promise. I'll call you later."

She hung up, pulled out the battery, and threw the phone and the battery into a puddle under the overpass.

"They'll be watching this number," she said, as if explaining. Then she stood staring at the puddle, at the phone sinking under the muddy water, and her hands started to shake. Not from the cold.

Elias stood a step away from her. He didn't know what to do, didn't know what people usually did at a moment like this. Maybe they hugged. Maybe they said something comforting. But he had no memory of ever comforting anyone, or of ever being comforted.

So he did the one thing he could. He took off his oversized wool coat and put it around her shoulders.

She looked up at him. Water dripped from the girders into the space between them.

"Tell me about Maren," she said, and her voice broke right on the name. "The little girl. What do you remember about her?"

Elias looked at her, this woman with soaked hair and a white face, wearing his coat. He thought about the girl in his head, the girl screaming in the firelight. He searched the smooth blank space behind his eyes for anything at all, a laugh, something she'd said, a game, and found only one thing.

"I only remember one thing," he said. "She made me promise I'd come find her."

Lena looked at him for a long time. On her face, the tears and the rain could no longer be told apart.

"And you did," she said.

In the distance, the fire on the fourth floor of the St. Jude Building burned a little brighter in the rain, and then began to die down.
