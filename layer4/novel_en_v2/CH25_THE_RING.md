# CHAPTER 25: THE RING

For three days at the lighthouse, they lived like real lighthouse keepers.

Viper found an old fishing rod in the basement, the line rotted, the hooks rusted. He sat on the rocks all morning fixing it, then sat there all afternoon fishing and didn't catch a thing. On the third day he landed a little cod the size of his hand, held it up to show everyone like a championship trophy, then threw it back because "it looked so sad at me."

Linh and Kev barely left the control room. They traded shifts working eighteen hours a day, tracing the undersea cable through cyberspace foot by foot. Three times a day Lena brought them food, and every time she found them in the same positions: Kev with his feet up on the chair, Linh on the floor leaning back against the legs of Kev's chair. On the third trip she found Linh asleep with her head on Kev's knee, and Kev typing with one hand so he wouldn't wake her.

Lena set the tray down very quietly and left, and didn't tell anyone.

Elias slept. Still a lot, but less. When he was awake he climbed to the top of the lighthouse and sat in the round lamp room with its storm-glass windows, and drew. He didn't draw the lab hallway anymore. Lena caught a glimpse once: he had drawn the lighthouse looking up from below, Viper's cod, the camping stove. Things from now.

And Lena peeled apples, read Corwin's notebook over and over, and didn't talk to anyone about it.

---

At ten in the morning on the fifth, Viper's satellite phone rang.

It was Diego, calling from the ranger station at the foot of the mountain, the only place with a signal for twenty miles. Viper had heard three sentences when he sat down hard on the rock, his face white, his hands shaking, and Lena thought something terrible had happened.

Then Rosie's voice came out of the phone's speaker, so loud the whole point could hear it.

"DAD! Dad! I saw a MOOSE! For real! It was as big as the taco truck! Uncle Diego says its name is Gerald!"

Viper laughed, then cried, then laughed and cried at the same time, the loud, messy way of a big man who had never done anything quietly in his life. He held the phone in both hands and kept nodding, even though Rosie couldn't see him.

"Gerald? You sure its name's Gerald? Sounds like an accountant."

"Uncle Diego named it! I wanted to name it Sir Antlers, but Uncle Diego says moose don't like names that start with Sir!"

Lena turned away to give him some privacy and followed the edge of the rocks down to the little beach below the point. She didn't know where she was going until she realized her hand was clenched around the ring on her chain.

---

The beach was just a narrow strip of pebbles between two granite outcrops, the stones worn smooth by the waves. Some were gray, some black, and every so often there was a pale pink one with a white stripe. The tide was going out, and seaweed lay in heaps on the pebbles, smelling of salt and iodine.

Lena walked to the water's edge. A small wave ran up and wet the toes of her shoes, then slid back. She took off the chain.

The ring lay in her palm, silver, plain, brushed, with a short line engraved on the inside that she had read so many times she knew every stroke: *For my anchor.*

Corwin had called her his anchor. One night on the point north of Bayside, the wind was so strong he had to shout for her to hear him. *There's always a storm in your head, Lena. I don't want to put it out. I just want to be here, next to you, while it blows.* She had laughed in his face and told him it was the cheesiest thing she'd ever heard. He said he'd practiced it all week. Then he knelt on the wet rock, completely forgetting he was wearing light-colored pants, and asked her.

She had said yes.

For eight months she had pictured this moment many times: standing at the water's edge, letting the ring drop, letting the sea take it. A ritual. An ending. In May, Nia had told her to do it, and so had her therapist. *A symbolic act can help the brain close the grief loop.* She knew that was true. She had said it to patients herself.

She raised her hand.

*If you're reading this, Lena, don't believe I'm dead.*

She lowered her hand, then tried again, raising it higher this time, her arm stretched straight out over the sea.

*I'm stubborn as hell. You know that.*

Her hand wouldn't open.

She stood there with her arm out over the water, her fist clenched so tight the edge of the ring dug into her palm. It wasn't that she didn't want to. Part of her wanted to very badly. It wanted relief. It wanted to stop waiting. That part had lived eight months with an empty grave, and it was so tired.

But he might still be alive. Day one hundred ninety-two, a white pod, a man under the ice thinking about the sun and calling her name. Throwing the ring into the sea right now would be like burying someone who was still breathing.

She lowered her arm and sank to her knees on the wet pebbles. The round, cold stones pressed into her knees. She stayed there with the ring in her fist, watching the little waves run up and slide back. She didn't cry. She was just tired, too tired to get up.

---

She didn't hear his footsteps on the pebbles. She only knew he was there when an oversized navy wool coat settled over her shoulders. The same coat, from the hospital, from under the overpass. It still smelled of rain and smoke and Viper's chili.

Elias sat down beside her, an arm's length away. He didn't say anything. He picked up a pink pebble with a white stripe and turned it over and over in his hand.

"I can't do it," Lena said.

"I know."

"For eight months everybody's told me I have to. Nia, my therapist, even his mother. Let it go. Let him go. Move on." She looked at her fist. "I was really going to. Today."

"And then you read the notebook."

"And then I read the notebook."

He set the pink pebble down, carefully, as if it were something that could break.

"You don't have to," he said.

She turned to look at him.

"You don't have to let it go," he said. "Nobody gets to tell you when you're done grieving. He might be alive. If he's alive, that ring isn't a keepsake. It's a promise that isn't finished yet." He looked out at the sea. "I know what it's like to hold on to a promise that isn't finished."

Lena didn't say anything. The sea wind pulled a strand of hair out of her knot and whipped it across her cheek.

"We'll find him," Elias said. "Kev and Linh are close to finding the relay node. Nyx has the coordinates. Viper has a taco truck he believes will float." The corner of his mouth moved up a little. "We'll find him and bring him home. Then you can decide for yourself what to do with the ring."

She looked at him for a long time.

She was an examiner. She made her living reading people's faces. She could see what people didn't say in the lag of a smile, in the way their eyes slid away a tenth of a second before they answered. Right now, on his face, she could see all of it. And still he said it, without looking away, without hesitating, as if it were the most natural thing in the world. As if he weren't promising to bring back the only man standing between him and...

She didn't let herself finish the thought.

"You're an idiot," she said, and her voice broke in the middle.

"Linh says that too."

"Linh's right."

She put the chain back around her neck, and the ring slid down under her collar and rested against her breastbone, freezing cold.

Then she did something she hadn't planned to do. She tipped her head and leaned it on his shoulder. Only a little, only part of her weight, the way you'd lean on a railing. He went still as a statue. She could feel him holding his breath.

They sat like that on the wet pebbles, her wearing his coat, until the tide had gone all the way out and the waves no longer reached their feet.

---

That night, Nguyễn Hoài Linh found Eli at the top of the lighthouse.

She had climbed a hundred and twelve spiral stairs to get there, and when she reached the top she was panting like a dog after a race. He was sitting in the lamp room with his back to the stairs, looking out at the night sea. The lighthouse lamp had been dark for decades, but he had found a flashlight and stood it on end on the floor, pointing at the ceiling, for a little light.

His sketchbook lay open on his lap. Linh glanced over his shoulder before she could stop herself and saw a charcoal drawing: two figures sitting on a pebble beach, seen from behind, one with her head on the other's shoulder.

He closed the sketchbook. Not in a hurry, not hiding it. He just closed it.

Linh sat down next to him, still panting. Bubbles sat on her shoulder and blew one tiny bubble.

"A hundred and twelve steps," she said. "I counted. Whoever built this lighthouse really hated people."

He didn't answer, and she didn't need him to.

They sat looking at the sea. Far, far out, a buoy's red light was blinking: on for three seconds, off for two.

"Eli," Linh said.

"Yeah."

"I'm going to ask you something, and you have to tell me the truth. You're not allowed to stay quiet and pretend that means you didn't lie. Viper says that's the worst thing a man can do."

"He's told me that already."

"So you know the rules." Linh took a breath. "You're in love with her, aren't you?"

Bubbles stopped blowing bubbles.

He didn't turn to look at her. He kept looking out at the sea, at the red buoy. On three seconds, off two, on three.

"Yes," he said.

Linh had guessed the answer a long time ago, from the second time she caught him looking at Lena across Viper's dinner table. But hearing him say it, in that flat voice like he was talking about the weather, still made her chest tighten.

"And Corwin?"

"I'm going to bring him back to her."

He said that in exactly the same weather-report voice.

Linh looked at the side of his face in the flashlight glow coming up from below. She thought about the last three years, about a friend who never said anything except *left*, *right*, *out of ammo*, about the time he abandoned his position and ran across half the map to pull her out of a trap. *Lose a match, you play it again. Lose a person, you don't.*

"You know," she said, "sometimes I wish you were a little more selfish."

He didn't answer.

Linh leaned her head on his shoulder, the way Lena had on the beach that afternoon. The only difference was that she did it because she knew he needed someone to lean on him, and Lena couldn't be that person yet.

They sat like that, watching the red buoy blink out at sea, until Kev called up over the radio to say he had just found the relay node.
