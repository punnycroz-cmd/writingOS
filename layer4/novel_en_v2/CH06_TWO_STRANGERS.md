# CHAPTER 6: TWO STRANGERS

In the end, Elias slept in the armchair in her waiting room.

That hadn't been the plan. The plan was that he would go home, she would lock up, and both of them would get a night to think. But by the time he could stand, it was nearly ten, the rain was coming down in sheets, and he had just lost close to a pint of blood through his nose. She asked how he was getting home. He said he'd walk. She asked where to. He said to an apartment with a black car parked outside it since the afternoon.

She handed him a blanket without a word.

Lena didn't sleep. She sat in her office with the frosted glass door half shut, going over the last stretch of data before the crash again and again. She looked at the frame of the girl dozens of times, zoomed in, sharpened it, compared it with her memory of the photo in Margaret's drawer. She told herself that the human brain finds faces in things that aren't faces, in clouds, in electrical outlets, in water stains on walls. She told herself that the lullaby was probably a well-known folk song she'd heard in some movie and forgotten.

She told herself all kinds of things, until it got light.

---

At six in the morning Elias woke with the feeling of being watched. Lena was standing in the office doorway holding two paper cups. She was still in yesterday's clothes, dark circles under her eyes, her hair in a loose knot with a few strands falling down her neck.

"Black coffee," she said, setting a cup on the side table next to him. "I didn't know how you take it."

"Neither do I." He sat up, his neck stiff, and took a sip. Bitter and hot. "Black's fine."

She sat down in the chair across from him, a full coffee table between them. He noticed that the distance wasn't an accident. She had put the chair exactly there, at exactly that angle, so she could see both him and the door.

"I need to set some ground rules," she said.

"Go ahead."

"One. This is an investigation, not therapy. I'm not your doctor. I'm trying to find out why your name is in my fiancé's notebook and why his memories are in your head."

Elias nodded.

"Two. Don't call me by that name anymore."

He was quiet for a second. "Okay."

"Three..." She stopped and looked down into her coffee. "You tell me everything you know. Don't hold anything back, even things you think don't matter."

He told her all of it: the hospital, the chart, the bare apartment, the notebook. He took the photo of the burned house and the folded contract out of his coat pocket and laid them on the table. She read without saying anything. Only her fingers tightened slightly at the line about *permanent removal*.

The black sedan was the one thing he left out. He told himself it was because she already knew. Really it was because he didn't want to say out loud what he'd been thinking all night: that the car had followed him here, and that right now it might be parked right outside this building.

When he finished, Lena went into her office and came back with a leather notebook.

"Corwin was investigating Halloway Neuro-Systems for over a year," she said, opening the notebook and setting it in front of him. "He started with his father's death. Thomas was a police officer. He died in 2012, when his car went into a river on a road he'd driven a thousand times. The report blamed the autonomous driving assistant, the Echo in the car misreading a sign. The carmaker paid a settlement. Halloway sent condolences. Corwin never believed it."

"Echo," Elias repeated. "The thing in Somnia?"

"Back then it was just an assistant on phones and in cars. Now it's everywhere." She turned a page. "Corwin wrote this: *Dad turned off the Echo on his phone in 2011. He couldn't turn off the one in the car.*"

Elias turned the pages one by one. Corwin's handwriting was big and loose, slanting hard to the left. Names, numbers, and then his own name, underlined twice.

"He found me before I knew who I was," he said quietly.

"And then he disappeared." Lena closed the notebook. "In February. He went out alone on a survey boat, said he was meeting a source. Three days later the search team found wreckage off the reef on the north shore. They never found a body."

"I'm sorry."

She gave a short nod, as though she had heard it so many times she'd learned how to take it without letting it touch her.

"The memory in your head," she said. "That kitchen is ours. It's Corwin's point of view. I don't know how it got to you. By every theory I know, memories can't move from one person to another unless..." She trailed off.

"Unless what?"

"Unless both people are on the same network at the same time."

They looked at each other. Neither of them said the next part, but it hung between them, heavy and cold. If Corwin died in February, how could he have been on the network in August?

Lena looked away first.

---

They spent the whole morning trying to fit the pieces together. She showed him a map of the places in Corwin's notebook. He didn't recognize any of them. She showed him photos of people Corwin had interviewed. No one was familiar. She asked about the thirty years in the coma, and he tried to describe them, but they kept slipping through his fingers like water: a ship, a train station, a child calling him Dad, a yellow kitchen. The pieces didn't belong to each other, and they didn't belong to him.

"There's one piece I can hold on to," he said. "A white corridor with two doors right next to each other."

"Do you remember which one you were behind?"

He closed his eyes. The corridor came up under fluorescent light: pale green linoleum with a crack running diagonally across it, two white doors. One was marked *0*, the other *1*.

"Zero," he said. "I was behind door zero."

Lena said nothing. When he opened his eyes, she was looking down at her hand, and her fingers had gone still.

"And door one?" she asked, very quietly.

"I don't know." That was the truth, but at the edge of the memory something was stirring, like a fish under cloudy water. A sound. Knocking. He couldn't catch it.

Lena stood up. "I have to go out for a while. There's someone I need to see."

"Can I come with you?"

She looked at him, and for a second he thought she'd say no. Then she glanced out the window at the street below, and he saw her eyes stop on one spot. He didn't need to follow her gaze to know what was there.

"No," she said. "Stay here. Lock the door. Don't open it for anyone but me or Nia." She pulled on her coat. "I'll be back before noon."

---

Willowbrook Senior Living sat at the edge of the city in the middle of a garden that was tended a little too carefully, its rosebushes clipped into perfect spheres. For three years Lena had come every Sunday. Today was Wednesday, and the nurse at the front desk raised her eyebrows but didn't ask.

Margaret was sitting by the window in her room with a wool blanket over her lap, looking out at the garden. She was seventy-eight. Her white hair was still combed neatly, and her age-spotted hands rested on the arms of the chair, the fingers moving slightly as if knitting an invisible scarf.

"Hi, Grandma," Lena said, sitting down in the chair beside her.

Margaret turned. Her pale blue eyes looked at Lena, kind, polite, and completely blank.

"Hello," she said. "Are you new here? Which floor do you work on?"

Lena smiled. Three years, and she was used to that smile, used even to the way it made her chest ache every time.

"It's Lena, Grandma. Your granddaughter."

"Oh." Margaret nodded as if Lena had told her an interesting story that had nothing to do with her. "I have a granddaughter too. She's very bright. She wants to be a doctor."

"I know, Grandma."

They sat together for a while. Lena talked about the weather, about Nia, about the rosebushes outside. Margaret listened and nodded and now and then said something unrelated, about a cat named Biscuit that had died in '98, about the blue dress she wore on her wedding day.

Then Lena took out her phone.

Last night she had saved a clip of audio from the scan data, twelve seconds long, scratchy and distorted, but the melody was intact. She didn't know why she'd done it. Maybe because she needed someone to tell her she was imagining things.

She played it very softly. A woman's low, rough voice, singing in a foreign language, rising and falling in threes.

Margaret's fingers stopped knitting the invisible scarf. She turned her head toward the phone, slowly, very slowly, and for a moment those cloudy pale eyes cleared, like a lake when the wind dies.

And she began to hum along.

No words, just the melody, but every note right, every rise and fall right, right down to the catch of breath at the end of the second line, the spot where the recording crackled so badly it almost dropped out.

Lena felt her heart stop.

"Grandma," she whispered. "You know this song?"

Margaret stopped humming. She looked at Lena, and for the first time in three years, those eyes actually saw her.

The spotted hand reached out and took Lena's. The thin fingers squeezed harder than she expected.

"Thomas said to keep you safe," Margaret said, her voice clear and urgent, like someone saying a thing she'd held in her mouth for twenty years. "He said never to tell. Never to let anyone know. I kept my word. I kept it."

"Tell what, Grandma? What did you keep your word about?"

Margaret's eyes filled with tears.

"Maren," she said. "My little girl. Maren."

Then the lake rippled again. Her gaze drifted, clouded, receded. Her fingers loosened. Margaret turned back to the window and the perfectly round rosebushes.

"The roses are so late this year," she said to no one. "Have you noticed?"

Lena sat like a statue, her hand still lying on the arm of the chair where Margaret had held it. The recording had ended long ago. The only sounds in the room were the hum of the air conditioner and a cart rolling past in the hall.

*Maren.*

The name had stopped being a stranger. It was knocking on a door somewhere very deep inside her, three quick, two slow, and she didn't know whether to open it or lock it tight.
