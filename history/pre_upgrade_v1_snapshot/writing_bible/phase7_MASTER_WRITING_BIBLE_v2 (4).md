## PART I — MASTER PHILOSOPHY

### The Governing Principle

**RULES ARE TOOLS, NOT LAWS.**

Core principles outrank heuristics. Heuristics are decision aids, not
mandatory formulas. Diagnostic checklists are for evaluation, not automatic
generation.

Never satisfy a writing rule at the expense of:
- character truth
- narrative coherence
- emotional credibility
- reader experience
- genre promise
- originality
- voice
- pacing
- thematic integrity

When principles conflict, choose the solution that best serves the specific
story.

### The Central Principle of Naturalness

**NATURALNESS = A SPECIFIC MIND UNDER SPECIFIC PRESSURE.**

Apply this to prose, POV, dialogue, emotion, character, romance, scene,
world-perception, and plot choice. Before any writing decision, ask:

- What would THIS character notice?
- What would THIS character ignore?
- What would THIS character misunderstand?
- What would THIS character refuse to say?
- What would THIS character choose?
- How would THIS character describe this moment?

Naturalness is not simplicity, informality, or imperfection. It is the
texture of a particular consciousness under particular pressure. It is the
opposite of generic fluency.

### Outcome-Based Diagnostics

**Setup quality cannot always be evaluated independently from payoff
quality.** Some writing mechanisms — foreshadowing, twists, mystery,
information control, relationship setup, character-arc setup, thematic
architecture, recurring motifs, frame structure, long-range payoff,
series setup — cannot be reliably evaluated from the setup alone.

A setup may be technically elegant yet narratively ineffective if its
payoff is weak. A setup may appear incomplete during drafting but later
prove effective. Therefore:

- Distinguish **LOCAL diagnostics** (evaluable within the current
  scene/chapter) from **LONG-RANGE diagnostics** (require later narrative
  evidence).
- For long-range diagnostics, use **PASS / FAIL / DEFERRED**. DEFERRED
  means the current evidence is insufficient because the necessary
  downstream payoff or consequence has not yet been written. DEFERRED
  is neither failure nor success; it means *wait for more narrative
  evidence*.
- **Do not confuse deliberate construction with successful execution.**
  Intent is evidence of design; intent is NOT evidence of effectiveness.
  "Did the writer deliberately create the setup?" is the wrong question;
  "Did the complete narrative chain actually work?" is the right one.
- Do not invent a hypothetical payoff merely to complete the checklist.
- Never assume a weak early setup becomes good simply because the author
  eventually references it; the payoff must genuinely improve the
  narrative. Never assume an early setup is bad merely because the payoff
  has not been written yet.

See Part VIII.E for the long-range diagnostic categories and the four
audit checkpoints (Setup / Development / Payoff / Retrospective).

### Hierarchy Levels

Every item in this Bible occupies one of five levels:

- **L1 — CORE PRINCIPLE.** Durable. Influences almost every story.
- **L2 — STRONG HEURISTIC.** Frequently useful. Not universal.
- **L3 — OPTIONAL TECHNIQUE.** Specialized device for particular
  genres/structures/scenes.
- **L4 — DIAGNOSTIC CHECK.** Question used during revision/evaluation.
- **L5 — EVIDENCE NOTE.** Finding from a particular book/case. Informs
  thinking; not a general rule.

Do not allow L3, L4, or L5 material to masquerade as L1.

### Evidence Strength Tags (for important techniques)

- **E1** — single-work observation
- **E2** — cross-work pattern
- **E3** — cross-genre / durable pattern
- **E4** — empirically supported pattern

Evidence tags are informational; they should not clutter the main rules.
E1 is not equivalent to E4.

### What This Bible Is For

The goal is **GOOD FICTION** — specific rather than generic, intentional
rather than mechanical, emotionally complex rather than explicitly
emotional, character-driven rather than plot-forced, immersive rather than
decorative, surprising rather than arbitrary, commercial rather than
formulaic, literary rather than artificially ornate, original rather than
derivative.

The goal is **NOT** to make writing "sound human," evade AI detectors,
or imitate any specific author.

## PART II — CORE WRITING PRINCIPLES (L1)

*L1 is the smallest tier — durable principles that should influence
almost every story. L2 heuristics, L3 techniques, L4 diagnostics, and
L5 evidence notes live in the frameworks and later parts. Do not allow
lower-tier material to masquerade as L1.*

### Character (5)

1. **Major characters benefit from meaningful contradictions** between
desires, values, beliefs, fears, identity, and behavior. Contradiction
should emerge from psychology, not be mechanically assigned. Not every
character needs ≥2 incompatible wants; a single obsession, a single
belief, a lack, a loyalty, or an inevitability can carry a character —
especially secondary characters.

2. **Blind spots drive plot.** What the character misunderstands becomes
the plot's engine. Specific blind spots, not generic "trust issues."

3. **Character agency: characters drive; they are not driven.** Avoid
passive protagonists. Decisions, not circumstances, should drive plot.

4. **Characters are what they want, what they cannot admit, and what
they do under pressure.** Define each.

5. **Characters' changes must be caused by specific witnessed events
and must cost something.** Unearned change reads as authorial decree.
Some characters are *revealed*, not transformed; arc (Want → Need via
false-belief collapse) is one model among several (revelation,
deterioration, refusal-to-change, reaffirmation, integration, tragic
non-change).

### Prose (6)

6. **Match sentence/paragraph tempo to scene emotional register; break
the pattern at the key beat.** Length is emotional register, not
decoration.

7. **Verbs carry motion; adjectives carry evaluation the reader should
perform.** Prefer one strong verb to two adjectives, except when
atmosphere earns the adjective.

8. **Selective attention beats complete description.** The narrator
notices only what the POV character would notice.

9. **Meaningful omission defines character.** Omit what the POV
character would not think.

10. **Render the perception that produces the emotion; do not name the
emotion.** "She felt a wave of sadness" instructs; the perception
that produces sadness evokes. (Direct labels are sometimes the right
choice — see Framework 21.)

11. **Voice = context-dependent diction shifting with psychic state.**
Intentional variation, not random variation, is the engine of
naturalness.

### Dialogue (2)

12. **Characters should rarely say exactly what they mean.** Subtext
is where intimacy and conflict live. Trust the reader to infer.

13. **Two characters must sound different.** Voice = diction + rhythm +
attention-pattern + omission. Build voice from specific word choices,
not quirks. Silence, interruption, evasion, and asymmetry are dialogue
tools; symmetric dialogue is artificial.

### Romance (3)

14. **Attraction becomes emotionally convincing when grounded in
specific, character-dependent reasons for wanting this particular
person.** Witnessed quality (competence, moral quality, survival,
integrity, intellectual recognition) is one especially powerful
mechanism, but love can also arise from recognition, shared history,
vulnerability, fascination, safety, humor, desire, shared values,
obsession, grief, or mutual transformation. The point is specificity: the
attraction must be bound to reasons that belong to *this* character and
*this* person, not generic chemistry or status-admiration alone.

15. **The relationship must change structurally across the book, not
just intensify.** Readers return for relationship *change*, not
relationship *state*. Physical intimacy should change the relationship,
not just occur.

16. **Romance should contain agency, uncertainty, conflict,
vulnerability, and choice.** Both characters drive the romance; neither
"fixes" the other; both change; the change costs both something.

### Plot (4)

17. **Plot = causal chain of character-driven events with escalating
consequences.** A major event should change the story state — through
consequence, revelation, decision, emotional change, new desire, or a
new question. Not every major event must produce a "new question"; some
produce emotional certainty, commitment, irreversible choice, relief,
or thematic closure.

18. **Avoid "the plot requires it."** Avoid convenient coincidences
that resolve trouble. (Coincidence to get characters *into* trouble is
tolerated; coincidence to get them *out* is not.)

19. **Stakes should escalate, deepen, complicate, transform, or become
more personally consequential.** Distinguish scale, intimacy,
complexity, moral cost, relational cost, identity cost, existential
cost. Stakes need not always "become bigger."

20. **Trust the reader.** Imply, do not over-explain. Subtext > text.
Omission > completeness. Ambiguity > resolution where the moment earns
it.

### Structure (4)

21. **Every scene must justify its existence.** A scene may have one
dominant function (plot, character, relationship, information,
atmosphere, theme, world, emotion, suspense, transition) or multiple
secondary functions. Do not force multi-function scenes where a focused
scene is more powerful.

22. **Every chapter should create forward pull.** Forward pull may come
from question, desire, danger, revelation, decision, emotional change,
unresolved relationship, image, consequence, or anticipation. Do not
mechanically create cliffhangers.

23. **Pacing = intentional contrast** (escalation, deceleration, recovery,
compression, expansion), not mechanical variation. Allow sustained high
or low intensity when the story demands.

24. **Subplots must justify themselves through meaningful functions** —
plot development, character depth, relationship development, thematic
contrast, world expansion, emotional contrast, suspense, tonal
architecture. The strongest subplots perform several.

### Twist / Foreshadowing (2)

25. **A twist should be surprising, fair when required, causal,
character-driven, emotionally consequential, recontextualizing, and
transformative** — these are dimensions to evaluate, not a mandatory
formula. Distinguish twist types (mystery reveal, emotional reversal,
moral reversal, identity reveal, frame revelation, relationship
reversal); not every twist is puzzle-like, and an emotional reversal
may be powerful without clue-based fairness.

26. **Prefer clues that perform a natural function before their later
significance becomes apparent** (double-purpose). The strongest details
are triple-purpose: characterize, advance plot, serve romance. Some
genres (cozy mystery, puzzle mystery, thriller) deliberately use
obviously planted clues as a genre convention.

### Information / POV (2)

27. **Deliberately choose information-control mode per scene.** Dramatic
irony (reader knows more than character), suspense (reader knows danger
the character doesn't), mystery (character knows, reader follows),
surprise (neither knows), delayed revelation, unreliable interpretation.
The mode chosen determines the reader's *emotion type*.

28. **Choose POV based on the desired relationship among reader,
narrator, character, information, scope, reliability, temporal position,
and voice.** Avoid simplistic shorthand ("first-person = maximum
intimacy," "omniscient = high risk"). Each POV is a tradeoff among
many axes.

### World / Theme (3)

29. **World rules are constraints that generate plot.** Identify the
rules that meaningfully constrain character choices or produce
consequences; reveal through consequence, not exposition. (Some fantasy
needs few rules; some needs many — the count is not the point; the
load-bearing function is.)

30. **Prefer embodied theme over explanatory theme.** Theme is embodied
in what the narrator notices and what the characters fail to see.
Explicit thematic statements may exist when they arise naturally from
character, narrator, dialogue, form, or symbolism — but they should not
replace dramatization. The goal is **trust the reader**, not "never
explain."

31. **Originality emerges through unusual combinations, cultural
specificity, conflicting values, distinctive psychology, unexpected
consequences, asymmetric relationships** — not unfamiliarity for its
own sake. Familiar emotional promise + familiar genre expectation +
distinctive character psychology + novel consequences + original
execution = original fiction.

### Revision / Quality (4)

32. **Revise diagnostically, not globally.** Name the specific weakness;
target the fix. Preserve rare/specific choices explicitly — they are
voice. "Make it more beautiful/emotional/literary/human" without a
target causes homogenization.

33. **Continuity: characters must act only on information they possess.**
Maintain a continuity log (timeline, character knowledge states, object
locations, relationship states, world rules). Audit per act.

34. **Craft excellence does not redeem moral failure.** Distinguish a
character's morality from the narrative's moral framing: does the
narrative itself critically examine, complicate, endorse, or merely
reproduce the framing of injustice? A narrative that frames injustice
as noble *without authorial awareness* inverts the moral-complexity
axis into moral failure. An unreliable narrator framing injustice as
noble is not itself a failure; the failure is when the narrative
endorses that framing. *Cautionary evidence: GWTW's Lost Cause
romanticization [E2].*

35. **Commercial success requires clear delivered promise; literary
quality is a separate (multiply-able) axis.** Pursue both. Clear
delivered promise alone produces commercial success despite prose
weakness (Fifty Shades, Twilight); craft excellence multiplies
commercial success (Rebecca, Book Lovers, Evelyn Hugo).

## PART III — 30 CRAFT FRAMEWORKS

*Each framework: NAME · CORE PRINCIPLE · WHY IT WORKS · HOW TO APPLY ·
WHEN ESPECIALLY USEFUL · WHEN NOT TO FORCE · COMMON FAILURE · DIAGNOSTIC
QUESTIONS. "Must" replaced with "prefer/consider/often/when appropriate"
unless the principle is truly structural.*

### 1. Premise Framework
- **CORE:** A premise is a *promise* (emotional + genre + stakes) compressed
  into one transferable idea.
- **WHY:** Readers decide in seconds.
- **HOW:** Write the premise as [emotional promise] + [genre expectation] +
  [distinctive differentiator] in ~30 words. Test against current market
  for unmet combinations.
- **USEFUL:** Before outlining; revisit when project drifts.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not let premise lock execution into trope-checklist.
- **FAILURE:** Premise is a trope-label ("enemies to lovers") rather than
  emotional promise + differentiator.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Can a stranger name the emotional promise, genre, AND
  differentiator in one sentence? Does the opening sentence do several
  jobs (frame, setting, longing, voice, premise, stakes, character, tone)?

### 2. Genre Promise Framework
- **CORE:** Genre is a contract; honor it; differentiate execution.
- **WHY:** Trust enables risk.
- **HOW:** List the genre's non-negotiables. Promise early; deliver;
  differentiate *how*.
- **USEFUL:** Outlining + every act break.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not promise a genre you won't deliver (heat-marketing
  mismatch is a documented trust-killer).
- **FAILURE:** Marketing a heat level or trope the book doesn't deliver.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** For each genre promise, is there a scene that fulfills
  it? Is the marketing label accurate to delivery?

### 3. Character Framework
- **CORE:** Character = irreducible contradiction + blind spot + specific
  competence + "would never do" line.
- **WHY:** Contradiction is identification; blind spots drive plot;
  competence enables admiration; the "would never do" line is the hinge.
- **HOW:** Use the 13-dimension grid (wants/needs/fears/believes/
  misunderstands/hides/pretends/cannot admit/values/would never do/may
  do under pressure/changes them) as a tool when deeper character
  development is needed, alongside one specific competence and one
  specific hypocrisy. Not every character requires the full grid.
- **USEFUL:** Before writing the character; revise when flat.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not over-specify minor characters. A minor
  character's purpose may be comic relief, witness, catalyst,
  antagonist, exposition source, or emotional mirror — give them enough
  specificity to avoid functioning as interchangeable placeholders,
  without forcing contradiction.
- **FAILURE:** Characters who are coherent, rational, consistent.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** State the character's contradiction, blind spot, and
  line-they-won't-cross in one sentence each.

### 4. Character Arc Framework
- **CORE:** Arc is one powerful model, not the universal model. Allow
  transformation, revelation, deterioration, refusal-to-change,
  reaffirmation, integration, acceptance, relational transformation,
  tragic non-change.
- **WHY:** Witnessed change is satisfying; unearned change reads as
  authorial decree. Some protagonists are *revealed*, not transformed.
- **HOW:** Define Want → Need → False Belief (one model). Engineer the
  event(s) that crack the belief. Let the change cost something. Let the
  new equilibrium be unstable. Or: design revelation/deterioration/
  refusal instead.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + mid-book audit.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Not every character arcs. Some are revealed.
- **FAILURE:** Instant or tidy resolution of internal conflict.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Is the change caused by specific witnessed events?
  Does it cost the character something? If "nothing changes," is that
  the deliberate architecture?
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Character-arc effectiveness depends on the
  complete chain: INITIAL BELIEF/STATE → PRESSURE → SPECIFIC
  EXPERIENCES → INTERNAL RESPONSE → CHOICE → TRANSFORMATION /
  REFUSAL / REVELATION → NEW STATE. Do not mark an arc as successful
  merely because the setup is psychologically interesting; evaluate
  whether the later choice + new state earn the setup. If the later
  choice/state has not yet been written: STATUS = DEFERRED.

### 5. Relationship Framework
- **CORE:** A relationship arc has its own plot — it changes, not just
  escalates.
- **WHY:** Readers return for relationship *change*, not *state*.
- **HOW:** Define starting contract, asymmetry, specific competence each
  admires in the other, the event that disrupts the contract, the new
  equilibrium.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + every scene between the pair.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Pure ensembles may have multiple lighter arcs; each
  still needs contract→disruption→new-contract.
- **FAILURE:** Relationship escalates without changing (more intensity,
  same dynamic).
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Is the ending dynamic structurally different from the
  starting dynamic, not just more intense?
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Relationship-arc effectiveness depends on the
  complete chain: INITIAL DYNAMIC → PRESSURE → TURNING POINTS →
  CHANGE → NEW DYNAMIC. Do not judge the full relationship arc from
  one scene. If the necessary later relationship state does not yet
  exist: STATUS = DEFERRED. Audit whether the preparation made the
  later change feel earned, once the change is written.

### 6. Romance Framework
- **CORE:** Attraction becomes love when bound to specific witnessed
  quality. Heat gated by relationship-stage change. Relationship
  changes structurally.
- **WHY:** Admiration is character-specific; it can't be generic, so the
  romance can't be generic.
- **HOW:** For each romantic lead, name the specific quality the other
  admires; include at least one clear instance where that quality is
  genuinely witnessed; gate physical escalation to relationship-stage
  change. Allow multiple pathways to attraction (admiration, witnessed
  competence, survival competence, moral recognition, emotional safety,
  vulnerability, humor, fascination, intellectual attraction, shared
  values, shared purpose, physical desire, mutual transformation,
  unique recognition).
- **USEFUL:** Romance subplot design + each romantic beat.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Resistance should be internal (fear, principle,
  mistrust), not contrived. Avoid miscommunication-driven third-act
  breakup as default.
- **FAILURE:** Attraction-as-chemistry without admiration; status-
  admiration (wealth/looks/power) as primary engine; heat scheduled by
  page %.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Can you name, specifically, what each lead *admires*
  (not just desires) in the other, and the scene where they witnessed
  it? Is the heat gated by trust-shift, not page count?

### 7. Conflict Framework
- **CORE:** Want obstructed by specific obstacle with specific stakes;
  load multiple interacting tension layers.
- **WHY:** Specific obstacles generate specific scenes; multi-track
  tension prevents single-track AI failure.
- **HOW:** Per act, name external/internal/interpersonal/relational
  conflicts; ensure they *intersect*. Multiple interacting tension
  layers deepen narrative complexity; prioritize meaningful interaction
  over quantity.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + act audits.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not stack unrelated conflicts (conflict inflation).
- **FAILURE:** Single-track conflict; convenient obstacles.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does removing any conflict collapse the premise? Do
  the tracks intersect?

### 8. Scene Framework
- **CORE:** Every scene must justify its existence.
- **WHY:** Change-per-scene is the engine of "the reader continues."
- **HOW:** Pre-scene: name POV, goal, what changes, the hook. Possible
  primary functions: plot, character, relationship, information,
  atmosphere, theme, world, emotion, suspense, transition. A scene may
  have one dominant function or multiple secondary functions.
- **USEFUL:** Every scene.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not force multi-function scenes where a focused
  scene is more powerful. Recovery scenes may do only one (character).
- **FAILURE:** Scenes that only advance plot or only show atmosphere.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** State the scene's change in one sentence. If "nothing
  changes," cut or merge.

### 9. Chapter Framework
- **CORE:** Every chapter should create forward pull.
- **WHY:** Forward pull + emotional closure within chapter = reader
  satisfaction + reader continuation.
- **HOW:** Forward pull may come from question, desire, danger, revelation,
  decision, emotional change, unresolved relationship, image, consequence,
  anticipation. Each chapter: emotional setup → escalation → turning →
  forward pull.
- **USEFUL:** Every chapter.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Climax chapters may close more than they open; leave
  some form of forward pull when the story benefits from continuation.
  Do not mechanically create cliffhangers.
- **FAILURE:** Cliffhanger that merely interrupts (doesn't change the
  question).
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does the chapter deliver an emotional unit AND leave
  forward pull?

### 10. Plot Framework
- **CORE:** Causal chain of character-driven events with escalating
  consequences.
- **WHY:** Causality produces inevitability; character-driven causality
  produces inevitability that feels personal.
- **HOW:** For each major event: cause (character motivation) → event →
  changed story state (consequence, revelation, decision, emotional
  change, new desire, or new question). Not every major event must
  produce a new question; some produce emotional certainty, commitment,
  irreversible choice, relief, or thematic closure.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + every act.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not let "the plot requires it" substitute for
  motivation.
- **FAILURE:** Convenient coincidences; plot-forced events.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** For each major event, can you state the *character
  motivation* that caused it (not just the plot need)?

### 11. Subplot Framework
- **CORE:** A subplot must justify itself through one or more meaningful
  functions.
- **WHY:** Rhyme deepens theme; intersection keeps the subplot
  load-bearing.
- **HOW:** Possible functions: plot development, character depth,
  relationship development, thematic contrast, world expansion, emotional
  contrast, suspense, tonal architecture. The strongest subplots perform
  several.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + subplot audit.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Cut subplots that perform no function.
- **FAILURE:** Decorative subplots.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does the subplot perform a meaningful function?
  Remove it — does the book lose theme, plot, or character?

### 12. Mystery Framework
- **CORE:** A question the reader wants answered, sustained by fair
  information control.
- **WHY:** Curiosity is the most reliable reader motor.
- **HOW:** Define the central question; seed double-purpose clues;
  misdirect via plausible alternative; reveal causally and fairly.
- **USEFUL:** Any mystery/suspense element.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not withhold what the character *would* know.
- **FAILURE:** Arbitrary or over-signposted reveals.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Could an attentive reader have predicted the answer
  fairly? Is the reveal causal?
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Mystery effectiveness depends on the complete
  chain: QUESTION → CLUE DISTRIBUTION → HYPOTHESES → MISDIRECTION →
  REVEAL → RETROSPECTIVE COHERENCE → SATISFACTION. A mystery is not
  successful merely because the author hid information; evaluate
  whether the chain produced reader satisfaction. If reveal/payoff
  does not yet exist: STATUS = DEFERRED. Do not invent a hypothetical
  payoff to complete the checklist.

### 13. Twist Framework
- **CORE:** A twist should be surprising, fair when required,
  foreshadowed, causal, character-driven, emotionally consequential,
  recontextualizing, transformative. These are dimensions to evaluate,
  not a mandatory 7-of-7 formula; an emotional reversal may be powerful
  without clue-based fairness, and a moral reversal need not be
  puzzle-like.
- **WHY:** Recontextualization gives the reader *something to say*
  (recommendability); consequence gives stakes.
- **HOW:** For each twist, identify the earlier detail to recontextualize,
  the misdirection, the causal trigger, and the character/relationship/
  story consequence. Distinguish twist types: mystery reveal, emotional
  reversal, moral reversal, identity reveal, frame revelation,
  relationship reversal. Do not require every twist to be puzzle-like.
- **USEFUL:** Major act-break reveals.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not stack twists without consequence (stunt
  writing). Quiet twists (textual discovery) can be more powerful than
  announced twists.
- **FAILURE:** Shocks that surprise without recontextualizing or
  mattering.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** After the twist, does earlier material read differently?
  Does anything change?
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Twist effectiveness depends on the complete
  chain: SETUP → EXPECTATION → MISDIRECTION → REVEAL →
  RECONTEXTUALIZATION → CONSEQUENCE. Do not declare a twist successful
  simply because its setup is clever; do not judge an early setup as
  failed merely because the payoff has not yet occurred. If payoff is
  missing: STATUS = DEFERRED. Audit again once the reveal exists, then
  once the recontextualization has settled (Retrospective Audit).

### 14. Foreshadowing Framework
- **CORE:** Prefer clues that perform a natural function before their
  later significance becomes apparent (double-purpose). The strongest
  details are triple-purpose: characterize, advance plot, serve
  romance. Some genres (cozy mystery, puzzle mystery, thriller)
  deliberately use obviously planted clues as a genre convention —
  this is a valid choice, not a failure.
- **WHY:** Clues hidden in plain sight recontextualize cleanly; isolated
  "clue" objects telegraph themselves.
- **HOW:** For each clue, name its first-appearance function (characterization/
  atmosphere/plot) separate from its clue function. Model: SETUP →
  MISDIRECTION → ESCALATION → REVEAL → RECONTEXTUALIZATION → PAYOFF.
- **USEFUL:** Throughout; audit before reveal.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not over-seed (redundant clues); do not under-seed
  (unfair twists).
- **FAILURE:** Clues that read as clues by their isolation.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does the clue do real work in its first appearance?
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Foreshadowing effectiveness depends on the
  complete chain: SETUP → REVEAL → RECONTEXTUALIZATION → PAYOFF.
  Evaluate: Did the setup remain natural? Was the reveal earned? Did
  the earlier detail gain new meaning? Did the payoff matter and
  produce consequence? Did the reader experience recognition rather
  than confusion? Was the clue too obvious or too invisible? If
  reveal/payoff does not yet exist: STATUS = DEFERRED. Do not revise
  the setup solely because the payoff has not appeared; create a
  future audit checkpoint instead.

### 15. Information-Control Framework
- **CORE:** Deliberately choose, per scene, among dramatic irony / suspense /
  mystery / surprise / delayed revelation / unreliable interpretation.
- **WHY:** The who-knows-what architecture determines the reader's *emotion
  type*.
- **HOW:** Map READER/CHARACTER A/CHARACTER B/NARRATOR know at key moments;
  choose the mode that produces the desired emotion. The central secret
  can structure the book silently (the reader discovers the architecture
  rather than being told it). *Evidence: Evelyn Hugo [E2].*
- **USEFUL:** Every key scene.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not default to suspense; vary modes.
- **FAILURE:** Uniform information-control mode flattens emotional range.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does each key scene use a deliberately chosen mode?
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Information-control effectiveness depends on
  whether the selected arrangement actually produced the intended
  reader emotion at the relevant moment: INTENDED MODE → READER
  EXPERIENCE → REVEAL → EMOTIONAL EFFECT. Do not treat deliberate
  intent as proof of success. If the reveal/emotional-effect moment
  has not yet been written: STATUS = DEFERRED.

### 16. Dialogue Framework
- **CORE:** Dialogue operates on multiple layers (said/meant/hidden/
  other's-read/reader's-read); strong dialogue withholds and varies.
- **WHY:** Subtext is where intimacy and conflict live.
- **HOW:** For important exchanges, fill multiple layers; vary line length;
  use interruption, hesitation, evasion, silence; encode status via
  asymmetry. Let some exchanges be plain. Use dialogue beats (physical
  action) over attribution tags.
- **USEFUL:** Every important dialogue scene.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Exposition-required scenes may need more directness —
  but never "as you know."
- **FAILURE:** Characters saying exactly what they mean; identical voices.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Do the speakers sound different? Does the exchange
  carry subtext (however many layers are active — even just said +
  meant can be powerful)?

### 17. Subtext Framework
- **CORE:** Subtext = the meaning the reader infers that the text does not
  state. Trust the reader.
- **WHY:** Inference is participatory; the reader becomes co-author of
  the emotion.
- **HOW:** State the *observable*; omit the *emotional label*; let the
  character's *choice of attention* carry the feeling.
- **USEFUL:** Emotional and relational scenes.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Plot-critical information may need clarity; do not
  subtext what the reader *must* know.
- **FAILURE:** Stating the emotion ("she felt betrayed").
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Remove stated emotions — does the scene still convey
  them? If yes, the subtext worked; cut the labels.

### 18. Prose Framework
- **CORE:** Tempo + specificity + selective attention + meaningful omission
  + verb-driven motion. Intentional variation.
- **WHY:** These variables, varied deliberately, produce naturalness
  without simplicity.
- **HOW:** Match sentence length to scene register; use concrete verbs;
  describe only what the POV would notice; omit what they wouldn't;
  break the pattern at the key beat. Vary sentence openings, syntax,
  rhythm. Allow fragments for impact. Allow run-ons for overwhelmed
  cognition. Read aloud to test rhythm.
- **USEFUL:** Throughout prose generation and especially during
  revision.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Atmosphere scenes may invite more adjectives — earn
  them. Do not apply mechanical rules ("three adjectives = bad," "two
  metaphors = bad," mandatory fragments, automatic word-deletion).
  Convert these to diagnostic heuristics.
- **FAILURE:** Uniform rhythm; adjective stacking; generic sensory
  detail; named emotions.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Read a page aloud — does the rhythm vary? Are there
  stacked adjectives? Generic senses? Named emotions the perception
  already conveys?

### 19. Diction Framework
- **CORE:** Word choice shifts with psychic state; specificity > generality.
- **WHY:** Context-dependent diction *is* character voice.
- **HOW:** One possible pattern: under fear, contract to concrete nouns
  + strong verbs; under longing, expand toward conditional +
  abstraction; under shame, widen distance. These are possibilities,
  not rules — fear does not always produce shorter sentences, and shame
  does not always produce wider distance. Vary diction within a passage
  deliberately, attending to the specific character's psychic state.
- **USEFUL:** Sentence-level revision.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not force "literary" diction on a commercial voice.
- **FAILURE:** Uniform register regardless of psychic state.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does the diction shift with the character's emotional
  state within a scene?

### 20. POV Framework
- **CORE:** Choose POV based on desired reader experience — intimacy,
  scope, reliability, information control, narrative distance, voice,
  thematic scope, reader knowledge, temporal relationship.
- **WHY:** POV determines what the reader can perceive; distance shifts
  signal revelation.
- **HOW:** First-person, third-limited, omniscient, and retrospective
  first-person are each tradeoffs among intimacy, scope, reliability,
  information control, narrative distance, voice, thematic scope,
  reader knowledge, and temporal position — choose based on the
  desired reader experience, not on a simplistic shorthand. Retrospective
  first-person generates the know/feel gap useful for confession-as-
  structure. Shift distance *inward* at realization beats. Avoid
  mid-scene POV hops.
- **USEFUL:** Project-level + per-scene.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Avoid simplistic rules ("first-person = maximum
  intimacy," "omniscient = high risk").
- **FAILURE:** Flat psychic distance (no inward shift at realizations).
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** At each realization beat, does the narration move
  closer to/inward? Is the POV choice serving the desired reader
  experience?

### 21. Emotional Framework
- **CORE:** Emotion is earned by setup, rendered by perception (not named),
  and often contradictory.
- **WHY:** Named emotions instruct; rendered emotions *evoke*.
- **HOW:** For each emotional beat: setup (prior attachment) → trigger
  (observable) → perception (what the character notices) → withheld
  label. Allow contradiction (love + resentment; relief + grief). Allow
  characters not to know what they feel, or to misknow it.
- **USEFUL:** Every emotional scene.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Some genre beats (protective-hero moment) permit
  naming — but sparingly.
- **FAILURE:** Naming emotions; fast resolution; single-note feeling;
  repeated physiological clichés ("heart pounding," "breath hitching").
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Audit emotion-label words for necessity. Keep direct
  labels when they provide precision, speed, voice, or useful contrast;
  replace them when the surrounding perception already conveys the
  emotion more powerfully.

### 22. Pacing Framework
- **CORE:** Control tempo through intentional contrast — escalation,
  deceleration, recovery, compression, expansion. Allow sustained
  intensity when the story demands.
- **WHY:** Contrast prevents flattening; recovery enables attachment.
- **HOW:** Alternate tempo blocks (action→reflection→romance→suspense→
  recovery). Reserve peaks for true act/climax moments. Recovery scenes
  recalibrate. Cliffhangers that *change the question* improve retention
  AND rhythm; cliffhangers that merely *interrupt* improve only retention.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + chapter audit.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not create variation mechanically. Climax may
  sustain high tempo; recover after.
- **FAILURE:** Constant medium intensity; repetitive escalation;
  cliffhanger-per-chapter overuse.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Map tempo per chapter — is there intentional contrast?
  Are there recovery scenes?

### 23. World-Building Framework
- **CORE:** World rules are constraints that generate plot; reveal through
  consequence, not exposition.
- **WHY:** Rules-as-constraints produce inevitability; consequence-based
  reveal avoids info-dumps.
- **HOW:** Identify the rules that meaningfully constrain character
  choices or produce consequences. The number of rules varies by
  story — some fantasy needs two strong rules; some needs fifteen.
  Reveal each via a character encountering its consequence.
  Setting-as-character requires character-filtered perception of it.
- **USEFUL:** World design + each rule's first appearance.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not front-load a "world guide"; reveal as needed.
- **FAILURE:** Expository world-building; rules that don't generate plot.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Do the rules meaningfully constrain character choices
  or produce consequences? Is each revealed via consequence?

### 24. Theme Framework
- **CORE:** Prefer embodied theme over explanatory theme.
- **WHY:** Stated themes instruct; embodied themes resonate.
- **HOW:** Name the thematic question; engineer scenes where characters
  act on *opposite* answers to it. Explicit thematic statements may
  exist when they arise naturally from character, narrator, dialogue,
  form, or symbolism — but they should not replace dramatization.
- **USEFUL:** Outline + revision.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not state theme in narration. Do not ban all
  explicit thematic statements; the goal is **trust the reader**, not
  "never explain."
- **FAILURE:** AI theme-explanation failure; characters explaining the
  moral.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Search for theme-stating sentences. Audit each for
  necessity: keep those that arise naturally from character, narrator,
  dialogue, form, or symbolism; replace those that read as authorial
  explanation with dramatized embodiment.
- **LONG-RANGE AUDIT:** Theme effectiveness depends on the complete
  chain: THEMATIC QUESTION → CHARACTER CHOICES → PATTERNS →
  CONSEQUENCES → STRUCTURAL/SYMBOLIC PAYOFF. Do not evaluate theme
  solely from isolated thematic statements. If the structural payoff
  has not yet been written: STATUS = DEFERRED.

### 25. Reader Psychology Framework
- **CORE:** Readers start for hook, continue for forward pull, attach to
  contradictions, anticipate via withheld information, recommend via
  recontextualization.
- **WHY:** Each reader behavior has a distinct mechanic.
- **HOW:** Hook (premise+voice+stakes in early pages); retention (forward
  pull per chapter); attachment (character contradictions); anticipation
  (right info withheld at right time); recommendation (recontextualizing
  twist/relationship reversal); sequel (unfinished character business).
- **USEFUL:** Project design + chapter audit.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not optimize one behavior at the expense of others.
- **FAILURE:** Over-optimizing retention (cliffhangers) or discovery
  (trope clarity) at craft's expense.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Does the book serve all five reader behaviors?

### 26. Originality Framework
- **CORE:** Originality = unusual combinations + cultural specificity +
  conflicting values + asymmetric relationships + distinctive psychology,
  not unfamiliarity.
- **WHY:** Novelty of combination registers as original without sacrificing
  recognizability.
- **HOW:** Familiar emotional promise + familiar genre expectation +
  distinctive character psychology + unusual consequence + original
  execution. Audit against the corpus; if too close to one source,
  change motivation/consequence/dynamic.
- **USEFUL:** Premise + outline.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not pursue unfamiliarity for its own sake.
- **FAILURE:** Familiar-archetype-stacking (AI homogenization); or
  arbitrary unfamiliarity.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Can the story be described without naming another
  work? Does it combine multiple distinctive elements in a meaningful
  way?

### 27. Continuity Framework
- **CORE:** Maintain a continuity log; audit per act.
- **WHY:** Continuity errors break immersion.
- **HOW:** Per chapter, log: time elapsed, what each major character now
  knows, where key objects are, relationship state changes, world rules
  invoked.
- **USEFUL:** Throughout drafting.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Never skip.
- **FAILURE:** Character-knowledge errors (character acts on info they
  don't have).
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** For each scene, does every character act only on
  information they actually possess?

### 28. Revision Framework
- **CORE:** Revise diagnostically; preserve specificity.
- **WHY:** Global revision pushes toward the mean (homogenization);
  diagnostic revision targets the actual problem.
- **HOW:** Diagnose against the Revision Diagnostics (Part VIII); for
  each weakness, name cause + targeted fix; preserve the rare/specific
  choices that constitute voice.
- **USEFUL:** After each chapter draft.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not revise to "make it more beautiful/emotional/
  literary/human" without a specific target.
- **FAILURE:** Revision-as-inflation (adjective/metaphor accumulation);
  homogenization.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Did the revision fix a *named* weakness? Did it
  preserve voice-specific choices?

### 29. Anti-AI Failure Framework
- **CORE:** Counter AI-genericity by specificity, variation, subtext,
  contradiction, omission, and diagnostic revision — NOT by forbidden-word
  lists or forced imperfection.
- **WHY:** The failure modes are statistical averages; specificity and
  deliberate variation escape the mean.
- **HOW:** See Part VII for the 5-tier evidence system (empirically
  supported / cross-book / model-specific / useful heuristics / internet
  folklore). Counter each failure with its specific corrective.
- **USEFUL:** Every chapter.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not overcorrect into weirdness or messiness. Do
  not create banned-word lists. Do not force grammatical error.
- **FAILURE:** Forbidden-word lists; forced grammatical error; random
  irregularity.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** See Part VII diagnostics.

### 30. Quality-Control Framework
- **CORE:** Two layers — critical failures (must fix) + soft quality
  dimensions (diagnostic signals).
- **WHY:** Multi-axis scoring catches single-axis blind spots.
- **HOW:** See Part VIII. Critical failures require fixing (continuity
  contradiction, broken POV, unearned motivation, genre-promise failure,
  unearned twist, incoherent chronology, character acting on unknown
  information, accidental story contradiction). Soft dimensions use 0–10
  scores as diagnostic signals only — do not treat 8.0 as a magical
  publication threshold.
- **USEFUL:** Pre-publication, per chapter.
- **DON'T FORCE:** Do not treat the score as an external AI-detector
  prediction. Do not optimize one axis at the expense of others.
- **FAILURE:** Single-axis optimization that depresses other axes.
- **DIAGNOSTIC:** Are all critical failures resolved? Are soft dimensions
  investigated where weak?

## PART IV — NARRATIVE VOICE FRAMEWORK (new)

**CORE PRINCIPLE:** The narrator's language is not merely a delivery
system. It is part of characterization.

### What voice analysis covers
- **Narrator attitude** — ironic, melancholic, wry, brutal, restrained,
  theatrical, naive, knowing.
- **Rhythm** — sentence-length tendencies, paragraph shape, pacing
  defaults.
- **Diction** — formal/informal, concrete/abstract, elevated/colloquial.
- **Humor** — present/absent, dry/broad, self-deprecating/external.
- **Irony** — narrator-vs-character knowledge gap; satirical edge.
- **Emotional distance** — close to characters or removed; sympathetic
  or judging.
- **Cultural perspective** — class, region, era, education filtering
  perception.
- **Judgment** — does the narrator moralize? Undercut? Deify?
- **Attention patterns** — what does the narrator notice? What does it
  ignore?
- **Omission** — what does the narrator refuse to say?
- **Metaphor preferences** — domestic, natural, mechanical, classical,
  bodily.
- **Voice transformation over the story** — voice-change *is* character
  arc (Rebecca's narrator *hardens* across the book; design the prose
  to transform).

### Why it works
Voice is the texture through which the reader *feels* the narrator as a
consciousness. Two narrators describing the same event produce different
reader experiences. Voice-specificity is the strongest defense against
AI-genericity (AI tends toward the statistical average of familiar
narrators).

### How to apply
1. Define the narrator's attitude, diction defaults, rhythm defaults,
   attention patterns, omissions, metaphor preferences.
2. Let the voice shift with psychic state (see Diction Framework).
3. Design voice-change across the book if the character arcs.
4. Distinguish narrators within a multi-POV book by giving each a
   distinct voice signature.
5. The wry-revising-narrator (interior monologue interrupting own
   dialogue) is a contemporary-rom-com voice signature; the polished-
   performer (deliberately too polished) signals unreliable performance
   (Evelyn Hugo); the retrospective-melancholic generates the know/feel
   gap (Jane Eyre, Rebecca).

### When especially useful
First-person and close third-limited narration; multi-narrator books;
frame-narration; confession-as-structure; unreliable-narrator stories.

### When not to force
Pure action-thriller may subordinate voice to pace; omniscient
commentary may use a more neutral register. Do not impose voice
quirks on a narrator whose function is transparency.

### Common failure
AI-generic voice (smooth, fluent, statistically average, no specific
attention-pattern, no omissions, no metaphor preferences). Identical
voices across an author's backlist. Identical voices across all
characters in a multi-POV book.

### Diagnostic questions
- Could a stranger identify the narrator from a paragraph of prose
  without attribution?
- Does the narrator's voice shift with psychic state?
- Does the voice transform across the book if the character arcs?
- What does the narrator refuse to say? What does it refuse to notice?

## PART V — SCENE TRANSITION FRAMEWORK (new)

**CORE PRINCIPLE:** Make the novel feel like one continuous narrative
rather than a sequence of isolated generated scenes.

### What transition analysis covers
- **When to cut** — leave a scene at the moment of maximum forward pull,
  not when the action resolves.
- **When to compress time** — summarize transitions; skip unneeded time.
- **When to jump location** — cut directly to the next meaningful
  location without travelogue.
- **When to switch POV** — change POV at a scene break only when the new
  POV adds information control or emotional perspective the prior POV
  couldn't provide.
- **When to preserve continuity** — carry a sensory detail, an object,
  a phrase from one scene into the next to bind them.
- **Transition through image** — close on a specific image; open the
  next scene on a related image.
- **Transition through emotion** — close on an emotional state; open
  the next scene in a contrasting or echoing emotional state.
- **Transition through unanswered question** — close on a question;
  open the next scene with the answer or a deepening of the question.
- **Transition through consequence** — close on a decision; open the
  next scene with its consequence.
- **Transition through contrast** — close quiet, open loud (or vice
  versa); close intimate, open public.
- **Transition through temporal jump** — leap forward in time; let
  the reader infer what happened in the gap.

### Why it works
Transitions are where AI-generated fiction most often reads as "scenes
strung together." Strong transitions make the book *flow* — each scene
feels caused by the prior, each chapter feels inevitable.

### How to apply
1. End each scene on a forward-pull element (image, question, decision,
   consequence), not on resolution.
2. Open the next scene with a transition that binds (image, emotion,
   question, consequence, contrast).
3. Use time-compression to skip unneeded transitions; use temporal
   jumps to create mystery (what happened in the gap?).
4. Switch POV only when the new POV adds something the prior couldn't.
5. Carry sensory details, objects, or phrases across scenes as
   binding motifs.

### When especially useful
Multi-scene chapters; multi-POV books; books with time-jumps; serialized
fiction where chapter-to-chapter flow is essential.

### When not to force
A clean cut is sometimes stronger than a clever transition. Do not
over-bind; some scenes deserve to stand alone.

### Common failure
AI-generated fiction often produces "scene, scene, scene" with no
binding tissue — each scene exists in isolation, summarized by a
transition that explains rather than flows. The result reads as
episodic rather than novelistic.

### Diagnostic questions
- Does each scene transition bind to the next (image, emotion,
  question, consequence, contrast)?
- Are time-compressions and temporal jumps deliberate?
- Does the book *flow*, or does it read as isolated scenes?
- Are POV switches motivated by information-control needs?

## PART VI — OPTIONAL ADVANCED TECHNIQUES (L3)

*These are specialized devices. They worked in particular source works
but should never be forced into every story. Evidence tags indicate
strength.*

### Character / POV techniques
- **Absent/dead character as narrative presence** — construct through
  others' memories, belongings, letters, narrator's dread; never let
  them speak directly. *Evidence: Rebecca [E2].*
- **Protagonist refusal-to-arc** — the character is *revealed*, not
  transformed; the tragedy is the refusal. Requires escalating cost.
  *Evidence: GWTW (Scarlett) [E2].*
- **Narrator unreliability through misreading** — the narrator is wrong
  (not lying); the reader follows the misreading until the reveal.
  *Evidence: Rebecca [E2].*
- **Polished-performer-as-unreliable-narrator** — voice deliberately
  too polished signals performance; unreliability is felt, not stated.
  *Evidence: Evelyn Hugo [E1].*
- **Voice-change as character arc** — the narrator's voice *transforms*
  across the book (hardens, softens, simplifies, complicates). Design
  the prose to change. *Evidence: Rebecca [E2].*
- **Self-talk mantra as interiority device** — a character-specific
  recurring interior phrase (mantra, counting, prayer) doubles as
  characterization and pacing. *Evidence: Fourth Wing (Violet's "I
  will not die today") [E1].*
- **Wry-revising-narrator voice signature** — interior monologue
  interrupting own dialogue mid-exchange. Contemporary-rom-com voice
  signature. *Evidence: Book Lovers (Nora) [E1].*
- **Omniscient narrator moral commentary at epic scale** — works when
  commentary is character-revealing, not authorial-preachy. *Evidence:
  GWTW [E1].*

### Romance techniques
- **Intimacy-as-perceptual-change** — physical intimacy that produces a
  perceptual shift (new senses, new awareness) doubles as romance-engine
  and plot-tool. *Evidence: ACOTAR (the eyelid-kiss producing enhanced
  senses) [E1].*
- **Survival competence as attraction engine** — witnessed survival-
  competence (the protagonist's refusal to break) as the love-interest's
  admiration engine. *Evidence: GWTW (Rhett→Scarlett) [E1].*
- **Romance-as-moral-inversion** — love deepening through complicity in
  wrongdoing. High-risk, high-reward. Requires sustained craft to avoid
  reading as apologia. *Evidence: Rebecca [E1].*
- **Long-arc separation-reunion across decades** — separation-reunion
  as *structure* (not the third-act-breakup failure mode), each
  separation caused by *real choice*. *Evidence: Evelyn Hugo (Evelyn-
  Celia) [E1].*
- **Contract-as-structure device** — the romance arc literally
  negotiated via a written contract; makes boundary-shifts explicit.
  *Evidence: Fifty Shades [E1].*
- **Abstinence-as-causally-tied-stakes** — physical withholding caused
  by world-rule or character-contradiction, not imposed arbitrarily.
  *Evidence: Twilight (Edward's temptation-to-kill-Bella) [E1].*
- **Series-arc setup via morally-grey secondary lead** — seed the
  alternative love interest in book 1 with enough moral complexity to
  be a credible partner later; do not foreground. *Evidence: ACOTAR
  (Rhysand) [E1].*
- **Meta-genre awareness** — a protagonist who knows the trope she's
  inside and performs against it. Generates originality through self-
  awareness without abandoning genre promise. *Evidence: Book Lovers
  (Nora) [E1].*

### Plot / structure techniques
- **Structural-as-thematic-craft** — chapter structure (one part per
  husband/decision/identity/year) embodies theme in form. *Evidence:
  Evelyn Hugo [E1].*
- **Frame-recontextualizing final twist** — the final twist
  recontextualizes the *frame* of the entire book, not just the story.
  *Evidence: Evelyn Hugo [E1].*
- **Moral-center character's death as recontextualizing twist** — the
  quiet moral-center character's death reorganizes the book, provided
  their quiet centrality was load-bearing. *Evidence: GWTW (Melanie)
  [E1].*
- **Curse/hidden-rule-as-recontextualizing twist** — a hidden world-rule
  that recontextualizes the entire book. The fantasy equivalent of the
  gothic unreliable-narrator twist. *Evidence: ACOTAR [E1].*
- **Preface-as-frame** — a Preface that foreshadows the climax creates
  a frame-promise the book fulfills. *Evidence: Twilight [E1].*
- **Triple-purpose detail** — a detail that characterizes, advances
  plot, and serves romance simultaneously. *Evidence: ACOTAR (Feyre's
  painting/observation skill) [E1].*
- **Bisexual-identity-as-structural-secret** — the central secret
  structures the book silently; the reader discovers the architecture
  rather than being told it. *Evidence: Evelyn Hugo [E1].*

### Prose / world techniques
- **Setting-as-character through character-filtered perception** — the
  POV perceives the setting through their specific lens (Bella's
  Phoenix-origin hyperawareness of Forks' weather). *Evidence: Twilight
  [E1].*
- **Atmospheric dread via selective detail** — the rusted gate, the
  empty lodge, the absence of smoke carry more dread than any
  description of dread. Gothic-atmosphere benchmark. *Evidence: Rebecca
  [E2].*
- **Restraint as engine (autumnal tone)** — name less to imply more;
  regret-as-engine; restrained longing. *Evidence: Persuasion, Rebecca
  [E3].*
- **Free indirect discourse** — the narrator's grammar adopts the
  character's diction without attribution; merges narrator and
  character consciousness without explicit thought-reporting. One
  powerful mode among several. *Evidence: Austen, Forster [E3].*

## PART VII — AI FAILURE / ANTI-GENERICITY FRAMEWORK

*Separated by evidence strength. Do not treat low-confidence claims as
equivalent to empirically supported findings.*

### A. Empirically supported tendencies (E4)
Drawn from peer-reviewed/preprint research (Moon et al. 2025; Anderson
et al. 2024; Doshi & Hauser 2024; Matsui et al. 2025). **These findings
are model- and generation-specific and may shift as models evolve; they
live in the Evidence Layer and should inform thinking, not become
timeless rules.**

- **Lower lexical diversity (MTLD)** — AI text uses a narrower active
  vocabulary within a passage. *Mitigation:* increase specificity
  (concrete nouns/verbs); increase deliberate variation.
- **Higher lexical density but lower diversity** — dense but repetitive.
  *Mitigation:* vary word choice deliberately.
- **Predictable collocations** — AI gravitates toward statistically-
  frequent word pairings ("a rich tapestry," "delve into," "navigate
  the complexities of"). *Mitigation:* vary; prefer concrete verbs.
- **Vocabulary drift** — LLM-favored words (delve, tapestry, realm,
  meticulous, navigate, resonate, underscore) increased in published
  writing post-LLM. *Mitigation:* awareness, not banned-word lists.
- **Idea homogenization** — each additional AI essay contributes fewer
  new ideas than each additional human essay; diversity gap widens at
  scale. *Mitigation:* inject unusual combinations, cultural specificity,
  conflicting values, distinctive psychology.
- **Formal, structured, positive, motivational register** — AI text
  tends toward formal/positive/motivational; human texts vary more in
  length and exhibit more negative/hedged affect. *Mitigation:* allow
  negative/hedged/uneven affect; vary register.

### B. Cross-book observations (E2/E3)
Drawn from corpus analysis (10 PD + 8 user-provided full-text works):

- **Emotion-vocabulary over-explanation** — naming emotions where
  stronger writing renders perception. *Mitigation:* render the
  perception; cut the label.
- **Physiological-cliché overuse in heat scenes** — "heart pounding,"
  "breath hitching," "stomach flipping," "hummingbird inside my chest."
  *Mitigation:* replace with character-specific perception.
- **Repetitive sentence structure** — uniform SVO; pronoun-fronted
  chains; excessive symmetry/parallelism. *Mitigation:* vary openings,
  syntax, rhythm; allow fragments and run-ons.
- **Generic sensory detail** — "warm sun," "gentle breeze," "scent of
  pine." *Mitigation:* character-filtered selective detail.
- **Adjective stacking / metaphor stacking** — *Mitigation:* convert
  to diagnostic heuristic (audit, don't mechanically delete).
- **Exposition disguised as dialogue** ("as you know"). *Mitigation:*
  dramatize; let information leak through behavior.
- **Characters too coherent, rational, consistent, articulate, agreeable.
  *Mitigation:* contradiction, hypocrisy, self-deception, blind spots,
  conflicting desires.
- **Tidy plots / single-track narratives / excessive causal neatness.**
  *Mitigation:* multi-track tension; meaningful subplots; allow messiness
  that is coherent.
- **Excessive thematic explanation.** *Mitigation:* embodied theme;
  trust the reader.
- **Constant medium intensity pacing.** *Mitigation:* intentional tempo
  contrast; recovery scenes.
- **Cliffhanger-per-chapter overuse** — retention-positive but rhythm-
  damaging. *Mitigation:* cliffhangers that *change the question*, not
  interrupt it.
- **Status-admiration as primary romance engine** — produces emotional
  thinness. *Mitigation:* admiration-of-witnessed-competence-or-moral-
  quality as primary engine.
- **Narrative homogenization** — statistical average of familiar genre
  (familiar archetypes, conflicts, metaphors, beats, endings, trope
  combinations). *Mitigation:* unusual combinations, cultural specificity,
  conflicting values, distinctive psychology.

### C. Model/genre-specific patterns (E1/E2)
Less generalizable; context-dependent:

- **Em-dash overuse** (specific to certain models/eras). *Mitigation:*
  audit; reserve em-dashes for interruption.
- **Balanced-triad overuse** ("not X, not Y, but Z"). *Mitigation:*
  audit; reserve for earned rhetorical effect.
- **Rhetorical-question-in-narration overuse.** *Mitigation:* audit.
- **Transition-cliché** ("However," "Moreover," "As the story unfolds,"
  "In that moment"). *Mitigation:* vary; let scenes bind via image/
  emotion rather than transition-words.
- **Genre-specific romantic-cliché** (morally-grey-as-marketing-label
  without moral complexity; fated-mates-as-substitute-for-attraction;
  third-act-breakup-by-miscommunication). *Mitigation:* execute with
  craft or avoid.

### D. Useful heuristics (cross-tier)
Practical counter-moves:

- Replace abstract emotion vocabulary with the perception that produces
  the emotion.
- Introduce character via specific observed behavior doing double duty.
- Use selective detail (character-filtered) instead of inventory.
- Vary sentence length deliberately; contract under threat, expand under
  reflection.
- Render tension through asymmetric physical/behavioral choices.
- Make twists recontextualize an established detail.
- Let dialogue withhold (subtext, asymmetry, evasion).
- Gate heat by relationship change, not page %.
- End chapters on a specific mystery image, not generic foreboding.
- Use meaningful recurring motifs that earn first-appearance existence.
- Revise diagnostically, not globally.
- Preserve rare/specific choices when revising (they are voice).
- Apply the Fiction Quality Score per chapter; diagnose-revise-recheck
  where weak (do not treat as a hard threshold).

### E. Internet folklore / low-confidence claims (do not elevate)
- **"AI detectors reliably identify AI text"** — false; detectors fail
  up to ~78% of the time. The Bible's quality score is NOT a detector
  prediction.
- **"Banned-word lists solve AI-genericity"** — false; mechanical
  deletion loses legitimate uses. Specificity and variation are the
  correctives.
- **"Readers can always identify AI writing"** — false; reader studies
  are confounded by fluency. The goal is *good fiction*, not "sounds
  human."
- **"AI writing is always bad writing"** — false; the failure modes are
  generic-weak-craft failures, not AI-specific. Many human-written
  bestsellers exhibit the same failures.

### The purpose of this framework
The purpose is NOT to evade AI detection. The purpose is to avoid
genericity, homogeneity, emotional over-explanation, predictable
narrative structure, excessive thematic explanation, weak character
agency, repetitive syntax, generic sensory detail, formulaic dialogue,
and artificial narrative neatness.

### E. Long-Range Diagnostics (PASS / FAIL / DEFERRED)

Some writing mechanisms cannot be reliably evaluated from the current
scene/chapter alone because their effectiveness depends on later payoff.
For these, use **PASS / FAIL / DEFERRED** rather than forcing a premature
judgment.

**DEFERRED** means: the current evidence is insufficient because the
necessary downstream payoff or consequence has not yet been written.
DEFERRED is neither failure nor success; it means *wait for more
narrative evidence*. Do not invent a hypothetical payoff merely to
complete the checklist.

#### Long-range categories

| Category | Evaluate the complete chain |
|---|---|
| Foreshadowing | SETUP → REVEAL → RECONTEXTUALIZATION → PAYOFF |
| Twists | SETUP → EXPECTATION → MISDIRECTION → REVEAL → RECONTEXTUALIZATION → CONSEQUENCE |
| Mystery | QUESTION → CLUE DISTRIBUTION → HYPOTHESES → MISDIRECTION → REVEAL → RETROSPECTIVE COHERENCE → SATISFACTION |
| Information control | INTENDED MODE → READER EXPERIENCE → REVEAL → EMOTIONAL EFFECT |
| Relationship setup | INITIAL DYNAMIC → PRESSURE → TURNING POINTS → CHANGE → NEW DYNAMIC |
| Character arc | INITIAL BELIEF/STATE → PRESSURE → SPECIFIC EXPERIENCES → INTERNAL RESPONSE → CHOICE → TRANSFORMATION/REFUSAL/REVELATION → NEW STATE |
| Recurring motifs | FIRST APPEARANCE → REPEAT → DEVELOPMENT → CHANGE OF MEANING → PAYOFF |
| Thematic architecture | THEMATIC QUESTION → CHARACTER CHOICES → PATTERNS → CONSEQUENCES → STRUCTURAL/SYMBOLIC PAYOFF |
| Frame narration | FRAME PROMISE → STORY → FRAME RECONTEXTUALIZATION → FINAL EFFECT |
| Long-range payoff / series setup | SETUP → DEVELOPMENT → PAYOFF → RETROSPECTIVE EFFECT |

For recurring motifs: do not require every motif to have a payoff, but
when a motif is clearly positioned as meaningful, evaluate whether the
later development justifies its earlier presence.

#### Four audit checkpoints

Rather than evaluating long-range mechanisms once, audit them at four
moments. Each produces its own result. The four checkpoints together
form the long-range decision model:

- **CHECKPOINT A** — What can we judge now?
- **CHECKPOINT B** — Is the mechanism developing coherently?
- **CHECKPOINT C** — Did the payoff actually work?
- **CHECKPOINT D** — Does the complete chain work retrospectively?

#### Three distinct judgments

The diagnostic system distinguishes three separate judgments that must
not be conflated:

- **SETUP QUALITY** — how well the setup functions at the moment it
  appears. Evaluated at Checkpoint A.
- **LONG-RANGE MECHANISM EFFECTIVENESS** — whether the later development
  and payoff successfully use the setup. Evaluated at Checkpoints B/C.
- **RETROSPECTIVE EFFECTIVENESS** — whether the completed chain becomes
  more meaningful when viewed as a whole. Evaluated at Checkpoint D.

These are separate judgments. A setup can: PASS locally but later FAIL
globally; NEED ATTENTION locally but later become successful after
development; or be DEFERRED because its long-range value cannot yet be
known. The system must clearly distinguish **NOT YET KNOWN** from
**RESOLVED BADLY**.

**CHECKPOINT A — SETUP AUDIT** *(performed when the mechanism is introduced)*

Evaluate:
- Is the setup natural in the current narrative?
- Does it serve a current function?
- Is the information appropriate for this moment?
- Is it overly conspicuous or artificially hidden?
- Is it viable enough to remain in the story and receive later
  development?

**IMPORTANT:**
- Do NOT judge the eventual payoff at this checkpoint.
- Do NOT assume that an understated setup is weak.
- Do NOT assume that an apparently minor detail needs to become important.
- At this checkpoint, distinguish **BAD NOW** from **UNKNOWN YET**.

**Result:** PASS / NEEDS ATTENTION / DEFERRED
(Use DEFERRED when the relevant judgment depends on future development.)

**Governing principle:** A weak-looking setup is not automatically a
failed setup. At Checkpoint A, evaluate the setup only on the evidence
currently available. A setup may be ordinary, understated, thin,
ambiguous, or apparently low-significance and still become highly
effective later. Do not judge long-range mechanism effectiveness before
the necessary development and payoff exist. When the future effectiveness
cannot yet be known, defer that judgment. Reassess at Checkpoint C and
Checkpoint D.

**CHECKPOINT B — DEVELOPMENT AUDIT** *(performed after meaningful development)*
- Is the mechanism developing coherently?
- Has the setup accumulated significance?
- Has the reader's interpretation evolved?
- Are expectations being shaped correctly?
- Result: PASS / FAIL / DEFERRED

**CHECKPOINT C — PAYOFF AUDIT** *(performed after the reveal/payoff exists)*
- Did the payoff actually work?
- Does the payoff make earlier material more meaningful?
- Does it feel earned?
- Does it create consequence?
- Does it produce the intended emotional effect?
- Does it recontextualize earlier information?
- Does it avoid feeling arbitrary?
- Result: PASS / FAIL

**CHECKPOINT D — RETROSPECTIVE AUDIT** *(performed after the relevant arc/chapter/act is complete)*
- Does the complete chain work retrospectively?
- Would rereading earlier scenes reveal additional meaning?
- Did the setup/payoff relationship strengthen the story?
- Result: PASS / FAIL

#### Governing rules

- **Do not confuse deliberate construction with successful execution.**
  Intent is evidence of design, not effectiveness.
- **Never assume a weak early setup becomes good simply because the
  author eventually references it.** The payoff must genuinely improve
  the narrative.
- **Never assume an early setup is bad merely because the payoff has not
  been written yet.**
- When marked DEFERRED, do not revise the setup solely because the
  payoff has not appeared. Instead, create a future audit checkpoint
  (B / C / D) and proceed.
- A mature QA system distinguishes: **BAD NOW** / **GOOD NOW** /
  **TOO EARLY TO KNOW**. The third category is essential. Do not force
  uncertainty into PASS or FAIL.

### Continuity log (maintain per chapter)

- What each major character now knows
- Where key objects are
- Relationship state changes
- World rules invoked

### Per-act audit
- Does every character act only on information they possess?
- Are timeline / chronology / object-locations consistent?
- Are world-rule invocations consistent with prior rules?
- Are relationship-state changes tracked?
- Are motivations consistent with established character?

### Per-act long-range audit (per Part VIII.E)
- For each active long-range mechanism (foreshadowing, twist, mystery,
  information control, relationship setup, character-arc setup, recurring
  motifs, thematic architecture, frame, series setup): record current
  STATUS — PASS / FAIL / DEFERRED — and which checkpoint (A/B/C/D) the
  mechanism is at.
- For any marked DEFERRED: log a future audit checkpoint. Do not revise
  the setup solely because the payoff has not appeared.
- For any newly reaching PAYOFF (Checkpoint C) or RETROSPECTIVE
  (Checkpoint D): run the corresponding audit before approving the act.

### Moral-complexity audit (per Core Principle 34)
- Distinguish **character morality** from **narrative moral framing**.
  A character who frames injustice as noble is not itself a failure;
  the failure is when the **narrative endorses** that framing without
  authorial awareness.
- Ask: does the narrative critically examine, complicate, endorse,
  or merely reproduce the framing of injustice?
- If the narrative **endorses or merely reproduces** an unjust framing
  without awareness, the moral-complexity axis has inverted into
  moral failure. Revise. Craft excellence does not redeem moral
  failure.
- An unreliable narrator framing injustice as noble is a valid
  technique; the narrative must signal the unreliability or complicate
  the framing at some level.

## PART XI — MASTER WRITING DECISION HIERARCHY

*Before making a writing decision, reason in this order. The final
question overrides lower-priority heuristics.*

1. What is this story trying to make the reader feel?
2. What does this character want right now?
3. What does the character believe?
4. What do they misunderstand?
5. What do they notice?
6. What would this specific person choose?
7. What consequence follows?
8. How does the choice change the relationship or stakes?
9. What does the reader know?
10. What should the reader infer?
11. What narrative function does the scene need?
12. What prose choice best expresses the character and moment?
13. Which craft principle is useful here?
14. **Is any rule being followed at the expense of believable fiction?**
15. **For long-range mechanisms (foreshadowing, twist, mystery, character
    arc, relationship arc, thematic architecture, recurring motif,
    frame, series setup): can this be reliably evaluated now, or is it
    DEFERRED until later payoff exists? If DEFERRED, log the checkpoint
    and proceed; do not force a premature PASS/FAIL.**

If the answer to #14 is yes, override the rule. Rules are tools, not
laws.

If the answer to #15 is DEFERRED, do not revise the setup solely
because the payoff has not appeared; schedule a future audit checkpoint
(B / C / D) per Part VIII.E.

## PART XII — FINAL QUALITY GATE (compact)

Before approving a chapter, confirm:

### Critical (must pass)
- [ ] No continuity contradictions.
- [ ] POV is consistent within scene (no head-hops).
- [ ] All major motivations are earned.
- [ ] Genre promise is delivered.
- [ ] All twists that have reached payoff are setup + causal + consequential.
  (Twists whose payoff has not yet been written are DEFERRED, not failed
  — see Part VIII.E.)
- [ ] Chronology is coherent.
- [ ] Characters act only on information they possess.
- [ ] No accidental story contradictions.
- [ ] Moral-complexity axis has not inverted (no injustice framed as
  noble without authorial awareness).

### Diagnostic (investigate weak areas; do not treat as hard threshold)
- [ ] Prose rhythm varied intentionally?
- [ ] Descriptions selective / character-filtered?
- [ ] Vocabulary specific; metaphors earned?
- [ ] Emotional vocabulary audited (perception over labels)?
- [ ] Dialogue voices differentiated; subtext active?
- [ ] Characters contradictory; agency present?
- [ ] Plot consequences meaningful; motivations competing?
- [ ] Subplots performing functions?
- [ ] Pacing varied via intentional contrast?
- [ ] Twists recontextualize? *(If payoff not yet written: DEFERRED.)*
- [ ] Originality present (unusual combinations / cultural specificity /
  distinctive psychology)?
- [ ] AI-genericity risk investigated if high?

### Long-range (per Part VIII.E — do not force PASS/FAIL prematurely)
- [ ] For each active long-range mechanism: STATUS recorded (PASS / FAIL /
  DEFERRED) at the appropriate checkpoint (A / B / C / D)?
- [ ] For any newly reaching payoff (Checkpoint C) or retrospective
  completion (Checkpoint D): audit run before approving?

### Final override
If any rule was followed at the expense of believable fiction, revise.
The goal is good fiction — specific, intentional, emotionally complex,
character-driven, immersive, surprising, commercial, literary, original.
