# CHAPTER 17: MOTHER

Kev read Nyx's messages three times, then handed the phone back to Lena and said, "Don't answer."

It was the morning after Rosie's birthday. The five of them sat around Viper's dining table while the coffee went cold in their cups. Kev had traced the crescent-moon signature through four relay servers before it vanished at a hub in Reykjavik, the same Reykjavik hub he was using to hide all of them.

"This person knows how to use the same tunnels I do," Kev said. "Or uses them better."

"She knew Thomas," Lena said. "There's an N in Corwin's notebook. *Ask N about the Island Station.* I thought N was just an ordinary source."

"Ten years," Viper said, reading the message again. "Ten years helping a cop. A hacker helping a cop." He scratched his beard. "Sounds like a movie."

"Or like someone who sells information to whoever pays more," Kev said.

Nobody argued with that. Lena looked at the last line on the screen, at the word *Maren* typed out so calmly, as if the person typing had been calling her that for a very long time, and she felt stripped bare. Somewhere out there was someone who knew her real name before she did.

Elias sat at the far end of the table and hadn't said a word all through breakfast. Last night she had seen him standing at the kitchen window, but by the time she came in he'd already gone upstairs before she could say anything. This morning he looked like he hadn't slept. Maybe she looked the same.

"I want to dive," she said.

Four heads turned toward her.

"Into my memories," she went on, before anyone could ask. "Not Elias's. Mine. In the last three dives, everything we found came through his brain, and Layer 4 tore his brain up. What he remembers is real, but it's in pieces. My memories are intact. They're just locked."

"Locked by your own brain," Kev said. "Defense mechanism. It locked them for a reason."

"I know what the reason is." Lena put down her coffee. "I've examined more than four hundred cases of trauma-sealed memory. I know exactly what happens when you open one too fast, and I know how to open one slowly." She paused. "There's a woman out there calling me by a name I don't remember. I'm not going to let her know more about me than I do."

Viper looked at Kev. Kev looked at Elias.

"Who's guiding you?" Elias asked. It was the first thing he'd said all morning.

Lena looked at him. "You are."

---

She had written him a list by hand on the back of a pizza delivery menu Viper kept stuck to the fridge.

*1. Heart rate over 130 → say "up," don't wait.*
*2. If I talk in a language you don't understand → normal, ignore it.*
*3. If I cry → normal, ignore it.*
*4. If I don't answer for more than 20 seconds → inject (LEFT vial, NOT the right one).*
*5. Don't ask "what do you see." Ask "where are you."*

Elias read it twice, then folded it and tucked it into his shirt pocket.

"Why can't I ask what you see?" he asked.

"Because that question suggests there's something to see," she said, settling back into the armchair. "The brain always wants to please whoever's asking. Ask me what I see, and it'll build something for me to look at." She put the sensor ring on her own head and turned the knob, and heard the six familiar clicks from a side she had never sat on. "*Where are you* doesn't suggest anything."

"Where are you," he murmured, like he was memorizing it.

He put his hand on the switch, the hand with the two stitches she had put in herself, small, perfectly even black stitches. Looking at it, she thought, for no reason at all, that it was the best suturing she had ever done, and she'd done it in a laundromat.

"Layer 2 in three," Elias said. His voice was lower than usual, slower, and she realized he was copying the voice she used when she guided him. "Two. One."

---

She knew the theory by heart. She had lectured on it, written on it, testified about it in court. A sealed memory isn't like a locked room. It's like a room with all the light drained out. The furniture's still there. You can still walk in. But you can't see anything until something lights a corner.

What lit it was the melody.

She heard it before she saw anything. A low, rough voice singing in Ladino, a language she didn't know she knew, until the words floated by and she understood them. *Nana, niña, nana. Snow is falling on the roof. Sleep, my girl, the snow will keep you warm.* Such a strange lullaby. Snow never kept anybody warm. Her mother had explained once that in her grandmother's village, when the snow fell deep, nobody had to go out to the fields, and the whole family got to stay inside together. Snow meant being together.

*Her mother had explained once.* Lena heard the thought come up in her head and didn't know where it had come from.

"Where are you?" Elias's voice, far away, outside.

"In bed," she said. Her mouth said it in the outside world, and she heard her voice come out distorted, smaller. "An iron bed. There's a whale on the wall. I drew it."

Light flowed into the room like water poured into a glass. Room One, white walls, the flying whale in blue crayon taped above the bed, a water stain on the ceiling shaped like Italy that the girl used to stare at when she couldn't sleep, a feather pillow that the girl knew, somehow, didn't belong to the institute. Her mother had brought it from home.

Her mother sat on the edge of the bed, singing.

Lena looked at her face. She had seen this face through Elias's eyes, smeared with ash, in firelight. Now it was clean and tired. About forty, little lines at the corners of the eyes, the kind you get from squinting at documents, her hair pinned up with a pencil, a coffee stain on the cuff of her lab coat, her nails bitten short. Dr. Elena Solis bit her nails. No file anywhere said that.

The song ended, but Elena didn't get up. She sat there with one hand still on the blanket, looking at the left wall, the wall shared with Room Zero. On the other side came the faint rustle of a child turning over.

"Mamá," the girl said. Or Lena said it. She couldn't tell anymore.

"Mm."

"Why are you sad?"

Elena turned and smiled, but the smile didn't reach her eyes. Lena knew that smile. She had seen it in the mirror every time Nia asked if she was okay.

"I'm not sad," Elena said. "Just tired."

"Did Miss Iris yell at you again?"

The hand on the blanket tightened.

"You heard?"

"In the hallway, when I was coming back from tests." The girl sat up and hugged her pillow. "She called you *soft*. I don't know what soft means, but she said it like a swear word."

Elena didn't answer right away. She looked at her daughter for a long time, the look of someone deciding whether to lie or tell the truth and finding both choices equally bad.

"Miss Iris wants me to do something," she said at last. "With you. And with Eli too. Something I think will hurt you both. I said no."

"So what did she do?"

"She said if I don't do it, someone else will, and someone else won't be as careful as me." Elena leaned down and pressed her forehead to her daughter's. "So I'm staying here. To make sure someone is careful."

The girl didn't understand, but Lena did.

This was Appendix C, the ultimatum she had read by the light of the moon lamp: *either stay on and keep working, or your daughter will be admitted to the program under institutional supervision.* But that sheet of paper hadn't recorded this. It didn't record that a woman sat on the edge of her daughter's bed every night, singing a song about snow, so that two children could sleep in a place she herself was helping to build. It didn't record that she stayed because she didn't trust anyone else to do this to her child. It didn't record that she was wrong.

Because staying hadn't saved anyone. She was careful, and every day Maren was still taken downstairs. She was careful, and the boy on the other side of the wall still cried his first night. She was careful for two years, and then one night she stopped being careful, carried in a canister of incendiary catalyst, and...

"Lena." Elias's voice, closer. "Heart rate one twenty-five. Where are you?"

She didn't answer. The memory was slipping away like film pulled out of a projector. The room went dark. She heard sirens. She smelled smoke.

*No. Not yet. Not that night.* She didn't want to see that night. Not now. Not alone.

But memory doesn't ask permission.

---

Cold. Cold so deep it stopped being cold and became pressure, like someone had pressed sheets of metal against every inch of the girl's body.

Water. She was in water up to her chest, a black creek running fast, smelling of mud and engine oil. Both hands were clamped around a tree root sticking out of the bank, her fingernails broken, and she didn't feel any of it.

Behind her, far away, the sky was orange-red, and fire sirens came and went on the wind.

The girl wasn't crying. She had cried herself out inside the vent. She just held on to the root and looked toward the orange sky and called, hoarse, without any sound: *Eli. Eli. Eli.*

He didn't come out. She had crawled out of the end of the shaft, rolled down the slope, fallen into the creek, and then turned around to look, and waited. He didn't come out.

A flashlight beam swept along the bank.

The girl curled in on herself. The institute's people, dressed in black, with guns, come to take her back. She let go of the root with one hand and tried to sink under the water, but the other hand wouldn't let go. The fingers had frozen in place.

The light stopped on her face.

"Jesus," a man's voice said. It was a stranger's voice, not like anyone at the institute, rough, with a little Southern in it, and full of alarm. "Jesus, it's a kid."

A man slid down the bank without a second's hesitation and waded in up to his waist. He was wearing a yellow police raincoat, the kind with reflective stripes on the sleeves. He worked her fingers loose from the root one at a time, gently, finger by finger, like untangling a knotted string. Then he picked her up, held her tight against his chest, took off the raincoat, and wrapped it around her, even though it was still raining and the clothes under it were already soaked.

"You're okay," he said. "I've got you. You're okay now."

She looked up at his face: stubble, brown eyes, a small scar on his chin. On his chest, under where the raincoat had been, there was a metal name tag: *T. DALY.*

"What's your name, sweetheart?" he asked.

She opened her mouth to answer, then remembered the men in black, remembered the institute, remembered what her mother had told her. *If you get outside, don't tell anyone your name. No one. Not until you're sure.*

She shut her mouth.

The man looked at her one second longer. He looked at the soaked white cotton clothes with a *1* printed on the chest, at the bruise from the blood draws in the crook of her elbow, at the orange sky where the sirens were. And Lena, twenty years later, saw the moment his face changed, the moment a police officer understood that the child in his arms was not the victim of some chemical warehouse fire. The moment he decided.

He pulled the edge of the raincoat over the number on her chest.

"That's okay," he said, more quietly. "You don't have to tell me yet. I won't ask again."

He carried her up the bank and walked away from the orange sky, toward a patrol car parked at the bend of the dirt road, away from the fire trucks, away from the men in black.

---

"Lena. Up. Come up."

She opened her eyes. Viper's living room, curtains drawn. Elias was kneeling in front of the chair, an injector in one hand, the left vial, she saw, his other hand on her shoulder. His face was white.

"Twenty-two seconds," he said. "You didn't answer for twenty-two seconds."

"You didn't inject."

"I was going to count to twenty-five." He set the injector down. His hand was still shaking. "The list said twenty. I broke the rules. I'm sorry."

She meant to say something about following protocol, but it wouldn't come out.

She sat up and took off the sensor ring. Her hair was plastered to her forehead with sweat, and she was cold all over, the exact cold of a creek in October, even though the living room was warm and the sun was out.

Viper stood in the kitchen doorway. Linh sat on the stairs hugging her knees. Kev stood behind Linh holding his phone, a number half dialed.

"What did you see?" Linh asked, then clapped a hand over her mouth. "Sorry. Banned question."

Lena looked at her fingers. Not one nail was broken.

"Thomas," she said. "He found me in a creek. He didn't carry me toward the fire trucks."

Nobody spoke.

She thought about the first memory of her life, the one she had always believed was first: a pale green hospital room, Thomas sitting by the bed peeling an apple, one long unbroken spiral of peel, then handing her the apple and the curled red peel like a snake. She had kept that peel until it shriveled up, and Margaret had to trick her for ages before she'd throw it away. For twenty years she had thought that was the beginning. It was the second day.

Lena got up, went into the kitchen, and opened the refrigerator. There were three apples in the crisper. She took one, got the paring knife from the drawer, sat down at the table, and started to peel.

On the third turn, the peel broke. She kept going.
