# THE NAME THAT SURVIVED
## CHAPTER 6: THE INSTITUTIONAL VICE (FULL NOVEL EDITION)

The morning sun broke through the bruised, low-hanging storm clouds like a pale, diseased eye, casting long, leaden shadows across the polished granite foyer of St. Jude Medical Plaza.

Maren arrived at seven-fifteen, her trench coat damp from the walk across the plaza, her eyes burning with the raw, gritty sting of a sleepless night. 

She carried a leather portfolio beneath her arm containing the digitized coroner’s report from October twenty years ago—the official document that declared Elena Solis dead from accidental inhalation of toxic combustion products. For twenty years, that document had been the cornerstone of her grief. Now, after seeing her mother’s living face burning in the holographic beam of Elias’s mind, the paper felt like a poisoned forgery.

As she stepped out of the elevator onto the fourth floor, she stopped dead.

Two men in dark charcoal wool suits stood flanked on either side of her office door. They were broad-shouldered, athletic, wearing wireless earpieces with translucent acoustic coils coiling down the backs of their necks. Their posture was relaxed but coiled, their hands clasped loosely in front of their belts—the universal body language of corporate executive protection details.

Between them stood Dr. Iris Halloway.

Halloway was in her late fifties, though her skin possessed the tight, poreless perfection of someone who underwent regular cellular rejuvenation therapies. She was dressed in an impeccably tailored navy wool coat over an ivory silk blouse, her steel-gray hair swept back into a severe, architectural bun secured with tortoiseshell pins. She wore no jewelry except for a discreet platinum lapel pin shaped like an interlocking double helix—the corporate insignia of Halloway Neuro-Systems.

Her eyes were a pale, crystalline blue—sharp, intelligent, and entirely devoid of human warmth.

"Dr. Solis," Halloway said, her voice smooth, cultured, carrying the effortless, commanding cadence of a woman who controlled a three-billion-dollar endowment and dined with federal cabinet secretaries. "I apologize for the unannounced intrusion at such an ungodly hour. But exceptional circumstances occasionally demand an abandonment of social etiquette."

Maren kept her grip firm on her leather portfolio. She did not extend a hand. She did not offer a smile.

"Dr. Halloway," Maren said, her voice cool and steady. "If you are seeking a forensic consultation, my office hours begin at nine. My receptionist handles intake appointments."

Halloway offered a thin, patronizing smile that did not reach her pale eyes. "This is not a consultation, Maren. May I call you Maren? I knew your mother quite well, after all. We were colleagues during the foundational years of the neuro-cognitive revolution."

"My mother died twenty years ago," Maren replied, her jaw tightening. "And according to her journals, she did not consider you a friend."

Halloway’s smile remained fixed, but the air in the carpeted hallway grew distinctly colder.

"Elena was a brilliant biochemist, but she was prone to emotional volatility," Halloway said smoothly, stepping forward until she stood barely two paces from Maren. "She had a profound vision for human evolution, but she lacked the operational discipline required to bring that vision to fruition. She let sentimental attachments cloud her scientific objectivity."

"Is that what you call a mother trying to protect children from illegal experimentation?" Maren asked, her voice dropping into a dangerous, icy whisper. "A sentimental attachment?"

For a fraction of a second, the polished mask slipped. 

The warmth in Halloway’s aristocratic posture vanished, revealing something cold, calculating, and predatory underneath. The pale blue eyes narrowed, assessing Maren not as a grieving daughter or a medical colleague, but as an operational liability that required immediate containment.

"I understand you conducted an unauthorized exploratory scan on one of our clinical subjects last evening," Halloway said, her tone shifting seamlessly from polite condescension to corporate authority. "A man named Elias Vane."

"Mr. Vane presented as a private walk-in seeking a baseline cognitive assessment," Maren replied. "Under state medical privacy statutes, the telemetry and diagnostic records generated in that session are privileged doctor-patient communications."

"Elias Vane is legally classified as an inpatient under active post-operative psychiatric observation," Halloway countered, her voice dropping into the rhythmic precision of a courtroom prosecutor. "He suffered an acute temporal-lobe seizure during a classified quantum synchronization trial eleven days ago. His cognitive faculties are severely compromised. He suffers from acute autobiographical confabulation, dissociative fugue, and dangerous paranoid delusions."

"It didn't look like confabulation to me, Dr. Halloway," Maren said, taking a deliberate step forward, refusing to be intimidated. "His neural telemetry displayed authentic episodic trauma encoding. First-person sensory data matching historical events from twenty years ago. Sensory data that belongs to my mother."

Halloway’s left hand, gloved in soft black leather, made a slight, dismissive gesture. 

One of the charcoal-suited security operators stepped forward, pulling a heavy, cream-colored legal envelope stamped with a crimson federal seal from his breast pocket. He held it out to Maren.

"This is a federal writ of administrative seizure issued under Title 14 of the National Critical Technology Security Act," Halloway said, her voice dropping into a flat, deadly register. "It orders the immediate impoundment and transfer of all raw telemetry, visual decodings, and diagnostic logs recorded in your facility over the past twenty-four hours."

Maren looked at the envelope but did not take it. "This clinic is an independent forensic practice. You have no jurisdiction here."

"The writ is signed by the Chief Judge of the Federal Circuit Court of Appeals," Halloway said softly. "You have until twelve hundred hours to upload the raw data to our secure server. If you refuse, your clinical license will be revoked by the State Medical Board before three o'clock. By five o'clock, this building will be seized by federal marshals, and you will be remanded into custody as an accessory to the unlawful possession of classified defense technology."

Halloway turned, the hem of her navy coat sweeping in an elegant arc. She walked toward the elevator bank, her leather heels clicking against the granite floor with the slow, rhythmic cadence of a metronome counting down to an execution.

Just before the elevator doors slid open, Halloway paused and looked back over her shoulder.

"Do not throw your life away for a ghost, Maren," she said softly. "Your mother made that choice. It would be a profound tragedy if her daughter proved to be equally stubborn."

The elevator chimed, and Halloway stepped inside, flanked by her two security shadows. The doors slid shut.

Maren stood in the empty corridor, the heavy legal envelope resting against the brass lock of her office door where the guard had left it. Her hands were shaking with an intoxicating mixture of adrenaline and cold, furious rage.

She shoved her key into the lock, pushed the door open, and walked straight to her desk.

She picked up the encrypted landline, picked up the slip of paper Elias had left on her desk the previous night, and dialed his unlisted burner number.

The line clicked on the second ring.

"Elias," Maren said, her voice trembling with absolute, lethal resolve. "Get to my office right now. Bring your storage drives. Bring everything you have."

"I'm already in the subway station across the street," Elias’s raspy voice responded. "I'm coming up."
