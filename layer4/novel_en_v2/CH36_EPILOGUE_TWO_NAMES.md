# CHAPTER 36: EPILOGUE — TWO NAMES

At the Island Station, nobody said *night shift*, because down here there was no night.

The island was just a bare lump of granite sticking up out of the sea, wrapped in fog all year round, fog so thick the Coast Guard could pass it a dozen times without ever seeing the pier with the naked eye. Above ground there was only a weather station, an antenna mast, and a rusty sign bearing the name of an ecological research foundation. Everything else was under the rock. The lights down there were always the same level, white and even, twenty-four hours a day. People worked twelve-hour shifts, and after about three months nobody remembered what season it was outside.

At 4:17 a.m. on October eighteenth, system time, technician Dana Ruiz was eating a packet of cheese crackers at the Section C duty desk when the screen in front of her chimed.

It was a small, polite chime, like an elevator bell. In fourteen months working here she had heard it exactly once, at her first training session, when the instructor played it for her and said, *If you ever hear this and it isn't a drill, call upstairs right away. Don't wait.*

She put the crackers down.

> `[MARK-IV POD / NO. 02]`
> `[SUBJECT: DALY, C.]`
> `[CONNECTION TO CENTRAL SERVER LOST: 77 HOURS]`
> `[NEURAL SUPPRESSION: NO LONGER MAINTAINED]`
> `[CONSCIOUS ACTIVITY: RISING]`

Dana read the third line again.

For three days the central server had been silent. No new commands, no updates, nothing. The supervisor on the shift before hers said upstairs was doing maintenance. The supply boat that should have come the day before yesterday still hadn't shown up, and the station chief had been locked in his office with the satellite phone for two days. Nobody explained anything to the technicians. And down here, nobody asked.

She picked up the internal phone and called the chief's office. It rang twelve times. Nobody answered.

She put the phone down and looked through the observation window.

Section C had three pods. Pod One was empty. Pod Three had been empty since before she arrived. Pod Two was not.

Behind the frosted glass of Pod Two, submerged in a layer of clear, faintly blue gel, lay a broad-shouldered man with eight months of beard nobody had shaved. Surgical scars ran diagonally across his left ribs and right shoulder, marks from the night they pulled him out of the sea, after his little boat was rammed and broken apart by one of the station's patrol boats off the northern reef.

He had arrived six months after she did. Every day since then she had read his brainwaves, logged them, sent the reports upstairs. She knew his Theta rhythm the way she knew her own heartbeat. She knew that whenever he thought of certain words, the line would jump a little, and she had to log every single time, because *she* wanted to know.

Except for *Daly, C.* on the screen, Dana had never known his name.

Inside the pod, the man's right index finger moved.

Dana stood up.

---

He didn't know who he was.

That was the first thought, and it wasn't as frightening as he would have expected. It was just a fact, like the fact that he was cold, like the fact that there was something hard and dry in his throat, pushing air into his lungs and then drawing it out again.

There was light, white and even, behind a sheet of frosted glass right in front of his face, close enough that it was only a hand's width away.

He tried to move his fingers. They were heavy as lead, but they obeyed.

He tried to remember.

There was nothing. Just a white wall, a deep, deep pit. He reached down to the bottom of the pit and touched only water. Cold, salty, black. An engine roaring. An impact. Water rushing into his mouth.

And then, at the very bottom, something small and warm stuck to the tip of his finger, like a grain of sand clinging to skin.

A kitchen. Morning sun through blue gingham curtains. A pot of basil, half dead, on the windowsill. A woman standing with her back to him, dark hair in a loose knot, wearing an oversized gray sweater. She turned around holding a mug of coffee, and smiled.

He didn't know who she was. But he knew her name.

He knew it the way he knew he was breathing. It was right there in the middle of his chest, heavy and warm like a pebble in a child's fist, the thing the child absolutely refuses to let go of.

His lips were dry and cracked and stuck to the breathing tube. He tried to move them. His breath fogged the inside of the glass.

*"Lena..."*

Outside the glass, beside the pod, something moved. He couldn't turn his head. He could only see it at the edge of his vision: a small black animal about the size of a cat, giving off a warm, dim glow, like a night-light. It was curled up right at the foot of the pod, its chin resting on its front paws, watching him.

He didn't know what it was, or where it had come from, or how long it had been lying there.

It blinked once, very slowly, the way a cat blinks at someone it trusts.

---

Two and a half thousand miles away, in Seattle, it was raining, and the clock said only 1:17 in the morning.

In a fifth-floor loft in an old warehouse in Georgetown, a woman sat in front of six monitors. She was about forty-five, her short hair half gone gray, wearing round wire-rimmed glasses and a long-sleeved sweater worn through at both elbows. Next to the keyboard sat a mug of green tea gone completely cold and a half-used blister pack of nicotine gum. A fat tabby cat lay sprawled across the keyboard, sound asleep, its tail twitching in front of the center screen.

"Hamish," the woman said. "Hamish, up."

The cat didn't get up. She lifted him and set him aside, and looked at the screen.

> `[ISLAND STATION / SECTION C / POD 02]`
> `[DALY, C.: CONSCIOUSNESS SURFACED]`
> `[SPONTANEOUS RESPIRATION: YES]`
> `[FIRST KEYWORD LOGGED: "LENA"]`

She read the last line, then read it again.

Twelve years ago, three weeks before he died, Thomas Daly had given her a string of characters handwritten on the back of a dry-cleaning receipt. He said it was a back door into the old Prometheus network, in case she ever needed it. That door had been dead since 2010, when the relay station at Point Lookout Light was abandoned. She had kept the receipt in her wallet for twelve years anyway, the way you keep the key to a house that has been torn down. Then a bunch of kids had woken up the cable under that lighthouse again, and three nights ago, when Halloway was thrown out of her own system, every door she had locked flew open.

Including the doors that led out to an island in the fog.

For three days she had sat watching that pod, watching the numbers, not daring to believe it, not daring not to. She knew exactly what hope cost. She had paid it once already, in 2012, when she heard that a car had gone into a river.

Rain tapped against the big window that looked out on the cranes of the Port of Seattle, the red lights on top of them blinking through the rain.

Then she smiled. A small, crooked smile, pulled to one side, the smile of someone who has lost a great many hands of cards and has finally won one.

"Tom," she said to the empty room. "The boy's alive."

Hamish opened one eye, looked at her, and closed it again.

She reached for the satellite phone on the desk, the untraceable kind, the kind she had used exactly three times in twelve years. She typed a message, deleted it, typed it again.

She thought about the woman sitting in the waiting room of a hospital in Portland, two and a half thousand miles away. The woman she had watched for twenty years from a safe distance, through graduation photos, scientific papers, news reports. The woman Thomas had hidden, and she had helped him hide. The woman who, less than three weeks ago, sitting on the floor of an abandoned warehouse, had typed the name *Maren* into a reply box like a password, without knowing it was the first time in twenty years she had called herself by that name.

She typed:

> **[N]:** He's alive. Woke up at 4:17 this morning, your time.
> **[N]:** The first word he said was "Lena."

She stopped, her finger hovering over the screen.

She thought about two men. One lying in a pod under an island, calling a name. One lying in a hospital bed in Portland, who had held on to a different name for twenty years without knowing whose it was.

*Poor girl*, she thought.

Then she finished typing.

> **[N]:** The only machine that can save Elias Vane is right next to him. Same section. The FBI has to wait for the Canadians. You don't have to wait for anybody.
> **[N]:** There's a boat waiting at Pier 4, Bayside.
> **[N]:** Don't go alone. Bring your kids.

She hit send.

Then she put down the phone, lifted the cat back onto the keyboard, picked up the cold tea, took a sip, winced, and sat watching the red lights on top of the cranes blink in the rain until the sky got light.

---

In Portland, in the waiting room of the neuro ICU, the hallway lights had been turned down as low as they would go.

Linh was curled up asleep across three plastic chairs pushed together, Kev's jacket over her. Kev was asleep sitting up, his head against the wall, his arms folded across his chest, his legs stretched out across the aisle as if even asleep he had to keep watch. The vending machine at the end of the hall hummed every now and then.

Maren wasn't asleep. She sat in the chair closest to the glass of the ICU room, looking through it at the blue glow of the monitors, at the still shape under the white blanket. Her fingertips still remembered the feeling from earlier that evening, one, two, three, quick, and then two slow, as light as a hair.

The phone buzzed on her knee. A black crescent moon on a purple background.

She read the first message, then the second, and she couldn't breathe.

Her hand went to her throat on its own, found the chain under her collar, and closed tight around the ring, the way it had for eight months. It was warm from lying against her skin all day. On the other side of the glass, he was still lying there, the one who had looked for her for twenty years without knowing who he was looking for. And somewhere out there, under the fog, someone else had just woken up, unable to remember who he was, but still remembering her name.

Lena. Maren.

She read the rest of the messages. When she got to the last line, she stood up.

She went over to Kev and bent down and shook his shoulder. He opened his eyes instantly, wide awake as if he'd never been asleep, the way a man who once lived two years in a week wakes up.

"Go wake Viper," she said. "And wake Linh."

Kev looked at her, at the phone in her hand, at her other hand still clenched around the ring.

"Where are we going?" he asked.

Maren looked through the glass one more time.

"To bring someone home," she said.
