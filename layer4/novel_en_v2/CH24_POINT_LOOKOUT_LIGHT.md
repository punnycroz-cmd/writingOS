# CHAPTER 24: POINT LOOKOUT LIGHT

The Maine coast in early October had a kind of light Lena had never seen anywhere else. It wasn't gold like autumn sunlight in the city. It was white and thin, like light through tracing paper, and it made everything look farther away than it was.

They arrived at nine in the morning on the second, after three days at an empty campground near Portland. The three days were Kev's idea: *The signal's off, but they still know where it last pinged from. Let them spend three days guessing the wrong direction.* For those three days Elias slept almost without stopping, fourteen, sixteen hours at a stretch, as if his body were collecting twenty years of missed sleep. And for those three days Lena took his pulse every four hours and wrote it down in a red notebook she'd bought at a gas station, and told no one that she couldn't sleep either.

The road out to the point was dirt, narrow, lined on both sides with wild blueberry bushes gone dark red. The taco truck bucked and bounced, and Kev's gray pickup stayed close behind.

Then the road opened up, and they saw it.

The lighthouse wasn't very tall, maybe eighty feet, built of gray granite gone mossy at the base, the upper part painted white but peeling off in patches like fish scales. Beside it, low and squat, stood a windowless concrete box, the kind of building people only put up when they're afraid of war. On its roof was a huge rusted satellite dish aimed out to sea. The sea behind it was the color of lead, flat calm, not a boat in sight.

Viper cut the engine. Nobody got out right away.

"Corwin was here," Lena said. She didn't know who she was saying it to.

---

The door of the concrete building was secured with a brand-new combination padlock, shiny, completely out of place with everything around it. Kev looked at it for a while.

"Not Prometheus's," he said. "You buy these at a hardware store. Thirty bucks."

"Corwin's," Lena said.

Kev looked at her. "Combination?"

She tried Corwin's birthday. Wrong. Her birthday. Wrong. The day they got engaged. Still wrong.

She stood looking at the lock, her fingers on the dials. The sea wind cut through, bitterly cold, carrying the smell of seaweed and salt.

Then she turned it: *2-2-0-4.* The lock sprang open.

April twenty-second. Maren Solis's birthday in the Prometheus file. Not the birthday on Lena Hale's papers, the date Margaret had picked herself when she put together her documents in 2004.

Lena stood frozen with the lock in her hand.

"He knew," she said. "He knew my real birthday."

"Maybe he just saw it in the file," Kev said. "Same as we did."

"Saw it, and used it for a combination." She looked at the lock. "Nobody takes a random date out of a file and makes it their combination, Kev. People use dates that mean something to them."

Nobody answered.

---

Inside, the concrete building was cold and dry. It smelled of motor oil and dust, and faintly of something Lena recognized instantly, with a twist in her stomach: Old Spice aftershave, the kind Corwin had worn since he was eighteen because his father wore it.

It was an old control room. Gray metal consoles lined the walls, full of switches and gauges that had died long ago. In the middle of the room stood a wooden table and a swivel chair. In one corner was a cot with the blanket folded, a camping stove, and a few empty bean cans stacked neatly in a trash bag. In another corner a spiral iron staircase led down to a basement, and a door led through to the base of the lighthouse.

Linh had already run over to the consoles. "This one's still running," she said, sweeping her hand over an old server cabinet with a weak green light blinking. "Somebody reconnected the power. There's fiber running under the floor, down to the basement, out to sea." She turned around, her eyes shining. "Kev, this cable goes straight into Halloway's network. It's a back door."

Kev was already kneeling beside her.

Elias stood in the doorway looking around, doing what he always did: finding the exits, estimating distances, noticing what didn't belong. One exit at the front door, one into the lighthouse, one staircase down to the basement. No windows. Thick walls, with copper mesh inside the concrete to block signals. He could see it where a patch of plaster had come away.

Viper went straight to the camping stove, picked it up, and shook it. "Still got gas," he announced, as if it were the most important discovery of the day.

Lena stood in the middle of the room looking at the cot in the corner.

She walked over. The blanket was folded Corwin's way, not squared off like the military, but rolled up from one end like a jelly roll. He folded it exactly like that at home, and for four years she had nagged him about it.

On the pillow was a small notebook with a moss-green cloth cover, the kind Thomas used to carry, its spine worn through. She had never seen it before.

She picked it up.

---

Most of the notebook was technical notes. Corwin had never been good at technical things, so his handwriting on these pages was more careful than usual, like a schoolboy copying from the board.

*Undersea cable runs northeast from the basement, 9 miles, to a relay node on the seabed. From there it splits in two: one branch inland (Halloway's Layer 3 server, location unknown), one branch offshore (Zone 9?).*

*N. says this is an old Prometheus station they used to send data out to the Island Station back in the 2000s. Abandoned since 2010. But the cable's still live.*

*If I can get into the relay node from here → I can get into both branches.*

Lena turned the page. Hand-drawn diagrams, strings of numbers, a nautical chart torn out of a fishing guide with a red circle drawn around a spot about eighty nautical miles offshore, where there was nothing but the dark blue of deep water. Beside the circle he had written: *Here. It has to be here.*

Then came the last pages, and the handwriting changed completely. It wasn't careful anymore. It slanted harder, ran faster, like his writing in the leather notebook.

*Feb 6. Read the Zero–One pair file again tonight, tenth time. Can't sleep.*

*Subject One. Solis, Maren. Born 4/22/1995. Missing 10/14/2004.*

*The photo in the file.*

*I looked at that photo all night.*

*Feb 7. Called Mom. Asked if she knew whether Dad ever hid someone. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "Don't ask me that again." She's never talked to me like that.*

*I know now.*

*I knew from the photo. Every morning for four years I've looked at that face. I've looked at it while she was asleep. I know that chin, the way her eyes are set just a little too far apart.*

*L. is Maren.*

*Dad hid her. Dad died because he hid her. And I fell in love with her without ever knowing.*

*Feb 8. Didn't tell L. Couldn't. What do I say? "You were a kid they took for experiments, your mother burned to death, and my father hid you for twenty years"? She'll want proof. I don't have any. Just a photo and a gut feeling.*

*I need proof. I need the original archive out at the Island Station. N. says don't go. N. says it's too dangerous.*

*I'm going. Then I'll come back. Then I'll tell her everything, with proof in my hand.*

The last page had only a few lines, written in pencil, as if the pen had run dry.

*Feb 9, 4 a.m. Sea's calm. Going now.*

*L. — if you're reading this, it means I didn't make it back in time to tell you myself. I'm sorry. I should have told you on the 7th. I was scared.*

*If you're reading this, Lena, don't believe I'm dead.*

*I'm stubborn as hell. You know that.*

---

Lena didn't know when she had sat down on the edge of the cot.

She could hear the others still working: Linh and Kev talking to each other in a technical language she only half understood, Viper fiddling with the camping stove, the waves outside slapping steadily against the rocks. She couldn't hear Elias at all. Since she had opened the notebook, he had been standing motionless in the doorway.

*I was scared.*

Corwin, the loudest man she had ever known, who sang badly while he cooked, who argued with the morning paper, who never knew where his keys were. She had always thought he wasn't afraid of anything. And he had known for two days, had sat across from her at dinner on the seventh and the eighth, had kissed her good morning on the ninth before he went out to sea, and said nothing. Because he was scared.

She remembered what she had said to Elias on the phone five days ago: *I told you things I've never told anyone, not even Corwin.* She had said it like an accusation.

Now she sat here with Corwin's notebook in her lap, and she understood that every man who had loved her had hidden something from her because he was afraid of losing her. Thomas. Corwin. Elias. And she had hidden something too, for three days. A message at two in the morning.

She laughed, one short, dry sound, like a cough. Everyone turned to look at her.

"Lena?" Linh said.

"It's okay." Lena closed the notebook and stood up. Her knees shook a little, but she stayed standing. "He knew. He knew who I was two days before he went out. He went to get proof." She took a breath. "He wrote that I shouldn't believe he's dead."

Nobody said anything.

Lena looked around the room: Linh still holding a cable, Kev half hidden behind the server cabinet, Viper holding the camping stove like a sacred relic. And Elias in the doorway, leaning against the frame, the thin white sea light behind him so bright she couldn't see his face.

"Coordinates," she said, holding up the notebook. "The Island Station. He marked it on the chart. Eighty nautical miles east. Kev, if this cable reaches that far—"

"It reaches," Kev said. "But it carries data. Not people."

"Then data first, people after."

Kev looked at her for a moment, then nodded and turned back to the server cabinet.

Lena set the notebook on the wooden table next to Linh's laptop and walked toward the door, toward Elias. He stepped aside to let her through, but she didn't go through. She stopped right in front of him.

"You okay?" she asked.

The question startled him, as if nobody had ever asked him that before. Maybe nobody had, and meant it.

"Fine," he said.

"You're a terrible liar."

He didn't answer. She looked at him, and because she was close this time, she could see his face clearly. He wasn't crying. He wasn't angry. He looked like someone who had just heard very happy news, except it was someone else's happy news, and he was trying his best to be happy for them.

"I'm glad for you," he said. "I really am."

She knew he meant it, and that was the worst part.

"Come outside with me a minute," she said. "I need some air."

They went out to the edge of the rocks, where the gray waves broke against the foot of the lighthouse, and stood side by side looking out at the sea without saying anything. Somewhere out there, eighty nautical miles east, under the fog they couldn't see from here, a man was lying in a white pod.

She took the ring on her chain and turned it between two fingers. He didn't look at the ring. He looked at the sea.
