# CHAPTER 34: OVERWRITE

Layer 4 had no sound.

That was the first thing Elias noticed this time, now that it wasn't swallowing him. The last time, in an eleven-day coma that lasted thirty years, it had been a landslide, the voices and images and tastes of a thousand lives crashing down on his head with nothing to hold on to. Now, standing at the edge of the crack with Maren's hand tight in his, he found it as quiet as a church at midnight, like an enormous room where everyone was holding their breath to see what would happen next.

And he could see everything.

Not with his eyes. He saw it the way you can stand in a dark room and still know how many corners it has, which side the door is on, whether the ceiling is high or low, without reaching out to touch anything. He saw the hexagonal room, saw the silver rivers running under the glass floor, saw millions of threads flowing to the glass sphere in the middle of the room, and this time he knew where every one of them led. A kitchen in Ohio, where a woman had fallen asleep at the table next to a pile of unpaid bills. A dorm room in Seoul. A hospital bed in São Paulo, where an old man was dreaming that he was swimming. A long-haul truck pulled over on the shoulder of a highway in Nebraska, the driver asleep sitting up with his head against the window. Three hundred million places, three hundred million people asleep, and beside every pillow a small glowing animal that was still awake.

Then he saw Halloway.

The woman in the navy wool coat was still standing ten steps away, but here she was also something else: a black cord, thick and rigid, running from her raised hand to the glass sphere, and from the sphere branching out into millions of smaller cords wrapped around those millions of silver threads, like a parasitic vine that had climbed over an entire forest without anyone at the foot of the trees ever noticing.

She had been standing there for twenty years. Right in the middle.

"You see it now," Halloway said. Her voice was still smooth, but the raised arm had begun to tremble, very slightly, like the arm of someone who has been holding something heavy for too long. "You see all of it. That's Layer 4, Zero. That's what I gave my whole life to stand in. And now you can stand here. With her." She smiled thinly. "Let me show you how to use it."

Elias didn't answer. He just squeezed Maren's hand, and she squeezed back.

Then he raised his other hand.

---

There was no magic. No pillar of light, no thunder, none of the things Viper always wanted to add whenever the team sat down to plan tactics. In Layer 4, everything was a matter of permissions. Who was allowed to touch what. For twenty years only Halloway had been allowed. Elias simply touched the black cord.

It was cold, as cold as an iron doorknob in a room in winter with no heat on. He felt it pause when it met him, like a strange dog stopping to sniff a stranger. It probed to find out who he was, checked his permissions once, then again, like a security guard turning an ID badge over and over under a lamp.

Then it let go.

It let go the way a fist finally opens after clenching too tight for too long. The black cord slid off the glass sphere, and its millions of offshoots slid off the millions of silver threads one after another, strand by strand, like hair falling out of the teeth of a comb.

Halloway screamed.

It was the scream of someone watching everything she had built in her life get taken away in one second. Her arm was yanked down as if someone had jerked a string, and she staggered back two steps.

"No," she said. "No. You don't have permission. I made you."

"You made Subject Zero," Elias said. "The name Eli, she gave me."

On the black walls of the hexagonal room, dozens of red lights flared at once. The fortress's defense system, the automatic thing that had blasted Viper apart and erased Kev without anyone giving the order, had just detected that its administrator was under attack. Gun emplacements rose out of the stone, barrels swung toward the two people at the edge of the crack, and they fired.

Maren gave one short cry and pressed close against him.

Elias didn't move. He just looked. In Layer 4, a bullet was nothing but a line of instruction that said *this is flying toward that*, and he could read that line as easily as the words on a sign. All he had to do was rewrite it.

Hundreds of rounds stopped in midair an arm's length from the two of them, hanging there like a swarm of frozen bees. He hadn't meant to turn them into anything. But in this place, whatever he thought of was what things became, and the first thought that came into his head was a rough voice singing in a smoke-filled corridor, singing about something neither of those two children had ever seen with their own eyes.

The bullets went white. Softened. Then they began to fall.

Snow. Big, slow snowflakes falling onto the black glass floor, onto his shoulders, into Maren's hair, melting where they touched the silver rivers below.

Maren tipped her face up to watch, her lips parted.

"*Nana de la nieve*," she whispered, like someone who has just remembered the words to a song.

On the other side of the snowfall, Halloway's image was coming apart. The hem of the navy coat was peeling off into pixels, drifting upward like ash from a burning sheet of paper. She looked down at her hands, at her fingers dissolving, and for the first time Elias saw something on her face he recognized instantly, because he had worn it on his own face for twenty years. Fear.

She lifted her head, but she didn't look at him. She looked at the snow.

"Elena," she said, to no one at all. "Elena, you..."

She didn't finish. Her image burst apart and was gone, and all that was left in the hexagonal room was a small *click*, like a cable yanked out of a socket.

Three hundred miles away, on the forty-second floor of a glass tower in Manhattan, in a dark office lit only by the city outside, a woman in her fifties sitting in a leather chair jerked violently, clutched her head in both hands, and pitched onto the floor, taking the chair with her.

---

The black animals were still sitting there.

Hundreds of them in a circle around the two of them, snow falling on their backs and not one of them shaking it off. Wolves, foxes, crows, cats, and some that hadn't finished taking shape, half dog and half something nobody had a name for. Their eyes were still dull silver, still watching, but the black cord that had jerked them around was gone, and they sat there like a classroom the moment the teacher steps out, none of them knowing what to do next.

The small pale-blue fox was still sitting exactly where Halloway's feet had been. It looked at the empty space in front of it for a long time. Then it lay down and rested its chin on its front paws.

"What are they?" Maren asked softly.

"Copies," Elias said. "Of the ones out there. Learned from three hundred million people." He looked at them. "She was going to use them to touch everyone's memories."

"Can you delete them?"

He thought he could. In here he could do almost anything. One sweep of his hand and these hundreds of animals would crumble to dust, like the soldiers in black outside the gate.

A small crow stepped out of the circle.

Its feathers were black with a blue sheen, its eyes ringed with dull silver. It hopped across the glass, three hops, leaving three tiny footprints in the thin layer of snow, and stopped at the toe of his boot. It tilted its head and looked at him with one eye, then the other, just like outside the gate.

This time it didn't caw like a question. It flapped up and landed on his shoulder.

Elias stood still. The crow was heavier than he expected. Its claws gripped the armor on his shoulder, not painfully, just firmly, as if it had perched there a thousand times. It rubbed its head once against his neck, exactly like a cat.

"Coal," Maren said, and her voice changed. "He knows you."

"My brainwave signature changed again," Elias said. He didn't know how he knew it, but he knew, the way you know you've grown a little after a long illness. "This time Halloway didn't change it."

Maren looked down at their two joined hands and said nothing.

Elias looked at the rest of the black animals. They weren't Coal. They had no owners. But they were made from everything three hundred million people had murmured to their Echoes at three in the morning when they couldn't sleep: complaints about the boss, confessions they couldn't tell anyone, lullabies sung to a child with a fever. He thought of Brisket and his pink bow, of Bubbles blowing bubbles every time Linh laughed, of Kev's gray paper crane standing guard on the bow. He didn't delete them.

He only undid the last cords still connecting them to the glass sphere, one by one, gently, the way you untangle a ball of yarn.

The black animals blinked. The silver points in their eyes changed to another color, warmer, like a night-light. Then, one by one, they stood, shook the snow off their backs, turned, and walked out of the hexagonal room through the open gate, into the black city beyond, scattering in every direction. Nobody told them where to go. The pale-blue fox went last, looking back at the empty spot on the floor one more time before it left.

He didn't know where they would go, or whether this was right or wrong.

That, he thought, was something for another day.

---

The glass sphere still floated in the middle of the room, with no cords attached to it now. Inside, the streams of light still moved like smoke. Twenty years were in there: Prometheus, Mnemosyne, Elena, Thomas, Corwin, four children's names on a list Kev still hadn't told anyone about, thousands of hours of recordings from the Echoes in people's bedrooms.

Elias held out his hand, and the sphere drifted into his palm, as light as a soap bubble.

"Now what?" Maren asked.

He looked at the sphere, then out at the gate, then at the line connecting him to her, the anchor line, running from his head through her hand, back up, through miles of fiber-optic cable lying on the seabed, to a console in a concrete building at the foot of a lighthouse.

"The fortress is sealed," he said. He could feel it. When Halloway was thrown out, the whole fortress had sealed itself up, like a hedgehog curling into a ball. "Every way out is closed. Except one."

Maren followed his eyes. She looked at the line.

"Mine," she said.

"Yours."

She understood immediately. She always understood immediately. Even at nine. "I take it up. Back to the console. And broadcast it from there."

"Yeah."

"And you stay down here."

"Just a few seconds," he said. "Without you, this door won't hold for long. But a few seconds, it will. Enough for you to get up."

She looked at him for a long time, and he knew what she was thinking. She was thinking about a ventilation shaft. A little girl crawling forward, and a little boy staying behind.

"This time's different," he said.

"Different how?"

"Last time, I didn't know if you'd come back." He put the glass sphere in her hands. "This time I know."

She looked at the sphere in her hands, then at him. Then she did something he didn't expect. She went up on her toes and pressed her forehead to his, the way she had on the warehouse floor, the way her mother used to do every night at the edge of the iron bed.

"Three seconds," she said. "No more."

"Three seconds."

"If you take longer, I'm coming back down here and dragging you up by your ear."

"I believe you."

She tapped the back of his hand. Three quick, two slow.

Then she let go.

---

Three seconds out there was fifty minutes in here.

Elias had worked that out ahead of time. But working it out was one thing. Standing alone in the hexagonal room with the snow still falling, watching the anchor line pull taut as a guitar string and draw one small point of light up and away, farther, farther, until he couldn't see it anymore, was something else.

Behind him, the door began to sway.

He felt it before he saw it, the way someone on the deck of a ship feels the first wave. The nameless light in the crack shivered, swelled, then pulled. Not as hard as before. Just enough to remind him it was still there. Still waiting.

He sat down on the glass floor with his back to the crack. Coal hopped down from his shoulder onto his knee and fluffed his feathers so the snow wouldn't settle. The snow fell on Elias's shoulders and wasn't cold.

He started to count.

Counting seconds meant nothing here, so he counted what he had. A name, given to him by a nine-year-old girl. A lime-yellow taco truck with a sombrero-wearing crow painted on the side. Eight grams of sugar a serving, and a woman snatching the can out of his hand and telling him to stop smiling. Two stitches under the flickering fluorescent lights of a laundromat. Linh calling him *Eli* like the big brother she'd never had. Viper standing in a hail of fire in a pink apron. Kev stepping in front of him without a word. A lighthouse, a notebook, a kitchen he had never stood in but still remembered, because somebody else had remembered it for him.

Three hundred million silver threads flowed under the glass floor, gentle, with nothing wrapped around them anymore. He could hear them if he wanted to. A mother in Ohio, a student in Seoul, an old man swimming in his sleep. He didn't listen. He sat still and kept counting.

In the fortieth minute, the crack behind him widened.

He felt it pulling at his hair, pulling at the edge of his coat, pulling at the names he had just counted, the way a tide pulls sand out from under your feet. Coal cawed and dug his claws into Elias's knee. Elias put a hand on his back.

He thought about the little girl inside the shaft, her wrists skinny, her eyes wide in the dark. He thought about the woman at the edge of the crack, her hair lifting as if she were underwater, shouting in his face that this time he didn't get to stay behind alone.

*Once you've found someone, you stay.*

He stood up, turned his back on the crack, and walked toward the place where the anchor line had disappeared. There was nothing left there, only a faint smear of light in the air, like the mark a finger leaves on a fogged-up window. He reached out and touched it, and knocked.

Three quick. Two slow.

Then everything went dark.

---

Maren opened her eyes in smoke.

The concrete building was full of it, black and thick and stinging, the reek of burning rubber and motor oil. One of the soldiers' trucks outside had exploded again while she was down there, and its fuel tank had thrown fire onto the pile of splintered wood heaped in front of the steel door, and now smoke was pouring in, rolling under the ceiling like a river flowing the wrong way.

Her nose was bleeding. She could taste iron on her lips. She fell out of the chair, her knees hit the concrete, and she didn't feel it.

The console was fifteen feet away, but she couldn't see it. All she could see was smoke.

"Doc!" Nyx's voice came from the speaker somewhere in the smoke. "Doc, you're up! Forty seconds, you were only gone forty seconds and I thought... Never mind. Listen to me. The data's here. It's sitting in the console's buffer, twenty-three terabytes. The cable running along the outside wall burned, so the automatic route's dead. You have to push it manually. Red button, lower right of the keyboard."

Maren crawled.

She crawled across the concrete floor with her face down low where the smoke was thinner, her knees and palms scraping over splinters of wood and broken glass. She didn't feel any of it. She just counted. Three feet. Six feet. To her left, through the smoke, she could see two folding chairs, see Kev's and Linh's chests still rising and falling, steady, like two people asleep on a night bus. On the floor, Viper still lay on his side where she'd left him, his bearded chin resting on one huge arm.

Nine feet.

In the doorway, through the smoke, she saw Bradley's silhouette. He hadn't run. He'd taken off his gray wool overcoat and was beating it against the flames climbing the doorframe, again and again, coughing hard, his graying hair plastered with sweat.

Twelve feet.

Her hand touched the leg of the console. She grabbed it and pulled herself up. Her eyes were stinging so badly she couldn't see anything, so she felt her way: the top of the console, the keyboard, the plastic edge, the lower right corner.

A round button, raised, cold.

She suddenly remembered that twenty years ago her mother had stood in a corridor full of smoke just like this one, and her mother hadn't had any button to press.

She pressed it.

> `[BROADCAST: TRANSMITTING]`
> `[RECIPIENTS: FEDERAL BUREAU OF INVESTIGATION, DEPARTMENT OF JUSTICE, CONGRESSIONAL OVERSIGHT COMMITTEE, REUTERS, ASSOCIATED PRESS, THE NEW YORK TIMES, BBC, +412 OTHER ADDRESSES]`
> `[PROGRESS: 4%... 11%... 26%...]`

She didn't stand there waiting for it to finish.

She turned around, and through the smoke she saw the last folding chair.

Elias was lying there. Not arched up anymore. Not convulsing. He lay still, his head tipped to one side, the blood dried in streaks on his cheek. On the console screen, his waveform wasn't red anymore. It had gone almost flat.

Almost.

And on his shoulder, here in the real world, where there was no reason for it to be, sat a small crow glowing pale blue, faint as a projection from the old phone with the cracked screen he still kept in his shirt pocket. It perched there with its head bowed, looking at him, and didn't fly away.

Maren let go of the edge of the console, dropped to her knees, and crawled toward him.
