# CHAPTER 4: THE EXAMINER'S OFFICE

"Look at second twelve," Lena said, tapping the stylus on the screen. The brainwave chart froze on a jagged red spike, sharp as a saw tooth.

Nia leaned over her shoulder, both hands around a coffee mug. "Amygdala lights up, heart rate jumps forty percent. Textbook panic response. He really is scared."

"He really is scared," Lena agreed. "Just not of the accident." She dragged the timeline back half a second and played it in slow motion. "See? The fear arrives first. Eight hundred and forty milliseconds later you get the auditory signal, the engine blowing. With real trauma the brain hears the explosion and then gets scared. Here it's backwards. Somebody planted the fear first and pasted the sound on afterward so it would make sense."

Nia studied the chart a little longer and let out a breath. "Planted through sleep therapy?"

"Layer 2, about three weeks ago, right around when he filed for seven million in damages against the airline." Lena double-tapped the screen and opened the report template. "Put this in the file for me: *Artificial memory. Deliberate Layer 2 intervention. Insufficient grounds for the claim.*"

"You're genuinely terrifying, you know that?" Nia took a sip of coffee. "One day someone's going to walk in here with a real memory, a total mess, doesn't fit any chart, and you won't have a clue what to do with it."

Lena didn't answer. The fingers of her left hand found the chain under the collar of her sweater on their own and touched the small metal circle hanging from it, a plain ring of brushed silver. For seven months she had worn it on the chain, ever since the day she ran out of the courage to wear it on her finger.

Nia saw. Nia always saw.

"You haven't eaten since this morning," Nia said, her voice going soft. "Come get phở with me? The place on Fourth is open till ten."

"I've still got two reports for the DA's office."

"Lena."

"I'll make up for it tomorrow."

Nia gave her a long look, the look of a friend who had known her for eight years, since they were both shaking interns in a university lab. Then she set down her coffee and picked up her bag.

"I put a container in the fridge for you. If it's still full tomorrow morning, I'm telling Margaret."

"Are you threatening me?"

"I'm promising you." Nia pulled on her coat, got as far as the door, and turned back. "Lock the downstairs lobby door, okay? Last week some stranger got all the way up to the fourth floor."

The door closed. Nia's footsteps faded down the hall, then the elevator, then silence.

---

Suite 4B of the St. Jude Medical Building had been designed so that nothing in it made a sound. The walls were lined with ash-gray acoustic panels. Cork lay under the wool carpet. There was no wall clock, no ceiling fan, no glass or polished metal that might throw a stray flash of light into the eyes of a patient coming up out of a deep dream. On the door hung a brass plate that she had polished every Monday morning for five years:

> **LENA HALE**
> *Cognitive Verification & Memory Forensics*
> *Experience is evidence. Memory is testimony.*

When she'd had the motto engraved, Corwin had laughed until he cried. *It sounds like the slogan for a cult,* he said, *a really strict cult where nobody's allowed to daydream.* Then he kissed her forehead and told her to keep it, because it was exactly her.

Lena finished one report, started the second, read the same paragraph three times without understanding it, and put the stylus down.

It was dark outside. Early autumn rain tapped gently on the window, and the streetlights smeared into yellow streaks on the wet glass. She looked at the small safe set into the filing cabinet in the corner. She had promised herself she wouldn't open it tonight.

She got up anyway, went over, and turned the dial. Corwin's birthday.

Inside were three boxes of documents, a hard drive, and a worn leather notebook. Everything he had left. Seven months ago, three days after the memorial, his mother Ruth had brought them over in a cardboard box and said, *He wanted you to have these. He told me a long time ago. If anything happened.*

*If anything happened.* As if he'd known.

Lena took out the notebook and sat down on the floor with her back against the cabinet. She had read it dozens of times. The first pages were an ordinary reporter's notes: names, phone numbers, contract figures. Further in, the pages changed. The handwriting got faster and shorter, arrows linked names together, questions were written in capitals.

*What did Dad find in 2012?*

*Prometheus — the 2004 fire — why were the fire department records sealed?*

*MNEMOSYNE. Not a product. A program.*

And on a page near the end, underlined twice:

*SUBJECT ZERO = ELIAS VANE. Alive. Under surveillance. They NEED him for something.*

Below it, in a different color of ink, as if added later: *Ask Nyx about the Island Station.*

In the very first week, Lena had looked up the name Elias Vane. All she found was a frozen gaming account and a few matches in a league called Vanguard. Nothing else. No address, no medical records, not a single photo. It was as if someone had carefully lifted the man out of the world and left only his name behind. She asked two acquaintances at the DA's office to run him. Both came back with the same answer: *Records sealed under a national security order. Stop asking.*

As for who or what Nyx was, she had no idea.

She turned to the last page. A single line, written in a hurry, the pen pressed so hard it nearly tore the paper:

*L — if I don't make it back in time, don't look. Promise me.*

She hadn't promised. And he hadn't come back.

Lena closed her eyes and let her head rest against the cabinet. The fingers of her right hand began to tap her knee, light and even, in a rhythm she had never paid attention to: three quick, two slow. Corwin had asked her once what it was. She'd said she didn't know, probably a habit from when she was little.

Except she didn't really have a when-she-was-little. She had no memories from before the age of nine. The doctors said it was a head injury from the car accident that killed her parents. Margaret said so, the records said so, everyone said so, and for twenty years she had believed it, because there was no reason not to. Her first memory was of a pale green hospital room and a man in a police uniform sitting by the bed, peeling an apple in one long unbroken spiral.

That was Thomas, Corwin's father. The first person she had ever known, before anyone else.

She sometimes thought that was why she'd chosen this work. A person with no memories, verifying other people's. A kind of compensation, or a kind of hiding. Nia said it was both.

*Ding.*

The brass bell over the waiting-room door rang out, long and clear. Lena opened her eyes.

Had she locked the downstairs door? She couldn't remember. The clock on her monitor said 6:40, and there were no appointments today.

She got up, put the notebook back in the safe, and shut it. Her heart was beating a little faster, which was silly. It was probably a delivery, or someone on the wrong floor. She went to the frosted glass door between the office and the waiting room and put her hand on the handle. The door opened from the other side before she could pull it.

---

A man stood in the doorway.

He was tall, very tall, but he stood as if he were trying to fold himself smaller. He was soaked from head to foot. Rain ran in streams off the hem of an oversized navy wool coat and dripped onto the carpet in dark circles. His black hair was plastered to his temples. His face was thin, the cheekbones standing out as if carved with a knife. Above the collar of the coat his collarbones showed sharp, like someone just through a long illness.

But what stopped Lena where she stood were his eyes.

They were the gray of the sky before a storm, and they were looking straight at her, unblinking, with an intensity that made her feel like she was standing naked in a brightly lit room.

Her body reacted before her mind could work out what was happening. A current ran down her spine from the nape of her neck to the small of her back. The fine hairs on her arms stood up. Her lungs forgot how to breathe in. On her wrist, her smartwatch buzzed: *Irregular heart rate — 118 bpm.*

She was an expert. She had seen this reaction hundreds of times on other people's charts. It was called somatic recognition, when the body recognizes a person or a smell or a sound before conscious thought catches up. It was common in trauma survivors when they came face to face with something from their past.

But she didn't have a past.

The man took one step into the room, his wet soles squeaking on the carpet. He stopped. His lips moved, as if testing a word he had whispered many times but never said out loud.

"Maren."

The name fell into the soundproofed room and didn't echo.

Lena didn't know anyone called Maren. She had never heard the name in her life. It was two unfamiliar syllables that meant nothing to her. So why were her knees shaking?

"I'm sorry," she heard herself say, her voice thin and far away, like it was coming from the next room. "You have the wrong person. I'm Lena Hale. The clinic's closed. If you need emergency care, the county trauma center is three blocks toward—"

"I know," he said. "I know your name is Lena. I read it in the paper." He drew a shaky breath. "But that's not the name I remember."

He looked at her a moment longer, as though comparing her face with something only he could see, and then went on, slowly, each word hauled up from the bottom of a well.

"I just woke up from an eleven-day coma. I don't remember who I am. I don't remember where I live. I don't remember my parents' faces. The only thing left in my head is a name and your face." He paused. "My name is Elias Vane."

Lena could hear the blood rushing in her ears.

*SUBJECT ZERO = ELIAS VANE.* Corwin's handwriting, underlined twice, lying thirteen feet away from her in a safe locked with his birthday.

The fingers of her right hand, hanging at her side, started tapping her thigh again. Three quick, two slow. The man's gray eyes slid down to her hand, and he went completely still, as if he had just heard someone call his name from very far away.
