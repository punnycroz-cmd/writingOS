# CHAPTER 11: VIPER'S HOUSE

The town was called Ashgrove, and it looked exactly like its name: a main street six blocks long; a paper mill that had closed ten years ago, its brick smokestack still standing alone at the end of the street like a finger pointing at the sky; a diner whose neon sign only lit up half its letters; a white-painted church; and two-story wooden houses with front porches, the paint peeling, the yards full of weeds and kids' toys.

Viper's house was the last one on a dead-end street at the edge of the woods. It was painted sky blue, with a tire swing hanging from an oak in the front yard. A row of potted sunflowers lined the porch, half wilted, half blazing yellow.

Rosie was waiting on the front steps. Lena spotted her before the truck had fully stopped: eight years old, curly hair in two crooked pigtails, a dinosaur nightgown pulled over her jeans, yellow rubber boots, arms folded across her chest, face as stern as a traffic cop's.

"Why didn't you wake me up when you left?" Rosie demanded the second Viper stepped out of the truck.

"I left a note."

"The note said *Dad went to rescue a friend. Pancakes in the microwave.* It didn't say which friend or where." Rosie squinted past her father's shoulder. "Which friend is this?"

Viper knelt down to her height. "This is Mr. Vane. He plays games with me. He's in a little trouble, so he's going to stay with us a few days."

Rosie looked Elias up and down, exactly the way her father had looked at him in the laundromat that morning, then turned to Lena. "And her?"

"That's Miss Lena," Viper said. "She's a doctor."

"Can you fix goldfish?" Rosie asked Lena. "Mine keeps swimming sideways."

Lena blinked. "I'm... not sure. I specialize in human brains."

"Goldfish have brains."

"That's true, they do." Lena nodded seriously. "I'll take a look."

Rosie nodded as if a contract had just been signed, then turned and ran inside, her yellow boots thumping on the wooden floor, yelling at the top of her lungs, "Grandma! Dad's back! He brought a skinny man and a fish doctor!"

Viper stood up, brushed off his knees, and turned to them with a grin that was half embarrassed and half proud.

"Welcome home," he said.

---

The goldfish was named Sir Floatsalot, and he really was swimming sideways. Lena watched him for ten minutes, asked Rosie about his diet, discovered she'd been feeding him nothing but cookie crumbs, and wrote a prescription: no food for two days, then shelled boiled peas. Rosie copied the prescription into a unicorn notebook in purple pencil, carefully sounding out each word.

"You're the best doctor I ever met," Rosie announced.

"How many doctors have you met?"

"Three." Rosie thought about it. "But you're the only one who'd look at Sir Floatsalot."

Lena couldn't remember the last time she had laughed that easily.

---

For dinner Viper made a pot of Texas chili, real this time, not virtual, with skillet cornbread and a huge bowl of sour cream in the middle of the table. Elias had smelled it in the Crow's Nest, but in real life it made him dizzy.

They sat around a chipped wooden table in the small kitchen, Viper at the head, Rosie on his left, Lena and Elias side by side across from her. Viper's mother, Rosa, a tiny woman with her white hair in a high bun, had brought over a tray of flan and gone back next door, but before she left she hugged Lena hard and said quietly in her ear, "Marcus told me everything. Nobody here's going to ask you questions, mija."

Elias ate slowly. He wasn't used to eating with other people. He didn't know how he knew that, but he knew.

Rosie talked nonstop: about school, about Sir Floatsalot, about a boy named Tyler who ate a bug at recess to win a dollar. Now and then Viper cut in to correct the details, "It was an earthworm, sweetheart, not a bug," and Rosie waved him off. "You weren't there."

"Mr. Vane," Rosie said, turning to Elias, sour cream all over her mouth, "did you know my dad's really good at video games?"

Elias looked at Viper. Viper shrugged.

"I don't remember," Elias said honestly. "I lost my memory."

Rosie's eyes went wide. "Like in a movie?"

"I guess so."

"So do you remember my dad?"

The whole table went quiet. Viper set down his spoon.

Elias looked at the girl, then at Viper. The big man was bent over his bowl of chili, carefully not looking at anyone.

"I don't remember," Elias said slowly. "But last night, when I was in trouble, your dad drove two hours in the middle of the night to come get me. So I think I already know what kind of person he is."

Rosie nodded as if that answer made perfect sense. "My dad's really nice," she said. "His food's kind of spicy, though."

Viper coughed, got up, and went to get more cornbread. Lena saw him wipe his eyes with the back of his hand when he turned around.

---

After dinner, Rosie was herded off to the bath. Elias washed the dishes and Lena dried, the two of them working quietly side by side at the sink, their elbows bumping now and then.

Viper came in, set two bottles of beer on the table, and dropped into a creaky chair.

"She's asleep," he said. "Made me tell her a story about the skinny guy who lost his memory."

"What did you tell her?" Elias asked.

"The truth, what else." Viper popped the cap off a bottle. "About your first year on the team, three months without a word except *left*, *right*, *out of ammo*. Lin thought you were a bot. I thought you hated us."

"Did I?"

"No." Viper took a swallow. "You just didn't know how to be around people. Like a stray dog somebody brought home. Stands in the corner and watches. Won't come closer, won't leave either." He looked over at Lena. "Three years, Doctor, and I never once heard him laugh. Not even over the mic."

Lena stopped drying the bowl in her hands. She didn't look at Elias.

"But today in the truck he laughed," Viper went on, his voice going a little rough. "When the pot fell. I heard it."

Elias didn't say anything. He picked up the last plate and washed it for much longer than it needed.

---

At ten that night, the pain came back.

Elias was sitting on the edge of the cot Viper had set up for him in the study, a little room crammed with cables and computer parts, trying to reread Corwin's notebook. The lines started to swim in front of his eyes, and then it was as if a red-hot nail had been driven straight in behind his left eye.

He didn't cry out. He doubled over, holding his head in both hands, his vision breaking apart into tiny glittering specks of white noise like snow on a broken screen. Something warm ran down onto his upper lip, with the tang of iron. Blood.

He heard the door open, bare feet on the wooden floor, and then a cool hand on the back of his neck.

"Head down. Don't tip it back," Lena said calmly. "Breathe through your mouth."

He did. She pressed a wad of gauze to his nose, and then he heard the latches of the case snap open and glass vials clinking together.

"This'll be cold," she said.

A nozzle pressed against the side of his neck, a soft hiss, and then an icy rush ran up his carotid artery into his brain, like someone pouring ice water into a furnace. The red-hot nail cooled. The specks thinned out. His heart, which had been pounding like a drum, slowed one beat at a time.

When he could lift his head, Lena was kneeling in front of him with an empty pneumatic injector in both hands, her face closer to his than it had ever been.

"Alpha-9," she said. "A cerebrovascular stabilizer. I brought four doses. Now there are three."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She sat down on the floor with her back against the cot, right next to his feet. "Listen to me, Elias. I looked at your scan at the clinic. The capillaries around your temporal lobes are damaged. It's like a wire that's been overloaded. The insulation is melting. Every time you go deep, every time you're under too much stress, it gets a little worse."

"How much worse?"

She didn't answer right away. "You can't go into Layer 4 again," she said. "Not ever, not for any reason. If you go in, your brain won't take it. Massive hemorrhage. You'll die, or worse." She turned to look at him. "Promise me."

Elias looked at her. The desk lamp lit one side of her face, and the other side was in shadow. The smear of soot on her cheek had been washed off.

"I promise," he said.

She nodded and sat there a little longer without saying anything, just listening to his breathing even out. Then she got up and took the case back to her room.

---

Lena couldn't sleep.

She sat on the single bed in Viper's spare room, small-flowered wallpaper gone yellow, Rosie's moon-shaped night-light on the nightstand. She opened Corwin's third box, the one she hadn't finished reading, and found a stack of blurry photocopies: Appendix C of the Prometheus file. Corwin had gotten it from somewhere without noting the source.

*Agreement with Lead Solis.*

She read it by the light of the moon lamp and saw that it wasn't an agreement at all. It was an ultimatum.

Elena Solis had discovered the program was testing on children and wanted out. Halloway gave her a choice: either Elena stayed on and kept working, or her daughter, a child with a rare neurological index that Elena herself had once mentioned in a meeting without thinking, would be "admitted to the program under institutional supervision" by a juvenile court custody order Halloway had already prepared.

Elena chose to stay. And Maren was taken into the program anyway.

At the end of the appendix was a handwritten note, in the hand of someone at the institute, as flat as a weather log:

*Solis requests permission to sing to the subjects at bedtime. Approved. Stabilizing effect on Theta rhythm observed in both Zero and One.*

Lena put the page down. *The subjects.* Not her daughter. The subjects, plural. Her mother had sung lullabies to both children, every night, through the walls of a laboratory, to her own daughter and to a seven-year-old boy who had no one in the world.

She thought about the man asleep in the room across the hall, about his clumsy laugh in the truck, about what he'd said to Rosie: *I think I already know what kind of person he is.* She thought about how, in his brain scan, that lullaby was stored on a level deeper than his name, deeper than any face, so deep that thirty years of Layer 4 chaos hadn't been able to wipe it away.

Outside the window the wind moved through the oak, and the tire swing rocked gently in the dark.

Lena turned off the light and lay down, and for the first time in months, she fell asleep without thinking about the sea.
