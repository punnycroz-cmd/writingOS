# CHAPTER 16: AN ORDINARY DAY

"You're dead," Linh said.

"I know."

"That's eleven times."

"I know, Linh."

"I'm just keeping you informed." Linh squatted next to Lena's virtual corpse, chin in her hand, ponytail swinging. Bubbles sat on her shoulder and blew one enormous bubble, as if offering condolences. "Technically you died four seconds slower than last time, so that's progress, in a sense."

Lena lay on her back on the fake concrete floor of an abandoned warehouse, looking up at a ceiling of rusted steel beams, and wondered why she had ever said yes to this.

This was the Vanguard practice range, a scaled-down copy of Layer 3 that anyone with a Somnia account could get into. Time here only ran five times faster, not a hundred, because civilian law didn't allow more: ten minutes outside was fifty minutes inside. After lunch Linh had dragged her in here on the grounds that "if you're going to come with us someplace dangerous, you should at least know how to hold a gun."

Lena had pointed out that she was a doctor. Linh had pointed out that Viper used to be a baker.

"Viper was a baker?"

"He had a bakery with his wife, back when she was alive. His croissants were amazing." Linh shrugged. "Now he sells tacos. That's life."

Lena hadn't known Viper's wife was dead. She thought about the sky-blue house, the tire swing, the row of sunflowers on the porch, half of them wilted. A big man who could only keep half the flowers alive, and had to let the other half go.

She got up and brushed virtual dust off her pants. "Again."

---

On the twelfth try, she died after a minute forty. On the thirteenth, she lasted two minutes ten and took down one target before she died. On the fourteenth, she stopped running.

She crouched behind a stack of wooden crates, looked around the warehouse, and for the first time did what she had been doing in her clinic for five years: she observed. The targets weren't moving randomly. They had a rhythm. The robot by the second pillar changed direction every four seconds. The one on the mezzanine froze whenever the one on the floor passed beneath it. They were programmed, like everything else was programmed, and anything programmed had a lag.

She waited, counted, and fired.

On the fourteenth try, she survived.

Linh stood in the warehouse doorway with her mouth hanging open. Bubbles blew three bubbles in a row.

"You just..." Linh began.

"Response latency," Lena said. She was still breathing hard, still holding the virtual gun, and something was rising in her she hadn't felt in a long time, a silly, childish joy. "From the moment they spot you to the moment they turn around, there's about a three-hundred-millisecond lag. It's exactly how I tell a real memory from a planted one. You look for the delay."

Linh stared at her for two more seconds, then screamed, ran over, and jumped onto Lena's back like a monkey.

"VIPER!" Linh yelled into the comm channel. "VIPER, THE FISH DOCTOR JUST SOLOED THE WHOLE RANGE!"

Viper's voice came back from somewhere, small and crackly. He was outside, in real life. "What? I'm chopping onions!"

"SHE'S BETTER THAN YOU NOW!"

"No way."

"THEN COME SEE!"

---

September fifteenth was Rosie's birthday. She was turning nine.

Viper had wanted to throw a little party in the yard with a cake and a few kids from her class, but things being what they were, they couldn't invite strangers to the house. So Linh came up with another idea: have the party in the Crow's Nest.

Rosie had had sub-admin rights for ages. She spent the whole morning redecorating the deck: rainbow ribbons wrapped around every railing, dinosaur balloons floating everywhere, and right in the middle a five-tier cake taller than Viper, each tier a different color, topped with a candle shaped like a goldfish swimming sideways.

"That's Sir Floatsalot," Rosie explained to Lena. "He's all better now. Because of you."

Rosie's Echo was a tiny purple unicorn named Candy who only came up to your knee. She galloped in circles around everyone's feet, her hooves clattering on the planks, sneezing glitter every so often. Brisket trailed after her with the weary face of an underpaid nanny.

Elias stood at the edge of the deck, leaning against the rail. For the first half hour Lena watched him stay right there. He wasn't avoiding anyone, but he didn't come closer either, exactly the way Viper had described: a stray dog somebody brought home, standing in the corner, watching.

Until Rosie ran over and grabbed his hand. "Mr. Vane, you have to play this."

"Play what?"

"The candle game. Whoever blows out the candle gets a wish. But this candle's special. My dad made it. You have to blow three times to put it out." Rosie looked up at him, solemn. "You get the second blow. The first one's mine and the last one's Miss Lena's."

"Why am I second?"

"Because you're in the middle," Rosie said, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. "You're in between me and Miss Lena."

Elias looked at her, and Lena could see he had no idea what to say. Neither did she. Rosie wasn't waiting for anyone to answer anyway. She had already pulled them both up to the cake, squeezed herself in between them, and taken each of them by the hand.

Rosie blew. The goldfish candle wobbled, went *bloop*, and relit. Elias blew. The candle wobbled harder, went *bloop bloop*, and relit again, this time spitting out a shower of bubbles.

"Miss Lena," Rosie urged.

Lena leaned down. Over Rosie's head she saw Elias looking at her, very close. The candlelight was on his face, and he was smiling, nothing like that clumsy laugh in the taco truck. This was a small, real smile, more in the corners of his eyes than his mouth.

She blew. The candle went out, every dinosaur balloon popped into glitter at once, Candy sneezed, Brisket barked, Linh screamed, and Viper hit play on a country-music version of "Happy Birthday" he had recorded himself, off-key from start to finish.

Rosie jumped up and down. "Make a wish! Everybody make a wish!"

Lena closed her eyes. She didn't know what to wish for. She hadn't wished for anything since February. She opened her eyes without having wished, and saw that Elias's eyes were still closed.

---

The real dinner, out in the real world, was cooked by Elias and Lena.

That was Viper's idea. He announced that he had been cooking for a whole week and it was time the two freeloaders did something useful. Then he disappeared into the yard with Rosie and a kite, leaving them a refrigerator, a greasy plastic-covered cookbook, and a note: *Spaghetti with red sauce. Rosie doesn't eat spicy. Kev eats anything. Lin's allergic to mushrooms.*

"Do you know how to cook?" Lena asked.

"Don't remember," Elias said. "There was nothing in my apartment but instant noodles, so probably not."

"Great."

She read the recipe. He chopped the onions. He chopped them very evenly, every piece a perfect square as if measured with a ruler, and also incredibly slowly.

"You don't have to make them pretty," she said. "They'll all cook down anyway."

"I'm not trying to make them pretty. My hands just do it."

She looked at the neat little squares of onion. Maybe that was something else they'd taught him as a child: precise, even, no deviations. She didn't say anything. She just pushed the bowl toward him.

They worked side by side. The water boiled, the pasta cooked, the sauce bubbled in the pan, the kitchen got warmer and the windows fogged up.

"Hand me the salt," Lena said, holding out her hand. He handed her the salt, and she sprinkled a pinch into the sauce.

"A little more," he said.

"That's enough."

"It's not. Taste it."

"The recipe says two teaspoons. I put in exactly two teaspoons."

"The recipe's written for a different brand of canned tomatoes. This brand's sweeter. You can tell by the color."

She turned to look at him. "You just told me you can't cook."

"I can't cook. I can read a label." He held up the empty tomato can and pointed at the nutrition panel. "Eight grams of sugar a serving. The brand in the book has three."

Lena snatched the can out of his hand, read the label, tasted the sauce, and added more salt without looking at him.

"Don't say anything," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're smiling."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're smiling with your eyes. I'm an examiner, Elias. I read faces for a living."

He didn't answer, but he couldn't keep the corners of his mouth down anymore.

In the kitchen doorway, Linh was leaning against the frame with her arms crossed and Bubbles draped over her shoulder, and girl and cat were both watching them with expressions of enormous satisfaction.

"You two bicker like an old married couple," Linh announced.

Lena dropped the ladle. Elias spun around and went back to chopping onions he had finished chopping ten minutes ago.

"Your sauce is about to burn," Linh said, and fled before anyone could throw something at her.

---

After dinner, while Elias washed the dishes and Rosie was in bed, Viper came out and sat next to Lena on the porch with two beers. They sat quietly for a while, looking out at the dark yard, where Rosie's kite was snagged in the oak, its paper tail fluttering in the wind.

"That guy," Viper said, jerking his chin toward the kitchen door, where light spilled out along with the sound of running water, "he looks at you exactly the way Brisket looks at a rack of ribs. Knows he can't have it, can't take his eyes off it anyway."

Lena nearly choked on her beer. "Marcus!"

"I'm just saying." Viper raised both hands. "I'm not telling you to do anything. I know you're still wearing the ring." He glanced at her collar, at the chain. "When my wife first passed, I wore her ring for two years. Right here." He touched his chest. "Some days I took it off, some days I put it back on. There's no right or wrong about it."

Lena didn't say anything. Her fingers had found the ring on their own.

"I just want you to know," Viper said as he got up and brushed off his pants, "in three years he's never looked at anybody like that. So whatever you end up doing with him, be gentle."

He went inside, and the screen door clacked shut behind him.

Lena sat alone on the porch holding a beer that had gone warm. The phone Linh had given her that morning, a cheap prepaid with nothing on it but the crow app, buzzed in her pocket. She took it out. Only Linh, Kev, Viper, and Elias knew this number, but the message wasn't from any of them. There was no sender name, only an icon: a black crescent moon on a purple background.

> **[N]:** I helped Thomas Daly for ten years. Then I helped his son. They both trusted me. You should too.
> **[N]:** They know who you are now, Maren.

Lena stared at the screen. The wind moved across the yard, and the kite's paper tail snapped against the oak branch.

In the kitchen, the water had stopped running at some point. Elias was standing at the window looking out at the porch, looking at her, and she didn't know how long he had been there.
