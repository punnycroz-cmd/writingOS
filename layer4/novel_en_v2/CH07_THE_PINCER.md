# CHAPTER 7: THE PINCER

On Thursday morning Lena reached her office at 7:15 and found someone already waiting for her.

She saw them the moment the elevator doors opened: two men in charcoal suits standing on either side of the door to 4B, hands folded in front of them, wireless earpieces with clear coiled cords running down behind their collars. Broad shoulders, relaxed stance, nowhere near careless. The kind of men who are paid never to be careless. And between them, with her back to the elevator, reading the brass plate on the door, stood a woman.

The woman turned. She was past fifty, but her skin had the smooth tightness that only money buys. Her silver hair was cut short and combed straight back, neat as a silver cap. A tailored navy wool coat over an ivory silk blouse, no jewelry except a platinum pin in the shape of a double helix. Pale blue eyes, clear as meltwater and just as cold.

Lena had never met her in person, but she had seen that face on hundreds of billboards, on magazine covers, on the giant screens in the lobby of every mall in the city.

"Dr. Hale," Iris Halloway said with a smile. "I apologize for showing up early without an appointment. I have a bad habit of assuming everyone gets up as early as I do."

"Dr. Halloway." Luckily, Lena's voice stayed level. Her hand tightened on the strap of her bag. "My office hours start at nine."

"Of course. I won't take much of your time." Halloway nodded toward the brass plate. "*Experience is evidence. Memory is testimony.* I love that. It's very precise. Not many people in our field understand the difference."

"Our field?"

"I started out as a memory researcher, just like you," Halloway said, her smile widening a little. "Before I realized that building dreams is a lot more interesting than taking them apart."

Lena unlocked the door. She didn't invite her in, but Halloway walked in as if she had been invited, leaving the two bodyguards in the hall. She made a slow circuit of the waiting room, taking in the acoustic panels, the wool carpet, the armchair where Elias had slept the night before last. The blanket was folded neatly on one corner of the chair, and Lena saw Halloway's eyes rest on it for half a second. Only half a second.

"I've read your work," Halloway said. "That paper on auditory latency in implanted memories was brilliant. Halloway Neuro-Systems is building an independent verification division for Somnia. We need people who can tell real from fake." She turned and looked straight at Lena. "I'd like you to lead it."

Lena waited. Nobody showed up at seven in the morning with two bodyguards just to offer someone a job.

"The salary will surprise you," Halloway added. "And you'd have access to technologies most people don't know exist."

"Thank you. I'll think about it."

"Please do." Halloway nodded as if she had expected exactly that answer. Then she went on in the same light voice, not a shade of it changed: "By the way, I understand that on Tuesday night you performed an after-hours scan on a man named Elias Vane."

Lena stood very still.

"Mr. Vane is our patient," Halloway said. "A very special patient, and a very sick one. He suffered a serious neurological event during a controlled trial and then left the hospital against medical advice. He may be delusional, disoriented, possibly agitated. We're very worried about him."

"He came to me as an independent client," Lena said. "All data from that session is confidential under state health law."

"I understand, and I respect that." Halloway opened a small leather clutch, took out a cream-colored envelope, and laid it on the reception desk. It bore the red wax seal of a federal court. "This is a subpoena for all raw data relating to Mr. Vane, under Section 14 of the Critical Technologies Protection Act. The deadline is noon tomorrow. Honestly, I hope we won't need it."

Lena looked at the envelope but didn't touch it.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the Medical Board suspends your license pending review, this office is sealed, and you get to explain to a federal judge why you were concealing data that belongs to a defense program." Halloway said all of this wearing the same smile. "But I don't think it will come to that. You're a smart woman."

Lena's right hand hung at her side. She didn't notice that it had started to move. Three quick, two slow.

Halloway had turned to go, but she stopped. Her ice-colored eyes dropped to Lena's hand, to the fingers tapping lightly against the fabric of her pants, and stayed there a little too long. Then she looked up into Lena's face, and for the first time since she'd stepped off the elevator, the smile was gone. It simply wasn't there anymore, as if Halloway had forgotten to hold it in place. What replaced it was a naked, intent look, almost hungry, the look of a collector who has just spotted the piece she's hunted her whole life sitting in a bin of junk at a flea market.

"How old are you, Dr. Hale?" Halloway asked.

"I don't see how that's relevant."

"Of course not." The smile came back, quick as a light switch. "Occupational habit. I ask people strange questions. Forgive me." She nodded goodbye. "Noon tomorrow. I'm looking forward to working with you."

The door closed. Her heels faded down the hallway, as even as a second hand.

Lena stood alone in the middle of the waiting room. She looked down at her hand, and for the first time in her life, she was afraid of her own fingers.

---

Elias arrived at eleven by the back fire stairs. The afternoon before, he had gone to his apartment to get his things and come back by a winding route through three subway stations, changing trains twice, cutting through a mall with four exits. The black sedan stayed with him to the second station and then lost him. Or at least it wanted him to think it had.

He brought a backpack. In it was the hard drive from the rig at home.

"Before the Layer 4 trial, they had me run five prep sessions on my home machine. It's in the contract," he said when Lena had told him about her morning. "My machine kept a copy of the cache, but it's encrypted. I can't open it."

"Corwin has something that opens it," Lena said.

She took out the notebook and turned to a page near the end. A long string of handwritten characters split into four blocks, with a note in the margin: *Key from N. Only opens the original Prometheus archive. Not enough to get into the current system.*

"N," Elias said. "Nyx."

Lena looked up. "You know that name?"

"While I was in the coma, someone posted a bounty online to find me. They signed it Nyx."

They looked at each other. One more thread, connecting Corwin to Elias through someone nobody had ever seen.

"Tonight," Lena said. "After the building closes, we open it."

---

Three blocks away, on the lowest level of a parking garage, a long black car sat in the darkest corner with the engine running to keep the air conditioning on.

Iris Halloway sat in the back seat with a tablet on her lap. On the screen was an old photograph taken in a white room, its corners faded: a nine-year-old girl sitting on the edge of an iron bed, her black hair cut short, her dark eyes staring straight into the lens. Below it, a line of print:

*Subject One — Solis, M. — Status: Missing, presumed dead (10/14/2004).*

Halloway put her finger on the photo and enlarged the girl's face, then opened a second image beside it: Lena Hale's headshot from the State Forensic Board's website. She looked at the two pictures for a long time.

"Back to headquarters, ma'am?" the driver asked through the rearview mirror.

"Not yet." She didn't look up. "Call Bradley. Tell him to reopen the paired-subject file. All of it, including the frozen sections."

"Yes, ma'am."

Halloway looked at the two pictures a little longer, then turned off the screen, leaned her head back against the seat, and closed her eyes. She smiled, a real smile this time, small and almost tender.

"Twenty years," she said softly to herself. "There you are. Subject One."
