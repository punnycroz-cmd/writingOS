# CHAPTER 18: THIRTY YEARS IN ELEVEN DAYS

"You said a few days," Kev said. "It's been ten."

The two of them were standing behind Viper's shed, where the taco truck was parked under a tin awning. Kev was changing the truck's oil, because Viper had asked him to, or because he needed something to keep his hands busy. Elias stood beside him, handing over whatever he asked for.

"Have you masked the signal?" Elias asked.

"No. But I'm close." Kev wiped his hands on a rag. "I know where it's coming from now. A small area in your left temporal lobe, right near your scar. They didn't implant anything. They trained a cluster of neurons to fire a rhythm on their own, like training a dog to bark on command. If you want it to shut up, you have to retrain it."

"How?"

"A dive. Deep. Right into that spot. With someone outside steering." Kev looked at him. "Someone who understands your brain."

Elias knew who he meant.

"After this afternoon," Elias said. "I'll tell her after this afternoon."

Kev didn't answer. He screwed the oil cap back on, slowly, until it clicked.

"Last time you said *later* too," he said.

---

The plan for that afternoon was Elias's, and Lena had argued against it for two days straight.

"You want to dive right into the damaged part," she said for the third time. "That's the place any decent doctor would keep everyone away from."

"That's where the thing we need is," Elias said.

Over the last ten days they had dived seven times, four times into his memories and three into hers, and every time they came back with one piece. Room Zero, and the long knock sequences they still hadn't decoded. Elena teaching Maren to count in Spanish. A technician named Bradley, twenty years younger than he was now, sneaking Elias a mint when no one was looking. They had learned a lot about their childhoods, and nothing at all about Halloway's servers, or the Island Station, or what Corwin had seen before he vanished.

If any of that was anywhere, it could only be in those thirty years, the thirty shattered years he had lived in an eleven-day coma, where his memories and strangers' memories had run together like paint poured into one bucket. Lena called it "the noise zone." Kev called it "the landfill." Linh called it "the blender," and Viper had banned her from calling it that in front of Rosie.

"Ten minutes," Lena said at last. "One second past ten minutes and I pull you, I don't care what you're in the middle of."

"Agreed."

"And if your nose bleeds, I pull you."

"My nose always bleeds."

"Then dive faster."

---

The noise zone had no bottom.

Elias had been through Layer 2 seven times, so he knew the feeling of sinking into warm water, of seeing the glowing fish of recent memories. This time he let himself sink past them, past the empty dark, past even the white hallway with its two doors, and kept on going down.

Down there the water had gone thick and gluey, crowded with things drifting in it: a red bicycle with a basket on the front; a wedding in a church he didn't know, where the bride was crying because her shoes were too tight; an old dog lying dead under a kitchen table; a man in an accounting office at ten at night staring out the window and thinking about running away; a little girl on a school stage who had forgotten the words to her song; a pool table; a hospital; a beach somewhere so hot it burned.

Every memory that slid past left a taste on his tongue, salty, sweet, bitter, metallic. He didn't hold on to any of them, because none of them were his. They belonged to people who had been logged into Halloway's network on August twenty-first at two in the afternoon, when his brain blew apart in Layer 4 and sucked in everything around it like a black hole.

This time he noticed something he'd missed in all seven dives before. In every single memory, somewhere in a corner, there was a small glowing animal. A fox sitting under the accountant's desk. A sparrow perched on the church window frame. A cat lying on the edge of the stage. They weren't doing anything. They were just sitting there, watching.

Echoes.

He marked it, like pinning a coordinate on a map, and moved on. He didn't have time to work out what it meant.

"Six minutes," Lena's voice called down, very far away. "Where are you?"

"In the paint bucket," he said, and somewhere up there he heard her laugh softly.

He was looking for salt. The first time, in the clinic, when Corwin's kitchen drifted past, he had tasted salt, though there was no reason a kitchen should taste salty. He wasn't looking for an image. He was looking for a taste.

There. Off to the left, drifting slowly, something dark and bitterly salty. He swam toward it.

---

The deck of a small boat, at night, rain driving sideways into his face.

He was looking out through the eyes of a tall, broad-shouldered man holding a satellite phone sealed in a plastic bag. There was a line of text on the screen: *[N]: Do not enter Zone 9. Repeat. Turn back.*

The man typed his reply with a frozen thumb: *I'm here. Lights to the north. They've got people out here.*

Ahead, through the rain, there was a faint light. No lighthouse had a light that color. It was a cold blue-green glow, as if shining up from under the water.

Then an engine. Something bigger, faster, bearing down from the right.

Elias felt the man thinking about a woman, in pictures rather than words: a kitchen, a half-dead pot of basil, an oversized gray sweater. And a feeling close to regret, but hotter, closer to anger. *I should have told her. I should have told her everything.*

Impact. Water.

The memory shattered, and Elias let it drift away. Somewhere far above, his lungs clenched as if he had really inhaled water. He started to swim up.

Then he saw it. Another piece, smaller, lying right behind the one that had just broken. Just as salty, but colder, like seawater kept in a refrigerator. He reached out and grabbed it.

---

White. A curved white ceiling, very close, so close it was only a hand's width from his face. The inside of a frosted glass lid, beaded with tiny drops of condensation.

He couldn't move. The man whose head he was in couldn't move. There was a tube in his throat, a gentle, steady pressure pushing air into his lungs and drawing it out. In, out, in, out.

Outside the frosted glass, shadows passed. A woman's voice, flat and bored, like someone reading off a form:

"...Daly. Day one hundred ninety-two. Theta rhythm stable. No response to Layer 1 stimuli. Note: subject shows a recurring 'anchor' phenomenon with the same keyword..."

A beep.

"...keyword is 'Lena.' Send it up. She wants to know every time it shows up."

And then the man in the white pod thought, very slowly, like a fish under the ice thinking about the sun: *Lena.*

---

"Up. Elias. Up, now."

He opened his eyes. Blood had run all the way down to his chin, and Lena was pressing gauze to his nose so hard it hurt.

"Ten minutes and four seconds," she said. "I told you one second past and—"

"He's alive."

Her hands stopped.

He hadn't meant to say it like that. He'd meant to say it slowly, carefully, choosing every word. He had practiced it in his head behind the shed with Kev. But it just came out, the way the blood came out of his nose.

"Corwin," he said through the gauze. "Day one hundred ninety-two. In a pod, with a breathing tube, with someone taking notes. He was thinking about you." He coughed and swallowed down the taste of iron. "On August twenty-first, he was still alive."

Lena didn't move. Behind her, Viper set the ladle in his hand down on the table, very gently, as if afraid of breaking it. Linh shot up off the stairs. Kev stayed where he was in the doorway, but the phone in his hand had fallen onto the carpet.

"One hundred ninety-two days," Lena said, her voice strangely level, an examiner's voice. "Counting from February ninth, that's... August nineteenth. Two days before you went into Layer 4."

She did the math in her head faster than he could.

"So maybe he's still..." Linh started, then stopped.

Lena stood up holding the bloody gauze, looked at it like she didn't know what it was, and set it down on the coffee table. She went to the window, pulled the curtain aside, and stood looking out at the yard, where Rosie's kite was still stuck in the oak from the birthday.

Elias watched her back. Her shoulders were perfectly straight. One hand hung at her side. The other gripped the chain under her collar so hard the chain left a red line on the back of her neck.

He didn't get up. He didn't know whether, if he stood, he would walk toward her or out of the room.

Viper broke the silence by dropping onto the arm of the chair right next to Elias, which made the chair crack.

"All right," Viper said. "Then we go get the guy."

Lena turned around.

"What?" Linh said.

"I said we go get him." Viper folded his arms. "We've got the best hacker on the East Coast, a sniper who never sleeps, a doctor who takes brains apart for a living, a lunatic who went into Layer 4 and crawled back out, and a taco truck. They've got a pod. I like our odds."

"Marcus," Lena said. "You have a daughter."

"Yeah. And last week my daughter asked me why the fish doctor always stares at the ocean when it's on TV. I didn't know what to tell her." Viper shrugged. "Now I do."

Linh came down the last two stairs. "I'm in."

"Linh—"

"I dropped out junior year to make a game about a cat that blows bubbles. My mom in Saigon still thinks I'm writing my master's thesis. I've got nothing more important to do." She planted herself next to Viper with her hands in her pockets. "Besides, I want to see that Halloway woman's face when I get into her server."

Kev bent down, picked up his phone, and looked at the screen for a moment.

"I need to know where the Island Station is," he said. "Real coordinates. Not guesses."

"Nyx knows," Lena said, looking at Kev. "Corwin asked her. In that memory, she was telling him not to go into Zone 9."

Kev was quiet for a moment. "So we have to trust somebody who sells information."

"Or trust nobody."

Kev put the phone in his pocket. He didn't say yes, and he didn't say no.

Lena looked at Elias. He was still sitting there with a red wad of gauze at his nose, looking up at her. He knew he ought to say something, something proper, like *I'm happy for you* or *we'll find him*. Those were the right things to say. The things a friend would say.

What he said was, "My nose is still bleeding."

Lena looked at him, and then did something he hadn't expected. She laughed, one short, wet sound, almost a sob. She knelt in front of the chair, pulled the old gauze away, and pressed a fresh piece on.

"Head down," she said, her hands shaking. "Don't tip it back."

He bowed his head, his forehead nearly touching her shoulder. She smelled of the cheap lavender soap Viper bought at the supermarket, and of apples. Every night for five nights she had peeled an apple. He hadn't asked why.

He thought about Kev, about the shed, about *last time you said later too*, and he told himself: not now. Not when she's just heard he's alive. Tonight. I'll tell her tonight.

---

At four o'clock Rosie came home, running in from the backyard, her yellow boots caked with mud, holding a glass jar with a beetle in it.

"Dad! Dad, there are toy planes outside!"

Viper was wiping down the table. "What kind of planes, sweetheart?"

"Toy planes! Three of them! Black! Flying over Mrs. Henderson's roof, going around and around and they won't leave!" Rosie held up the jar. "I showed Gus, but Gus isn't scared. Gus is a dung beetle, so he's not scared of anything."

The rag in Viper's hand stopped. Kev was at the front window before Rosie finished the sentence, parting the curtain just a crack, looking up at the sky.

Elias stood up. The bleeding had stopped. He went to stand beside Kev. Three black dots against the gray sky, flying slowly in a triangle right over the roof of the third house from the end of the street, without a sound, too high to hear.

Elias looked at the clock on the wall. 4:02. His brain's last transmission, according to the countdown Kev had kept stuck in the corner of his screen for ten days, had been at 3:51.

Kev didn't look at him. He didn't need to.
