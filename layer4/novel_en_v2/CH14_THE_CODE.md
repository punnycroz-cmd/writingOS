# CHAPTER 14: THE CODE

Lena couldn't sleep.

She lay on the single bed in the spare room, looking up at the ceiling where Rosie's moon lamp cast a round, cream-colored glow. The clock on the nightstand said 12:40. She had been lying like that for two hours.

Her fingers tapped on the edge of the blanket. Three quick, two slow. She made them stop. Five seconds later they started again.

She had been doing it for twenty years, in tense meetings, in courtrooms while she waited for the lawyers' questions, on nights when Corwin was away on assignment and the apartment got too quiet. She had never once wondered why. A habit doesn't need a reason.

Now she knew the reason, and she didn't know what to do with it. A piece of memory handed back to her with no instructions, no hint where in her life it was supposed to go.

She closed her eyes. And this time, as the dark behind her eyelids thickened, something surfaced.

---

A white room. An iron bed. A desk bolted to the wall. A box of crayons.

The girl sat cross-legged on the bed with a sheet of paper on her lap, drawing a blue whale flying through the clouds, because there was no sea here.

On the other side of the wall there were sounds. A door opening, footsteps, a door closing, a lock turning. He was back.

The girl put down her crayon and listened. Nothing. He was always quiet when he first came back. She knew why. After the "tests" downstairs, your head was empty, like a house that had just had all the furniture hauled out, and it took a while to figure out where you were.

She crawled over to the wall, pressed her palm flat against it, and knocked. Three quick, two slow.

She was the one who had made up the knock. She remembered the day clearly. It was a long time ago, maybe a whole year, in this world without calendars. He had just been moved into the room next door, and the first night he cried, very softly, trying to hold it in so nobody would hear. But the wall was thin. She heard all of it.

She had knocked on the wall. It didn't mean anything. It was just so he'd know someone was on the other side. He stopped crying. The next night she knocked again, and the night after that he knocked back. And little by little, the knock turned into a sentence. *I'm here.*

From the other side of the wall, after a long silence, came an answer. Weak, slower than usual, but the right rhythm. The girl let out her breath. Every time, she held it until she heard that knock.

Then her own door opened, and a woman came in: white lab coat, dark hair in a neat bun, eyes that were tired but kind. She knelt by the bed, and the girl threw herself into her arms.

*Mamá.*

Here she wasn't allowed to say Mamá. Here it had to be *Dr. Solis*, because there were cameras and people listening. But when it was just the two of them, after the lights went out, her mother always leaned close to her ear and whispered, *Mi niña.*

"How is he today?" the girl asked, her face buried in her mother's lab coat. "Number Zero?"

The woman was quiet for a moment. "He's tired. But he's okay."

"Did they hurt him again?"

"Not hurt. Just tired." She stroked her daughter's hair. "Why do you keep calling him Number Zero? He has a name."

"I know. I saw it on Mr. Bradley's clipboard. *Vane, Elias.* But it's too long. And nobody calls him by his name anyway. Everybody says Zero."

"So what do you want to call him?"

The girl thought for a moment. Then she turned, put her mouth right up against the wall, and said loudly, so the other side could hear:

"Eli! From now on your name is Eli! Got it?"

A silence. Then from the other side, very softly, through the bricks:

"...Eli."

As if he were tasting the name. As if no one had ever given him a name before.

The woman laughed. It was the first time she had laughed in this memory, and the laugh lit up her whole tired face.

---

Lena opened her eyes in the dark with her face soaked. She didn't know when she had started crying. Her chest hurt, a dull, deep ache, like a bruise under the ribs.

She had a mother. That mother had a face, a voice, a laugh. That mother called her *mi niña* in the dark when no one was listening. And for twenty years she hadn't known.

She sat up. She couldn't lie there any longer.

---

The back of Viper's roof was flat, where the kitchen had been built out as an addition, and an iron ladder stood against the wall. Lena climbed up, wearing a sweater Rosa had lent her.

Elias was already there. He sat at the edge of the roof with his legs hanging over, his back a little hunched, looking up at the sky. Ashgrove didn't have many lights, so there were more stars than you could count.

He turned when he heard her on the ladder. He didn't look surprised, as if he'd been waiting for her.

"Can't sleep?" he asked.

"Neither can you."

She sat down beside him with some space between them. Not as far as the coffee table in the waiting room, not as close as under the overpass when he put his coat around her. Just enough.

They sat in silence for a while. The night wind rustled the oak leaves, and somewhere in the woods an owl called.

"I remembered something," Lena said.

He turned to look at her, and she told him all of it: the white room, the flying whale, the knocking, the woman in the lab coat, *mi niña*, the name that was too long. She told it fast, as if afraid it would dissolve if she stopped.

When she got to *Eli*, he closed his eyes.

"I've heard that name," he said. "At the hospital, when I first woke up. Somewhere in those thirty shattered years, someone called me by a name that wasn't mine. I thought it was one of the strange memories." He opened his eyes. "It was you."

Lena nodded. She didn't trust her voice right then.

"My turn," he said.

He told her what he'd seen in the dive that afternoon: the hallway, the child's low eye level, the swinging ID badge, the room with the iron bed, the child sitting at the foot of the wall, the lullaby coming through.

"He wasn't scared," he said. "That's the strangest part. I thought I'd feel scared, but I didn't. The kid was just tired, and waiting. Waiting to hear the knock from the other side." He paused. "It was the only thing he looked forward to all day."

"Me too," Lena said. "In my memory I hold my breath until I hear the knock come back."

They looked at each other, and then she understood. Each of them had kept only half. He remembered the hallway, room zero, the feeling of waiting. She remembered room one, her mother's face, the name being given. Each half on its own meant nothing. Put together, they made a story.

"Like two halves of a torn photograph," she said.

"Or two sides of a wall."

Lena laughed softly, then went quiet. Her hand found the chain under her collar on its own and touched the silver ring. Elias saw it, but he didn't say anything.

"Corwin used to ask me about the knock," she said after a while. "He said it sounded like Morse code. He even looked it up. Three dots and two dashes is the number three. He said I must have liked the number three when I was little." She smiled, small and sad. "He had to explain everything. Reporter to the bone. He couldn't stand it when something didn't have an answer."

"What was he like?"

She thought about it. "Loud. He sang when he cooked, badly. Every morning he read the paper and argued with it like it could hear him. Never remembered where he left his keys." She stopped. "We knew each other since we were kids. Margaret was close friends with the Dalys, so I grew up in both houses. He was the neighbor boy who teased me because I wouldn't talk to anybody. Then one day, when I was twenty-five, he asked me to dinner, and I realized he hadn't teased me in a very long time."

"Did he know about this? About Prometheus, about her?"

"No." Lena shook her head. "I'm sure he didn't. He was investigating Halloway because of his father's death, not because of me. If he'd known..." She trailed off. She didn't know what would have happened if he'd known. "Thomas kept the secret even from his own son."

Elias was quiet for a moment. "And he still found me," he said. "In the notebook. Subject Zero. He didn't know what I was to you, but he found me first."

"And you found me through his memory," Lena said. "My face in that kitchen. That's what led you to my office."

They both went quiet, thinking about it. A strange circle: three people tied together by threads that no one had been able to see until now.

"I'm sorry," Elias said.

"For what?"

"For dragging you into this. If I hadn't come, you'd still have your office. Your life."

"The life of somebody who didn't know who she was, you mean," Lena said, looking up at the sky. "For five years I've been telling people that memory is testimony, not truth. That you can sincerely believe something completely false. I never thought the person would be me."

The wind blew, and the tire swing in the yard creaked as it rocked.

"I'm so angry," she said. "Not at you. At Thomas. At Margaret. Twenty years they let me live inside a made-up story. And I know they did it to protect me. I know if they'd told me the truth, I might not have lived this long. I know all that, and I'm still angry. Isn't that ridiculous?"

"It's not ridiculous," Elias said. "I'm angry too. That notebook. Twenty years I blamed myself for a candle that never existed. Now I know it was fake, and I don't feel as relieved as I thought I would. I just feel robbed. Like somebody took twenty years of my grief away, and it was the only thing I had."

Lena turned to look at him. The starlight was faint, just enough to show the outline of his face, the thin cheekbones, the eyes turned up to the sky. He had described exactly what she was feeling, and it was the first time in her life she had met someone who understood precisely what she felt without her having to explain.

Her fingers tapped on the roof. Three quick, two slow. This time she knew she was doing it. A second later, his fingers tapped back beside her hand.

They didn't look at each other. They just sat there and watched the stars.

"It's getting cold," Lena said after a long while. "We've got another dive tomorrow. Go to bed."

"You too."

She stood up, brushed off her sweater, and walked to the ladder. At the edge of the roof she stopped and turned her head.

"Good night, Eli."

The name was out of her mouth before she could think. She froze. So did he, turning to look at her, his eyes wide in the dark. Nobody said anything. In the woods, the owl called twice.

Lena turned and went down the ladder fast, without looking back.

On the roof, Elias sat alone for a long time, looking down at his hand, the fingers still curled the way they'd been when he knocked.
