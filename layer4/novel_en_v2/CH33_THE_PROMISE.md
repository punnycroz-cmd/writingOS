# CHAPTER 33: THE PROMISE

Lena was being pulled under.

She was still standing behind the console, still seeing the room: the concrete walls, the cut steel door, Dr. Bradley slumped on the threshold, Viper lying on his side on the floor, three folding chairs with three sleeping bodies. She could still hear the waves out there, a truck still crackling as it burned in the lot, Nyx's voice on the speaker counting down the minutes until the helicopter. But all of it was thinning out, like a photograph left too long in the sun.

And under it, in the corner of her head where the line connected her to him, another hand was pulling. Cold, dry, patient. It didn't yank. It just pulled, steady and even, the way you haul a net up from the bottom of the sea.

She held on to the edge of the console so hard her knuckles went white. She knew it was pointless. The thing pulling her wasn't touching her body. But she didn't know what else to do.

"Doc." Nyx, urgent. "Your waveform. It's merging into Vane's."

Lena looked at the screen. The two waveforms weren't running side by side anymore. They were twisting around each other like two threads being wound into one.

"I know," she said, and her voice sounded strange. "She's pulling."

"Pull the cable."

"If I pull the cable, he loses his anchor. He'll bleed out in Layer 3 before SWAT gets here."

"If you don't, she gets both of you."

Lena didn't answer.

The cold hand in her head pulled a little more, and the control room blurred a little more. At the edges of her vision she began to see things that weren't here: a hexagonal room, silver rivers under a glass floor, a glass sphere, a woman in a navy wool coat with one hand raised like a conductor.

And him, standing with his back to her, both blades lowered.

---

Then she felt the knock, at the other end of the line. Very light. There were no hands in here. It was just a rhythm traveling down the line like a vibration.

*Three quick. Two slow. I'm here.*

Lena went still.

She knew that rhythm. She had tapped it her whole life without knowing why, on walls, on tables, on her knees, on the arms of chairs. She knew what it meant. She also knew when a child knocked it: every night, through a wall, before sleep. And one other time, only once, in the memory she had seen sixteen days ago on the floor of an abandoned warehouse: a seven-year-old boy sitting in front of a ventilation shaft, at the other end of which a little girl was crawling away, and the boy knocking one last time on the metal of the shaft, very softly, before the men in black came.

*I'm here.* A greeting and a goodbye at the same time.

"No," Lena said.

The screen in front of her jumped.

> `[WARNING: SUBJECT ZERO — LAYER 4 SEQUENCE INITIATED FROM SUBJECT SIDE]`
> `[INTRACRANIAL PRESSURE: 52 mmHg]`
> `[ANCHOR SYNC: SEPARATING]`

The line in her head jerked hard, and this time the jerk came from his side. A push, like someone shoving hard against her heels, pushing her forward, toward the far end of a pitch-dark tunnel.

The control room rushed back, sharp and bright and loud: the waves, the fire, Nyx's voice. She was being pushed out, back into her body, back to safety. And he was staying behind.

In the last folding chair, Elias's body bowed up off the back of the chair. Blood began to run from both nostrils, from the corner of his left eye, from his ear. His fingers curled and clamped onto the frame of the chair.

He was doing it again, the same exact thing. He had done the math, the way he always did the math. One line, two ends, one hand pulling. If he opened Layer 4 from his own side, the force would cut the line and throw her out of Halloway's reach, and he would stay, alone, at the mouth of the tunnel.

*Refused to leave position until restrained.*

"No," Lena said again, and her voice broke. "No. No, no, no."

"Doc, your sync is separating," Nyx said. "Thirty percent. You're safe. Let it go."

Lena looked at the screen. The two twisted threads were unwinding. Her line was turning green again. His was going red, redder and redder, climbing right out of the frame.

Twenty years not knowing who she was. Twenty years tapping a rhythm without understanding why. Twenty years believing the first memory of her life was a man peeling an apple in a hospital room, when the real first memory, the one that mattered most, was a little boy sitting in front of a ventilation shaft so she could live.

He had stayed behind once already, twenty years ago. He stayed behind, and they took everything from him: his name, his memories, his whole life. They left him only one name. Hers.

She was not going to let him stay behind again.

---

Lena let go of the edge of the console.

She didn't pull the cable. She did the opposite. She reached up to the sensor ring on her head, the shallow one, sync only, the one keeping her standing here safe in the real world, and turned the depth dial all the way up.

"Doc, what are you doing?" Nyx yelled. "Lena. LENA—"

The control room disappeared.

---

She didn't fall. She shot forward, along the snapping line, like a swimmer going up a waterfall. Through the burning asphalt, through the silver rivers, through the black animals sitting in their silent circle, past the woman in the navy coat, who turned her head with an expression Lena would remember forever: for the first time in twenty years, Iris Halloway had not seen something coming.

She saw him.

He was standing in front of a door, or rather a crack in the air, running from the floor up to where she couldn't see the top anymore, getting wider. Through the crack was the nameless light, the feeling of too much, every voice of every person who had ever lived.

He was stepping into it. One foot was already across the edge, and half his body had blurred, dissolving into that light like a watercolor dipped in water.

Lena didn't call his name. Not *Elias*. *Elias* was the name on a medical chart, in the newspapers, the name of the twenty-seven-year-old man who had walked into her office one rainy night, and it couldn't reach where he was standing. She called the name a nine-year-old girl had given a little boy who had no one in the world.

"ELI!"

He stopped. He didn't turn his head, but he stopped. The foot still on this side froze in mid-step.

"You don't get to stay behind alone this time!" she shouted. Her voice rang through the hexagonal room, off the black walls, echoing back the way it would inside a metal shaft. "Do you hear me? You don't get to! Last time you stayed, I lost you for twenty years! I lost my mom, I lost my name, I lost you too! I'm not doing it again!"

He turned his head. Half his face had dissolved into the light. The other half was looking at her, gray-eyed, soaking wet.

"You shouldn't be here," he said, his voice coming from very far away. "She wants you here. I pushed you out."

"I know. That's why I came back."

"Why?"

She walked toward him, and with every step the pull from the crack got stronger. She could feel her hair start to lift as if she were underwater, could feel the edges of her body start to blur.

"Because you promised," she said.

She stopped right in front of him, right at the edge of the crack, and looked straight into what was left of his face.

"That night, at the mouth of the vent. I made you promise. Remember? I made you promise you'd come find me." Her voice broke and put itself back together. "You kept it. Twenty years, not remembering anything, and you still kept it. You found me. So now you don't get to go anywhere. Once you've found someone, you stay. That's the other half of the promise. You're not done yet."

He looked at her.

"You promised, Eli," she said.

And then she said the thing she hadn't been able to say for twenty years, the thing she hadn't known was there, the thing a nine-year-old girl had screamed down a ventilation shaft and that had been buried under a layer of ice so thick and so cold, until now.

"My name is Maren."

---

Something broke open inside her. It didn't hurt. It was like ice breaking on a lake in early spring, like a door locked for twenty years suddenly flying open. And everything rushed in.

Room One. The whale flying on the wall. The water stain shaped like Italy on the ceiling. The smell of crayons. Mamá sitting on the edge of the bed, her nails bitten short, singing about snow. The knocking through the wall every night, three quick and two slow, and the longer sequences too, the ones she had forgotten. *Two quick, one slow, four quick: did it hurt today? One slow, two quick: a little. Three slow: go to sleep.* A skinny boy with hair sticking straight up and gray eyes, standing in the white corridor and looking at her for the first time, and her thinking, *he looks like a cat that got caught in the rain.* The tests on the lower floor, the needles, the cold metal rings on her head, and his hand, always finding hers through the gap in the door whenever the grown-ups weren't looking.

The last night. Smoke. The vent. His skinny wrist in her hand. Her fingers pried loose one by one.

*I promise. I'll come find you.*

Maren Solis opened her eyes in the middle of the hexagonal room, and she knew who she was.

---

She reached out. Not to pull him out of the crack. She knew she couldn't. The pull of Layer 4 was stronger than anything she had. She reached out and took his hand, the half of his hand that hadn't dissolved into the light yet.

And she held on to the line. She didn't let it snap. She didn't let Halloway pull it away. She took hold of it, at her end, with every bit of will she had, and held on tight. Willingly. Not because she'd been dragged in, but because she was choosing to stay.

Somewhere behind her she heard Halloway, one short breath out, almost a laugh.

"Thank you," Halloway said. "That's exactly what I needed."

Maren didn't turn around. She looked at Eli.

"She thinks she's won," she said.

"She's got both hinges now," he said. "That's what she wanted."

"Yeah." She squeezed his hand tighter. "But she forgot something."

"What?"

"A door has two hinges, sure," she said, looking straight into his eyes. "But who said she gets to stand in the middle?"

He looked at her, and then, very slowly, the corner of what was left of his mouth turned up.

The light in the crack changed. It stopped pulling and went still, like water when the wind dies, like a heavy door finally standing firm on both its hinges.

The dissolved half of his face began to come back, little by little, like a photograph coming up in a developing tray. And he wasn't standing at the edge of the crack alone anymore. They stood there together, the two of them, hand in hand, their backs to the nameless light, facing Iris Halloway.
